## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words describe the speaker and the speech. *Un orateur* or *une oratrice* is someone who speaks in public, and a good one knows how to *captiver* an audience. *Un plaidoyer* is a speech that defends a cause. *Un argument d'autorité* relies on an expert or an official source rather than on reasoning: it's useful, but a careful listener checks the source. ## Building a persuasive speech

A short persuasive speech combines the tools of the whole unit. As a rule of thumb, follow this plan:

1. **Address and hook**: *mesdames et messieurs*, then a rhetorical question that interpellates the audience.
2. **The plan**, in one sentence, with signposts: *je commencerai par…, puis j'en viendrai à…*.
3. **Your main argument**, supported by a fact or an argument from authority.
4. **The strategic concession**: grant the other side's best point, then turn it. A fronted *que* clause works well here: *que…, je l'admets. Il n'en demeure pas moins que…*.
5. **Your proposal**, made memorable with one device, such as an anaphora.
6. **The ending**: *permettez-moi de conclure*, a final question or contrast, and the thanks.

Choose three devices at most, and place them at the hook, the proposal and the end. Between them, keep the sentences plain and factual: the devices stand out only if the rest of the speech is sober, and plain facts make your claims easier to believe. A concession placed before your proposal makes the proposal easier to accept: the audience has seen that you listened.

## A model speech

This speech is an original text written for this lesson. The town, the market and the speaker are invented.

*Discours de Madame Leroy, au nom des commerçants du marché, lors d'une réunion publique*

*Mesdames et messieurs, chers amis,*

*Qui d'entre vous n'a jamais fait ses courses au marché du samedi ? Je vois que personne ne lève la main.*

*Je commencerai par ce que le marché nous apporte, puis j'en viendrai aux critiques que nous avons entendues, et enfin à nos solutions.*

*Ce marché existe depuis plus de cent ans, et il fait partie de l'histoire de notre ville. Le marché, c'est d'abord un lieu de rencontre. On y vient pour acheter, mais aussi pour parler et pour retrouver ses voisins. Une étude de la mairie elle-même montre que le jour du marché, le centre-ville accueille deux fois plus de visiteurs que les autres jours. La place est alors pleine de monde, et les commerçants du centre vendent plus.*

*Que le marché pose des problèmes de circulation, je l'admets. Que certains habitants soient gênés par le bruit, je le comprends. Nous avons entendu ces critiques, et elles sont légitimes. Il n'en demeure pas moins que déplacer le marché loin du centre serait une erreur. Ce serait la fin d'une tradition.*

*J'en viens maintenant à nos solutions. Nous proposons de commencer plus tard, à huit heures, et de libérer la place à treize heures. Nous proposons de nettoyer la place nous-mêmes. Nous proposons d'en parler tous ensemble, avant toute décision.*

*Alors, permettez-moi de conclure par une question simple : voulons-nous un centre-ville vivant, ou un centre-ville vide ?*

*Je vous remercie de votre attention.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Speech by Madame Leroy, on behalf of the market traders, at a public meeting. Ladies and gentlemen, dear friends, Which of you has never done their shopping at the Saturday market? I see that nobody raises a hand. I'll begin with what the market brings us, then I'll come to the criticisms we have heard, and finally to our solutions. This market has existed for more than a hundred years, and it is part of our town's history. The market is first of all a meeting place. People come to buy, but also to talk and to see their neighbours. A study by the town hall itself shows that on market day the town centre receives twice as many visitors as on other days. The square is then full of people, and the shops in the centre sell more. That the market causes traffic problems, I admit. That some residents are bothered by the noise, I understand. We have heard these criticisms, and they are legitimate. The fact remains that moving the market away from the centre would be a mistake. It would be the end of a tradition. I now come to our solutions. We propose to start later, at eight, and to clear the square at one. We propose to clean the square ourselves. We propose to talk about it all together, before any decision. So allow me to conclude with a simple question: do we want a lively town centre, or an empty one? Thank you for your attention.)

## How the speech uses the unit

- **Speech structure**: address (*mesdames et messieurs, chers amis*), a hook, the plan announcing all three parts (*je commencerai par…, puis j'en viendrai à…, et enfin à…*), the signpost *j'en viens maintenant à*, *permettez-moi de conclure*, the thanks.
- **Three devices, one at each key moment**: a rhetorical question at the hook (*qui d'entre vous n'a jamais… ?*), an anaphora for the proposal (*nous proposons…*, three times), and a closing rhetorical question (*voulons-nous un centre-ville vivant, ou un centre-ville vide ?*). Everything between them is plain.
- **An argument from authority**: *une étude de la mairie elle-même montre que…*.
- **One strategic concession**, before the proposal: two fronted *que* clauses with the subjunctive (*que le marché pose…, je l'admets*; *que certains habitants soient gênés…, je le comprends*), then the turn with *il n'en demeure pas moins que*, stated plainly (*ce serait la fin d'une tradition*).

## Your turn

Write a short persuasive speech of eight to ten sentences for a cause you care about: a park, a school project, a local festival. Follow the six-step plan. Use exactly three devices, one strategic concession with a fronted *que* clause and *il n'en demeure pas moins que*, and one argument from authority. Then read it aloud, marking your pauses, and check that someone hearing it once could repeat your main proposal.

## Check your work

- Does the speech open with an address and a hook, and announce its plan?
- Is the verb in the fronted *que* clause in the subjunctive, with a form that differs from the indicative where possible (*soient*, *ait*, *fasse*)?
- Does the concession come before your proposal, and does it turn with *il n'en demeure pas moins que* or *mais*?
- Did you use exactly three devices, one at the hook, one at the proposal and one at the end, with plain sentences in between?
- Does the end come back to the hook, and thank the audience?

<Callout type="warning" title="Que le marché pose des problèmes, je l'admets: don't forget le">
  Learners who front a *que* clause often leave the main verb without its object. The clause must be taken up by a pronoun: **que le marché pose des problèmes, je l'admets**, **qu'ils soient gênés, je le comprends**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Record yourself once">
  Record your speech on your phone and listen to it the next day, as a member of the audience. You'll hear at once where a pause is missing, where a device sounds forced, and whether the ending really lands.
</Callout>
