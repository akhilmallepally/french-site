By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand the most common French proverbs, recognize them when people half-quote or twist them, and translate them by an equivalent rather than word for word.

## What you already know

You know many French idioms from <LessonLink id="b2-idioms-everyday" text="the lesson on everyday idioms" />, such as *il pleut des cordes* and *appeler un chat un chat*, and the term *une expression imagée*. An idiom is a phrase you use inside a sentence; a proverb is a whole sentence that states a general truth. This lesson is about proverbs.

## What a proverb is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

*Un proverbe* is a short, fixed sentence that gives advice or states a truth about life: *mieux vaut tard que jamais*. *Un dicton* is similar but often more local or practical, about the weather or the seasons. Both have a fixed form: you don't change their words.

People introduce a proverb with *comme dit le proverbe* or *comme on dit*, or simply drop it into the conversation.

<Example fr="Comme dit le proverbe, mieux vaut tard que jamais." en="As the saying goes, better late than never." />
<Example fr="Mon grand-père connaît un dicton pour chaque saison." en="My grandfather knows a saying for every season." />

## Effort and patience

<VocabTable group="effort" />

Many French proverbs encourage effort. *Qui ne risque rien n'a rien* (nothing ventured, nothing gained) pushes someone to try. *Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid* praises slow, steady work. *C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron* says that you learn by doing. *Vouloir, c'est pouvoir* says that will is enough; in speech, people also say *quand on veut, on peut*.

<Example fr="Tu hésites à postuler ? Qui ne risque rien n'a rien !" en="You're hesitating to apply? Nothing ventured, nothing gained!" />
<Example fr="Ton français progresse : petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid." en="Your French is improving: slow and steady wins the race." />
<Example fr="Tu peux réussir : vouloir, c'est pouvoir." en="You can succeed: where there's a will, there's a way." />

## Caution and judgement

<VocabTable group="caution" />

Other proverbs warn. *Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué* means don't count on a success too early. *L'habit ne fait pas le moine* warns against judging by appearances. *Il n'y a pas de fumée sans feu* suggests that a rumour has some basis, and it's often used to justify suspicion. *La nuit porte conseil* advises waiting before deciding. *Les absents ont toujours tort* observes that people who aren't there get the blame.

<Example fr="On n'a pas encore signé le contrat : il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué." en="We haven't signed the contract yet: don't count your chickens before they're hatched." />
<Example fr="Ne décide pas ce soir : la nuit porte conseil." en="Don't decide tonight: sleep on it." />
<Example fr="Tout le monde en parle, et il n'y a pas de fumée sans feu." en="Everyone is talking about it, and there's no smoke without fire." />
<Example fr="Il n'était pas à la réunion, et comme on dit, les absents ont toujours tort." en="He wasn't at the meeting, and as they say, the absent are always in the wrong." />

## Life and people

<VocabTable group="life" />

*Après la pluie, le beau temps* comforts someone after a difficulty. *Tout vient à point à qui sait attendre* praises patience. *Qui se ressemble s'assemble* notes that people of the same kind stay together, often with a hint of criticism.

<Example fr="Courage, après la pluie, le beau temps !" en="Chin up, things will get better!" />
<Example fr="Sois patient : tout vient à point à qui sait attendre." en="Be patient: good things come to those who wait." />
<Example fr="Ils sont toujours ensemble : qui se ressemble s'assemble." en="They're always together: birds of a feather flock together." />

## Half-quoting and twisting proverbs

Because everyone knows them, French speakers often say only the first half of a proverb, and leave the listener to finish it: *bon, qui ne risque rien…*, *tu sais ce qu'on dit : petit à petit…*. Saying the whole proverb can even sound a little heavy in conversation.

Writers and advertisers also twist proverbs for effect, changing one word: a sports article might say that a team *a vendu la peau de l'ours trop tôt*. Recognizing the original is the key to the joke.

<Example fr="L'équipe a vendu la peau de l'ours trop tôt : elle a perdu dans les dernières minutes." en="The team counted its chickens too early: it lost in the last minutes." />

## Translating proverbs

Don't translate a proverb word for word by default. Look for the proverb that does the same job in the other language. Sometimes the two match closely (*mieux vaut tard que jamais*, better late than never); often they use different images (*il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours*, don't count your chickens). If there's no equivalent, translate the meaning plainly.

As a rule of thumb, use one proverb per conversation or text at most: a proverb gives weight to a point, but several in a row sound like a parody.

## En français

Inès has been offered a job abroad. She talks to her grandmother.

- *Mamie, on m'a proposé un poste à Montréal, mais j'ai peur.* (Grandma, I've been offered a job in Montreal, but I'm scared.)
- *Peur de quoi ? Qui ne risque rien…* (Scared of what? Nothing ventured…)
- *… n'a rien, je sais. Mais je ne parle pas encore bien anglais.* (… nothing gained, I know. But I don't speak English well yet.)
- *C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron. Tu apprendras sur place.* (Practice makes perfect. You'll learn there.)
- *Et si ça ne marche pas ?* (And what if it doesn't work out?)
- *Tu n'as pas encore commencé ! Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours…* (You haven't even started! Don't count your chickens…)
- *Mamie, ce proverbe parle d'un succès, pas d'un échec.* (Grandma, that proverb is about success, not failure.)
- *Tu as raison. Alors : après la pluie, le beau temps !* (You're right. Then: things will get better!)
- *Tu as un proverbe pour tout.* (You have a proverb for everything.)
- *À mon âge, on en a appris quelques-uns. Et ne décide pas ce soir.* (At my age, you've learned a few. And don't decide tonight.)
- *La nuit porte conseil, c'est ça ?* (Sleep on it, is that it?)
- *Exactement. Mieux vaut décider tard que mal.* (Exactly. Better to decide late than badly.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Proverbs have a fixed form">
  Learners often change a word in a proverb, which makes it sound wrong rather than creative. Keep the exact form: **l'habit ne fait pas le moine**, **les absents ont toujours tort**. Twist a proverb only when you know the original well and want a deliberate effect.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn the first half">
  For each proverb, learn the first half by heart and say the rest aloud only when you're sure. French speakers will finish it for you, and recognizing the first half in a conversation is what matters most.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un proverbe est une phrase courte et fixe qui donne un conseil ou dit une vérité sur la vie. On ne change pas ses mots. Certains proverbes encouragent l'effort : qui ne risque rien n'a rien, petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid, c'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron. D'autres conseillent d'être prudent : il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué, l'habit ne fait pas le moine, la nuit porte conseil. Après la pluie, le beau temps fait du bien après une difficulté. À l'oral, on dit souvent seulement le début du proverbe, et l'autre personne comprend la suite. Pour traduire un proverbe, il faut chercher un proverbe qui a le même sens, et pas traduire mot à mot. Mieux vaut dire un seul proverbe à la fois.
