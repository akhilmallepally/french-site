## Euphemisms

<VocabTable group="soft" />

*Un euphémisme* replaces a hard or unpleasant word with a softer one. To *euphémiser* is to play something down with gentle words. French uses euphemisms for three reasons, and it helps to know which one is at work.

**Respect.** Some euphemisms have simply become the standard, respectful term. *Une personne âgée* (an older person) is the usual word for someone old; *une personne en situation de handicap* (a person with a disability) puts the person first; *un non-voyant* is a polite word for a blind person. In public and professional French, use these terms.

<Example fr="Ce service de transport est réservé aux personnes âgées et aux personnes en situation de handicap." en="This transport service is reserved for older people and people with disabilities." />

**Politeness and grief.** To speak of a death gently, people say *il nous a quittés* (he has passed away) or *elle a disparu* (she has gone); you know *disparaître* from <LessonLink id="b1-plus-que-parfait" text="the lesson on the plus-que-parfait" />.

<Example fr="Notre ancien directeur nous a quittés la semaine dernière." en="Our former director passed away last week." />

**Administration and business.** Official language also softens. *Un demandeur d'emploi* (a jobseeker) is the administrative term for someone looking for work, more neutral than *un chômeur*. *Un plan social* means a plan to make employees redundant; its official name today is *un plan de sauvegarde de l'emploi* (literally, a job protection plan), itself a euphemism. *Un technicien de surface* (a cleaner) is often quoted, and mocked, as an example of over-polite language.

<Example fr="L'entreprise a annoncé un plan social : deux cents postes seront supprimés." en="The company announced a redundancy plan: two hundred jobs will be cut." />
<Example fr="Les demandeurs d'emploi peuvent se former sans payer." en="Jobseekers can get training without paying." />

Views on these softer words differ. For some, they show respect and avoid hurting people. For others, some of them hide reality: a reader should know that *un plan social* means job losses. As a reader, translate each euphemism back into plain words before you judge the text; as a writer, use the respectful terms, and avoid the ones that hide facts.

A euphemism is close to the litote you know: both say less. The difference is the aim. A litote says less to mean more (*ce n'est pas mal*, it's very good), while a euphemism says less to soften. In irony, a speaker may also use a euphemism on purpose, to make a hard fact sound absurdly gentle.

<Example fr="Le journaliste refuse d'euphémiser : il parle de licenciements, pas de plan social." en="The journalist refuses to soften it: he talks about redundancies, not a &quot;social plan&quot;." />
<Example fr="« Technicien de surface » est un euphémisme dont on se moque souvent." en="&quot;Technicien de surface&quot; is a euphemism that people often make fun of." />

## Near-synonyms: strength

<VocabTable group="near" />

*Un synonyme* has the same meaning as another word; in practice, most are *des quasi-synonymes* (near-synonyms) that differ in strength or register. Learn them as ladders.

| Weaker or neutral | Stronger | Strongest |
|---|---|---|
| *la crainte* (apprehension, formal) | *la peur* (fear) | *la terreur*; *l'effroi* (literary) |
| *content* (pleased) | *ravi* (delighted) | *comblé* (overjoyed, fulfilled) |
| *regarder* (to look at) | *observer* (to watch closely) | *contempler* (to gaze at with admiration) |

<Example fr="Elle a exprimé sa crainte d'un nouvel échec." en="She expressed her apprehension of another failure." />
<Example fr="Pendant la guerre, les habitants vivaient dans la terreur." en="During the war, the inhabitants lived in terror." />
<Example fr="Il resta immobile, saisi d'effroi." en="He stood still, seized with dread. (literary)" />
<Example fr="Avec sa famille et son travail, elle se sent comblée." en="With her family and her work, she feels fulfilled." />
<Example fr="Le chercheur observe les oiseaux depuis dix ans ; le voyageur, lui, contemple la mer." en="The researcher has been observing birds for ten years; the traveller, for his part, gazes at the sea." />

## Near-synonyms: register

Some synonyms differ less in strength than in register. Un *problème* is neutral; *un souci* (a worry, a problem) is everyday and slightly softer; *une difficulté* is neutral to formal; *une préoccupation* (a concern) belongs to formal writing.

<Example fr="J'ai un petit souci avec mon ordinateur." en="I've got a little problem with my computer. (everyday)" />
<Example fr="La sécurité des usagers est notre première préoccupation." en="The safety of users is our first concern. (formal)" />

As a rule of thumb, when two words seem equal, ask two questions: is one stronger, and is one more formal? Then choose for your reader, as you learned in the lesson on register shifting.

## En français

Two journalists, Hugo and Samira, check a headline before it goes online.

- *Tu as vu le titre ? « L'usine annonce un plan social ».* (Have you seen the headline? "The factory announces a social plan".)
- *C'est un euphémisme. Nos lecteurs doivent comprendre qu'il y aura des licenciements.* (That's a euphemism. Our readers must understand that there will be redundancies.)
- *D'accord, on écrit « L'usine va supprimer des emplois ».* (OK, we write "The factory is going to cut jobs".)
- *Et dans l'article, tu parles des « vieux » du quartier.* (And in the article, you talk about the "old people" of the neighbourhood.)
- *Oui, c'est un peu brutal. Je mets « les personnes âgées ».* (Yes, it's a bit harsh. I'll put "older people".)
- *Là, l'euphémisme est justifié : c'est le terme respectueux.* (There, the euphemism is justified: it's the respectful term.)
- *Et « la peur des salariés », c'est assez fort ?* (And "the employees' fear", is that strong enough?)
- *Pour un article, je dirais « l'inquiétude ». « La terreur », ce serait exagéré.* (For an article, I'd say "worry". "Terror" would be exaggerated.)
- *Tu as raison. Il faut le mot juste, ni plus fort ni plus faible.* (You're right. You need the right word, neither stronger nor weaker.)
- *Exactement. On ne doit ni euphémiser ni exagérer.* (Exactly. We must neither play it down nor exaggerate.)
- *Bon, je relis une dernière fois et je publie.* (Right, I'll reread it one last time and publish.)
- *Parfait. Le directeur sera ravi.* (Perfect. The editor will be delighted.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une personne âgée, not une vieille personne, in public French">
  Learners often translate "an old person" word for word. In public, professional or written French, use the respectful term: **une personne âgée**, **les personnes âgées**. *Un vieux* or *une vieille* can sound rude.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Build a three-step ladder">
  Each time you learn a word for a feeling, look for one weaker and one stronger word, and write the three on one line: *contrarié, fâché, furieux*. When you write, check that the step you chose matches the facts, neither softer nor stronger.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un euphémisme remplace un mot dur par un mot plus doux, et euphémiser, c'est présenter une réalité de façon moins dure. Certains euphémismes sont devenus les termes respectueux : on dit une personne âgée, une personne en situation de handicap, un non-voyant, et un demandeur d'emploi dans la langue administrative. Pour parler d'une mort, on dit qu'une personne nous a quittés. D'autres euphémismes cachent une réalité : un plan social annonce des licenciements, et le mot technicien de surface fait souvent rire. Il faut donc traduire chaque euphémisme en mots simples avant de juger un texte. Les quasi-synonymes se distinguent par la force et par le registre : la crainte est moins forte que la peur, et la terreur ou l'effroi sont plus forts. Un souci est courant, une préoccupation est soutenue. On regarde, on observe avec attention, on contemple ce qu'on trouve beau, et une personne comblée a tout ce qu'elle voulait.
