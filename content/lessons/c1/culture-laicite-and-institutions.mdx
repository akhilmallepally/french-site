By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain what laïcité means in French law and how it's applied, and describe the main institutions of the Fifth Republic, in neutral, precise French.

## What you already know

You know *la laïcité* and the law of 1905 in outline from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-french-republic" text="the lesson on the French Republic" />. You know *l'Assemblée nationale*, *le Sénat*, *le gouvernement* and *le Premier ministre* from <LessonLink id="b2-politics-and-society" text="the lesson on politics and society" />, and *la Constitution*, *le Conseil constitutionnel* and *le Conseil d'État* from <LessonLink id="c1-law-and-institutions" text="the lesson on law and institutions" />. This lesson looks at laïcité as a set of rules, and at how the institutions share power.

## Laïcité in the law

<VocabTable group="law" />

**The 1905 law.** The law of 9 December 1905 organized *la séparation des Églises et de l'État*. Its first article says that the Republic ensures *la liberté de conscience* (freedom of conscience) and guarantees *le libre exercice des cultes* (the free exercise of worship), within the limits of public order. Its second article says that the Republic does not recognize, pay or *subventionner* (subsidize) any religion.

<Example fr="La République garantit la liberté de conscience et le libre exercice des cultes." en="The Republic guarantees freedom of conscience and the free exercise of worship." />
<Example fr="Depuis 1905, l'État ne subventionne aucun culte." en="Since 1905, the State hasn't subsidized any religion." />

**The Constitution.** The Constitution of 1958, adopted by referendum that year, begins with this sentence:

<Example fr="« La France est une République indivisible, laïque, démocratique et sociale. » (Constitution de 1958, article 1)" en="&quot;France is an indivisible, secular, democratic and social Republic.&quot;" />

*Laïque* (secular) describes the State and its institutions, not the citizens: the State is neutral, and citizens are free to believe or not to believe, and to practise their religion.

## How laïcité is applied

<VocabTable group="apply" />

**Public services.** The State and public services must respect *la neutralité* (neutrality). This applies to *les agents publics* (public employees): at work, they don't show their religious beliefs, and they treat everyone equally. The people who use public services, *les usagers*, keep their freedom of conscience, within the rules of each service.

<Example fr="Au travail, un agent public doit respecter la neutralité." en="At work, a public employee must respect neutrality." />

**State schools.** *L'école publique* (state schools) gives no religious teaching. A law adopted in 2004 forbids pupils in state primary and secondary schools from wearing *des signes religieux ostensibles* (conspicuous religious symbols). This rule doesn't apply at university, and private schools under contract with the State may have a religious character.

<Example fr="À l'école publique, les élèves ne peuvent pas porter de signes religieux ostensibles." en="In state schools, pupils can't wear conspicuous religious symbols." />

**Exceptions.** The 1905 law itself provides for *des aumôniers* (chaplains) in places that people can't easily leave, such as hospitals and prisons; the army also has its chaplains. And in Alsace and Moselle, which were German in 1905, *le régime concordataire* (the Concordat system) still applies: there, some religions have an official status.

<Example fr="En Alsace et en Moselle, le régime concordataire existe encore." en="In Alsace and Moselle, the Concordat system still exists." />
<Example fr="À l'hôpital, un aumônier peut rendre visite aux malades qui le demandent." en="In hospital, a chaplain can visit patients who ask for it." />

How to apply laïcité in particular cases is regularly debated, and courts, especially the Conseil d'État, often decide where the line falls. In French, present these debates as questions about the rules, and attribute each position; this course doesn't take sides.

## The institutions of the Fifth Republic

<VocabTable group="power" />

**The President.** The President of the Republic is *le chef de l'État* (the head of State), elected by *le suffrage universel direct* (direct universal suffrage). He or she *nomme* (appoints) the Prime Minister and can dissolve the Assemblée nationale, as you saw in the lesson on May 68.

**The government.** The Prime Minister leads the government, which runs the country's policy and answers to the Assemblée nationale.

**Parliament.** The Parliament, made of the Assemblée nationale and the Sénat, *vote les lois* (passes the laws) and checks the government's action. The Sénat is elected indirectly, by local elected representatives, and represents the local authorities. When the two chambers disagree on a bill, the Assemblée nationale can, in the end, have the last word.

**The guardians.** The Conseil constitutionnel checks that laws respect the Constitution, and the Conseil d'État advises the government and judges disputes with the administration. Laïcité, written in the Constitution, is one of the principles they protect.

<Example fr="Le président de la République est le chef de l'État." en="The President of the Republic is the head of State." />
<Example fr="Le président est élu au suffrage universel direct, et il nomme le Premier ministre." en="The President is elected by direct universal suffrage, and he or she appoints the Prime Minister." />
<Example fr="Le Parlement vote les lois, et le Conseil constitutionnel vérifie qu'elles respectent la Constitution." en="Parliament passes the laws, and the Conseil constitutionnel checks that they respect the Constitution." />

## A short article

The article below is an original text written for this lesson.

*La laïcité au quotidien : ce que dit la règle*

*En France, la laïcité n'est pas une opinion : c'est un principe écrit dans la Constitution. Elle repose sur deux idées. D'abord, l'État est neutre : il n'a pas de religion officielle et ne subventionne aucun culte. Ensuite, chaque personne est libre de croire ou de ne pas croire, et de pratiquer sa religion dans le respect de l'ordre public.*

*Cette règle ne concerne pas tout le monde de la même façon. À la mairie, à la poste ou à l'hôpital, les agents publics doivent respecter la neutralité : ils ne montrent pas leurs convictions religieuses pendant leur travail. Les usagers, eux, gardent leur liberté.*

*À l'école publique, la situation est particulière. Depuis une loi de 2004, les élèves des écoles, des collèges et des lycées publics ne peuvent pas porter de signes religieux ostensibles. À l'université, en revanche, les étudiants sont libres.*

*La laïcité protège donc à la fois l'État et les religions : l'État ne dépend d'aucune religion, et aucune religion ne dépend de l'État. Les élèves étudient d'ailleurs ce principe en classe, comme les autres principes de la République.*

*La loi de 1905 prévoit aussi des exceptions : des aumôniers peuvent être présents à l'hôpital ou en prison. L'armée a, elle aussi, ses aumôniers. Et en Alsace et en Moselle, qui étaient allemandes en 1905, un régime particulier existe toujours.*

*Quand l'application de la règle pose une question, ce sont souvent les juges, en particulier ceux du Conseil d'État, qui la tranchent. Le principe reste le même : la neutralité de l'État, et la liberté de chacun.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Laïcité in everyday life: what the rule says. In France, laïcité isn't an opinion: it is a principle written into the Constitution. It rests on two ideas. First, the State is neutral: it has no official religion and doesn't subsidize any religion. Second, everyone is free to believe or not to believe, and to practise their religion while respecting public order. This rule doesn't concern everyone in the same way. At the town hall, the post office or the hospital, public employees must respect neutrality: they don't show their religious beliefs during their work. Users, for their part, keep their freedom. In state schools, the situation is particular. Since a law of 2004, pupils in state primary schools, collèges and lycées can't wear conspicuous religious symbols. At university, on the other hand, students are free. Laïcité therefore protects both the State and religions: the State depends on no religion, and no religion depends on the State. Pupils in fact study this principle in class, like the other principles of the Republic. The 1905 law also provides for exceptions: chaplains can be present in hospitals or in prisons. The army, too, has its chaplains. And in Alsace and Moselle, which were German in 1905, a special system still exists. When applying the rule raises a question, it's often judges, in particular those of the Conseil d'État, who settle it. The principle stays the same: the neutrality of the State, and the freedom of each person.)

<Callout type="warning" title="L'État est laïque, not les Français sont laïques">
  Learners often apply *laïque* to people, as if it meant "not religious". In law, it describes the State and its institutions: **l'État est laïque**, **l'école publique est laïque**. A citizen can be religious or not; the State stays neutral.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Check who the rule applies to">
  Before you write a sentence about laïcité, ask who the rule concerns: the State, a public employee, a pupil, a student or a user. Most mistakes about laïcité come from applying a rule meant for one of them to another.
</Callout>
