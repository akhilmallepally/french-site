By the end of this lesson you'll be able to move *seulement*, *même* and *aussi* to change what a sentence means, use sentence adverbs to comment on what you say, and place long adverbs in compound tenses.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="a2-adverbs-ment" text="the lesson on adverbs" />, you placed short adverbs between the auxiliary and the participle (*j'ai bien mangé*) and most *-ment* adverbs after it, as a guide. You know *seulement* from <LessonLink id="b1-ne-que-ni-ni" text="the lesson on ne… que and ni… ni" />, and *aussi* with inversion (therefore) from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />, which <LessonLink id="c1-stylistic-inversion" text="the lesson on stylistic inversion" /> came back to. This lesson is about how the position of an adverb can change its meaning.

## Seulement: what it restricts

<VocabTable group="position" />

*Seulement* restricts the word it stands next to, usually the one right after it. Put it where the restriction belongs.

<Example fr="Il travaille seulement le matin." en="He works only in the morning." />
<Example fr="Il a parlé seulement à Marie." en="He spoke only to Marie (to nobody else)." />

Between the auxiliary and the participle, *seulement* can restrict what follows it or the action itself. *Il a seulement lu le résumé* usually means he read the summary and not the book. Said with stress on *lu*, it can mean that he only read it and did nothing more with it. In speech, the voice shows what's restricted; in writing, put *seulement* right before the word you mean, or use *ne… que*: *il n'a lu que le résumé*.

At the start of a sentence, followed by a comma, *seulement* changes meaning: it introduces a drawback, like "the only thing is" or "but".

<Example fr="Le restaurant est très bon. Seulement, il est cher." en="The restaurant is very good. The only thing is, it's expensive." />

## Même: even, or itself

*Même* has two meanings, and its position tells them apart.

- **Before a noun or a pronoun**, *même* means "even": *même Paul a ri* (even Paul laughed).
- **After a stressed pronoun**, joined with a hyphen, it means "himself, herself": *lui-même*, *elle-même*. In the plural, *même* agrees: *nous-mêmes*, *eux-mêmes*.
- **After a noun**, it means "very" or "itself": *le jour même* (that very day).

<Example fr="Même les enfants ont compris." en="Even the children understood." />
<Example fr="Le directeur lui-même est venu nous voir." en="The director himself came to see us." />
<Example fr="Il a reçu la lettre et il est parti le jour même." en="He got the letter and left that very day." />

Compare *le même jour* (the same day), where *même* is an adjective before the noun. After the verb, *même* means "even" and stresses the action: *il a même ri* (he even laughed). So *même Paul a ri* and *Paul a même ri* don't say the same thing: in the first, Paul is the surprise; in the second, the laughing is.

## Aussi: also, or therefore

Inside the sentence, *aussi* means "also": *nous sommes aussi restés à la maison* (we also stayed at home). At the start of a sentence, followed by inversion, it means "therefore" in formal writing.

<Example fr="Il pleuvait. Aussi sommes-nous restés à la maison." en="It was raining. We therefore stayed at home. (formal)" />

In everyday French, say *donc* or *alors* for "therefore", and keep *aussi* for "also".

## Sentence adverbs

<VocabTable group="sentence" />

Some adverbs don't describe how something is done: they comment on the whole sentence. This is *un adverbe de phrase* (a sentence adverb). You know *heureusement* from <LessonLink id="a2-adverbs-ment" text="the lesson on adverbs" />, *franchement* from <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-opinions" text="the lesson on expressing opinions" />, and *apparemment* from <LessonLink id="b1-conditionnel-present" text="the lesson on the conditionnel présent" />.

A sentence adverb often goes at the start with a comma, after the conjugated verb, or at the end after a comma. The meaning stays the same.

<Example fr="Visiblement, elle était fatiguée." en="She was visibly tired." />
<Example fr="Elle était visiblement fatiguée." en="She was visibly tired." />

Some adverbs can be either kind, and the position helps you tell them apart. At the start with a comma, *franchement* and *sérieusement* comment on the sentence. After the verb, with no comma, they describe the manner.

<Example fr="Franchement, ce film est trop long." en="Frankly, this film is too long." />
<Example fr="Il nous a parlé franchement." en="He spoke to us frankly (in a frank way)." />
<Example fr="Sérieusement, tu pars demain ?" en="Seriously, you're leaving tomorrow?" />
<Example fr="Il fait son travail sérieusement." en="He does his work seriously." />

The four sentence adverbs below have different shades:

- **Visiblement** means "from what you can see": *il est visiblement content*.
- **Apparemment** means "from what people say or how it looks": *apparemment, la réunion est annulée*.
- **Décidément** reacts to something that has happened again: *décidément, tu es toujours en retard !*
- **Forcément** means "necessarily", and *pas forcément* means "not necessarily". Put *pas* first: *ce n'est pas forcément vrai*.

<Example fr="Un examen difficile n'est pas forcément un mauvais examen." en="A difficult exam isn't necessarily a bad exam." />

## Long adverbs in compound tenses

<VocabTable group="long" />

At A2, you put most *-ment* adverbs after the participle. At this level, you'll see that many of them can also go between the auxiliary and the participle. Treat what follows as tendencies, not laws.

- Adverbs of degree and completeness (*complètement*, *totalement*) and of time (*longuement*) usually go before the participle, and they can also follow it.
- Sentence adverbs (*visiblement*, *probablement*, *heureusement*) usually go before the participle.
- Adverbs of manner that say how the action was done (*attentivement*, *soigneusement*, *lentement*) more often go after it.

<Example fr="Il a complètement oublié la réunion." en="He completely forgot the meeting." />
<Example fr="J'y ai longuement réfléchi." en="I thought about it at length." />
<Example fr="Elle a probablement raté son train." en="She's probably missed her train." />
<Example fr="Relisez attentivement votre copie avant de la rendre." en="Reread your paper carefully before you hand it in." />

With an infinitive, a short adverb usually comes before it: *il faut bien relire*. A long adverb of manner usually follows: *il faut relire soigneusement chaque phrase*.

## The whole list

- Restriction: *seulement* before the word it restricts; *Seulement,* at the start: "the only thing is".
- Even or very: *même* + noun means even; noun + *même*, very; *lui-même*, himself; verb + *même*, even (the action).
- Also or therefore: *aussi* inside the sentence means also; *Aussi* + inversion at the start, therefore (formal).
- Sentence adverbs: at the start, after the verb, or at the end, with no change of meaning.
- Two kinds at once: *franchement* and *sérieusement* comment at the start, and give the manner after the verb.
- Long adverbs in compound tenses: degree, time and sentence adverbs usually before the participle; manner more often after it.

## En français

Lucas and Emma, two students, have just come out of an exam.

- *Alors, l'examen ? Tu as tout fini ?* (So, the exam? Did you finish everything?)
- *Non, j'ai seulement fini la première partie.* (No, I only finished the first part.)
- *Moi aussi ! Même Julie n'a pas eu le temps.* (Me too! Even Julie didn't have time.)
- *Julie ? Elle finit toujours en avance, pourtant.* (Julie? She always finishes early, though.)
- *Oui, mais aujourd'hui, elle était visiblement stressée.* (Yes, but today she was visibly stressed.)
- *Apparemment, le professeur va donner plus de temps la prochaine fois.* (Apparently, the teacher is going to give more time next time.)
- *Qui t'a dit ça ?* (Who told you that?)
- *Julie elle-même. Elle lui a parlé juste après l'examen.* (Julie herself. She spoke to him right after the exam.)
- *Décidément, elle pense à tout !* (She really does think of everything!)
- *Et toi, tu as relu tes réponses ?* (And you, did you read over your answers?)
- *J'ai seulement relu la fin, et pas très attentivement.* (I only read over the end, and not very carefully.)
- *Ce n'est pas forcément grave. On verra bien !* (That's not necessarily a problem. We'll see!)
- *Seulement, si mes notes sont mauvaises, mes parents vont être déçus.* (The only thing is, if my marks are bad, my parents will be disappointed.)
- *Tu as longuement révisé. Ça va aller.* (You revised for a long time. It'll be fine.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Pas forcément, not forcément pas">
  Learners often put *forcément* where English puts "necessarily". For "not necessarily", *pas* comes first: **ce n'est pas forcément vrai**. *Ce n'est forcément pas vrai* says something else: that it's necessarily untrue.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Move one word, read three sentences">
  Take one short sentence of your own and move *même* or *seulement* into every possible slot: *même Paul a ri*, *Paul a même ri*, *Paul lui-même a ri*. Write the English meaning next to each. Doing this with your own sentences fixes the positions faster than any table.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La place d'un adverbe peut changer le sens de la phrase. Seulement se met devant le mot qu'il limite : il a parlé seulement à Marie. Au début de la phrase, suivi d'une virgule, seulement présente un problème : le restaurant est bon. Seulement, il est cher. Même devant un nom montre une surprise : même Paul a ri. Après un pronom, lui-même ou eux-mêmes insiste sur la personne, et le jour même veut dire ce jour-là, pas un autre. Aussi au milieu de la phrase ajoute une idée, mais au début, avec l'inversion, il veut dire donc : aussi sommes-nous restés à la maison. Un adverbe de phrase, comme visiblement, décidément ou forcément, donne l'avis de la personne qui parle sur toute la phrase. Au passé composé, complètement et longuement se mettent souvent avant le participe, et attentivement plutôt après. Ce sont des habitudes, pas des règles fixes.
