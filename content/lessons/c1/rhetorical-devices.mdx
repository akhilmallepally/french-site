By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize the main figures of style in a speech or an essay, name them in French, and use a few of them yourself.

## What you already know

You know rhetorical questions such as *n'est-il pas vrai que* and the appeals *avouez que* and *convenez que* from <LessonLink id="b2-persuasion-and-hedging" text="the lesson on persuasion and hedging" />, and *l'antithèse* as a part of the essay from <LessonLink id="b2-argumentative-essay" text="the lesson on the argumentative essay" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-review-style" text="the review of style" />, you used *un effet de style* with restraint. This lesson gives you the names of the devices themselves.

## Why name the devices

<VocabTable group="terms" />

*Une figure de style* is a way of saying something that gives it more force than the plain version. French schools teach these devices by name, and essays, commentaries and debates use the names freely. Knowing them helps you in two ways: you notice *le procédé* when a speaker uses it on you, and you can choose one when you want a sentence to *marquer les esprits*.

<Example fr="Ce slogan est percutant : il marque les esprits." en="This slogan is punchy: it sticks in people's minds." />

## Repetition and rhythm

<VocabTable group="repeat" />

*L'anaphore* repeats the same words at the start of several sentences or clauses. It builds *le rythme* and makes a list sound like a march. *La gradation* lists words or ideas in increasing (or decreasing) strength.

<Example fr="Nous voulons une ville propre. Nous voulons une ville sûre. Nous voulons une ville pour tous." en="We want a clean town. We want a safe town. We want a town for everyone." />
<Example fr="Il faut agir aujourd'hui, demain, toujours." en="We must act today, tomorrow, always." />
<Example fr="C'est une erreur, une faute, presque un scandale." en="It's a mistake, a fault, almost a scandal." />

As a rule of thumb, three items are enough: a fourth repetition starts to sound mechanical, and the audience stops listening to the words and hears only the pattern.

## Opposition

<VocabTable group="oppose" />

You know *l'antithèse* as the second part of a dialectical essay; as a figure of style, it places two opposite ideas side by side in one sentence. *L'oxymore* goes further and joins two opposite words directly. *La question rhétorique* asks something that needs no answer: the answer is obvious, and the question makes the point more strongly than a statement.

<Example fr="Ils parlent beaucoup et agissent peu." en="They talk a lot and act little. (antithesis)" />
<Example fr="C'était une victoire amère." en="It was a bitter victory. (oxymoron)" />
<Example fr="Qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ?" en="Who can still believe that nothing will change? (rhetorical question)" />

## Saying more, saying less

<VocabTable group="degree" />

*La litote* says less in order to mean more, usually by denying the opposite: *ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée* means "it's a good idea". *L'hyperbole* does the reverse: it *exagère* on purpose. Both are common in everyday speech as well as in speeches.

<Example fr="Ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée." en="It's not a bad idea (it's a good one)." />
<Example fr="Je vous l'ai dit mille fois !" en="I've told you a thousand times! (hyperbole)" />

The litote is especially French: a speaker who says *ce n'était pas mal* often means it was very good. <LessonLink id="c1-irony-and-understatement" text="The lesson on irony and understatement" /> comes back to it.

## Images

<VocabTable group="image" />

*Une comparaison* links two things with a word such as *comme*: *il est rusé comme un renard*. *La métaphore* makes the link without the word: *ce débat est une guerre*. Both turn an abstract idea into an image the *auditoire* can see. A *slogan* often combines an image with rhythm.

<Example fr="Ce débat est une guerre." en="This debate is a war. (metaphor)" />
<Example fr="Le temps file comme le vent." en="Time flies like the wind. (simile)" />

## Using devices yourself

At C1, the aim is to recognize every device and to use a few well. As a rule of thumb, one or two devices per paragraph are enough in an essay, and three or four in a whole speech. Choose the device by its effect:

- **To make a list memorable**: anaphora or gradation.
- **To contrast two positions**: antithesis.
- **To involve the audience**: a rhetorical question.
- **To soften or to be ironic**: litotes.
- **To make an abstract idea concrete**: a metaphor or a comparison.

Avoid hyperbole in a formal argument: an exaggeration that a reader can check weakens your credibility. In a speech, by contrast, a single hyperbole at the right moment can wake up a tired audience.

## The whole list

| Device | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| anaphore | repeats the start | *Nous voulons… Nous voulons…* |
| gradation | increasing strength | *aujourd'hui, demain, toujours* |
| antithèse | opposite ideas side by side | *parlent beaucoup, agissent peu* |
| oxymore | opposite words joined | *une victoire amère* |
| question rhétorique | a question with an obvious answer | *Qui peut encore croire… ?* |
| litote | says less to mean more | *ce n'est pas mal* |
| hyperbole | deliberate exaggeration | *mille fois* |

## En français

Hugo and Clara are preparing a short speech for their university debating club.

- *Notre discours est clair, mais il ne marque pas les esprits.* (Our speech is clear, but it doesn't stick in people's minds.)
- *On pourrait commencer par une question rhétorique.* (We could start with a rhetorical question. (spoken))
- *Par exemple : « Qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ? »* (For example: "Who can still believe that nothing will change?")
- *Bien. Et pour nos trois arguments, une anaphore ?* (Good. And for our three arguments, an anaphora?)
- *« Nous voulons… Nous voulons… Nous voulons… » Ça donne du rythme.* ("We want… We want… We want…" That gives it rhythm.)
- *Attention, pas plus de trois fois, sinon on ne l'écoute plus.* (Careful, no more than three times, otherwise people stop listening.)
- *Et j'ai écrit : « Ce projet coûtera des millions de millions. »* (And I wrote: "This project will cost millions and millions.")
- *C'est une hyperbole. Dans un débat, l'autre équipe va vérifier.* (That's hyperbole. In a debate, the other team will check.)
- *Tu as raison. J'écris plutôt : « Ce projet n'est pas bon marché. »* (You're right. I'll write instead: "This project isn't cheap.")
- *Une litote ! C'est plus fin, et l'auditoire comprend quand même.* (A litote! It's subtler, and the audience still understands.)
- *Et pour finir, une métaphore ?* (And to finish, a metaphor?)
- *« Ce débat est un pont entre nous », par exemple.* ("This debate is a bridge between us", for example.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée means it's a good idea">
  Learners often build a litote by negating the wrong word. To mean "good", deny the opposite: **ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée**. *Ce n'est pas une bonne idée* simply means it's a bad one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Annotate one speech">
  Find a short French speech, or a paragraph of an opinion piece, and mark every device you can name in the margin. After two or three texts, you'll start to hear the devices as you listen, and to see why a speaker chose each one.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Une figure de style donne plus de force à une idée. L'anaphore répète les mêmes mots au début de plusieurs phrases et crée un rythme : nous voulons une ville propre, nous voulons une ville sûre. La gradation présente des mots de plus en plus forts. L'antithèse place deux idées opposées côte à côte, et l'oxymore unit deux mots contraires, comme une victoire amère. La question rhétorique n'attend pas de réponse : qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ? La litote dit moins pour dire plus : ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée. L'hyperbole exagère pour frapper l'esprit. La comparaison et la métaphore créent des images. Dans une dissertation, il vaut mieux choisir peu de procédés, et toujours pour un effet précis, car l'auditoire remarque vite quand il y en a trop.
