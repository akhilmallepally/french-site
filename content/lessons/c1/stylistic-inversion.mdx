By the end of this lesson you'll be able to put the verb before its subject where careful written French expects it, and to tell when inversion is required, optional or wrong.

## What you already know

You know inversion in questions (*parlez-vous… ?*) from <LessonLink id="a1-yes-no-questions" text="the lesson on yes/no questions" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />, you met complex inversion with a noun subject (*Paul vient-il ?*) and inversion after *peut-être*, *sans doute* and *aussi* at the start of a sentence. You also used *à peine… que* with inversion in <LessonLink id="c1-passe-anterieur" text="the lesson on the passé antérieur" />. This lesson adds the other places where written French inverts.

## Three cases: required, optional, wrong

Stylistic inversion isn't a question. It's a word order that marks careful writing, and it follows a few patterns. In some places it's required: leaving it out sounds like a mistake. In others it's optional: it raises the register, and the plain order is also correct. In a few places it's wrong. Usage varies between writers, so treat the lists in this lesson as rules of thumb for careful modern prose.

The two forms work as in questions. With a pronoun subject, the pronoun follows the verb or the auxiliary, with *-t-* between two vowels. With a noun subject, the noun stays in front and a pronoun follows the verb: this is complex inversion.

<Example fr="Sans doute le témoin s'est-il trompé." en="The witness was probably mistaken. (formal)" />

## After adverbs at the start of a sentence

<VocabTable group="adverbs" />

You know *ainsi* (thus) from <LessonLink id="b2-logical-connectors" text="the lesson on logical connectors" />. At the start of a sentence, it can take inversion. With a comma and the normal order, it's also correct, and less formal.

<Example fr="Ainsi le lecteur comprend-il enfin la fin du roman." en="Thus the reader finally understands the ending of the novel. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Ainsi, le lecteur comprend enfin la fin du roman." en="Thus the reader finally understands the ending of the novel. (standard)" />

*Du moins* corrects or limits what was just said: "or at least". After a comma, inversion is common in writing, but the normal order is also correct.

<Example fr="La romancière a fini son livre, du moins le dit-elle." en="The novelist has finished her book, or at least that's what she says. (formal)" />

*Encore* at the start of a sentence, with inversion, means "still" or "even so": it adds a condition that limits what came before. In this meaning, inversion is required. In the middle of a sentence, *encore* keeps its usual meaning, "still", which you know from <LessonLink id="a2-negative-expressions" text="the lesson on negative expressions" />.

<Example fr="Le plan est bon. Encore faut-il que tout le monde soit d'accord." en="The plan is good. Even so, everyone still has to agree. (formal)" />

*Toujours est-il que* is a fixed phrase: the inversion is frozen inside it, and the clause after *que* keeps normal order. It means "the fact remains that" and moves past a doubt to what is certain.

<Example fr="Je ne sais pas pourquoi il est parti. Toujours est-il qu'il n'est pas revenu." en="I don't know why he left. The fact remains that he hasn't come back." />

When *à peine* opens a written sentence, careful writers invert, in any tense. Inside the sentence, *à peine* follows the auxiliary and there's no inversion.

<Example fr="À peine sommes-nous arrivés qu'il a commencé à pleuvoir." en="No sooner had we arrived than it started to rain. (formal)" />

## After direct speech

<VocabTable group="incises" />

When a reporting verb follows or interrupts a quotation, its subject comes after it. This short clause is called *une incise*. In written French, the inversion is required, whatever the tense.

- With a pronoun subject, the verb and the pronoun are joined by a hyphen, with *-t-* between two vowels: *dit-il*, *demanda-t-elle*, *a-t-il répondu*.
- With a noun subject, there's no extra pronoun: *répondit le témoin*, *dit Paul*. This is simple inversion, unlike complex inversion in questions.
- An incise can also sit inside the quotation, between two commas.

<Example fr="« Je pars demain », dit-elle." en="“I'm leaving tomorrow,” she said." />
<Example fr="« Déjà ? » s'étonna le romancier." en="“Already?” the novelist wondered." />
<Example fr="« Je sais, soupira-t-il, mais c'est trop tard. »" en="“I know,” he sighed, “but it's too late.”" />
<Example fr="« Vous avez tort », rétorqua la lectrice." en="“You're wrong,” the reader retorted." />

Novels use many reporting verbs besides *dire*: *murmurer*, *soupirer*, *s'étonner*, *rétorquer*, and *reprendre* (to go on speaking).

<Example fr="« Il faut partir, reprit-elle. Il faut partir maintenant », murmura-t-elle." en="“We have to leave,” she went on. “We have to leave now,” she whispered." />

In casual speech, you'll hear *« Je pars », il a dit* with normal order. It's fine when you talk, but in writing it reads as a mistake.

## In relative clauses

<VocabTable group="writing" />

After *que*, *où* and *dont*, a noun subject can follow the verb. It's optional, and writers use it when the verb is short and the subject is long, so that the sentence doesn't end on a lone verb.

<Example fr="La maison où vivait la romancière est aujourd'hui un musée." en="The house where the novelist lived is a museum today." />
<Example fr="C'est la règle que suivent beaucoup de lecteurs." en="It's the rule that many readers follow." />

This inversion is wrong in two cases. With a pronoun subject, keep normal order: *le livre qu'il lit*, not *que lit-il*. And when the verb has its own direct object, the subject must stay in front: *le jour où Paul a rencontré sa femme*.

## Where inversion is wrong

As a rule of thumb, don't invert in these places:

- After *peut-être que* and *sans doute que*: *que* replaces the inversion. Say *peut-être qu'il est malade* or *peut-être est-il malade*, never both.
- In a relative clause with a pronoun subject, or with a direct object after the verb.
- After *aussi* in the middle of a sentence, where it means "also".

## The whole list

- Required: in an incise (*dit-il*, *répondit le témoin*), after *encore* meaning "even so", and in *toujours est-il que*.
- Usual in careful writing: after *à peine*, *peut-être* and *sans doute* at the start of a sentence, and after *aussi* meaning "therefore".
- Optional: after *ainsi* and *du moins*, and in a relative clause with a noun subject.
- Wrong: after *peut-être que*, and in a relative clause with a pronoun subject or a direct object.

## En français

Inès reads the short story her colleague Théo wrote for the office newsletter. They speak in everyday spoken French and quote his text.

- *J'ai lu ton histoire. Elle est bien, mais il y a un problème dans les dialogues.* (I've read your story. It's good, but there's a problem in the dialogues.)
- *Quel problème ? J'ai écrit : « Je pars », il a dit.* (What problem? I wrote: "I'm leaving," he said.)
- *À l'écrit, on met le verbe avant le sujet : « Je pars », dit-il.* (In writing, you put the verb before the subject: "Je pars", dit-il.)
- *Et avec un prénom ? « Je pars », Paul dit-il ?* (And with a first name? "Je pars", Paul dit-il?)
- *Non, là, il n'y a pas de pronom : « Je pars », dit Paul.* (No, then there's no pronoun: "Je pars", dit Paul.)
- *D'accord. Et la phrase avec « peut-être » ?* (OK. And the sentence with peut-être?)
- *Tu as écrit « peut-être qu'il est malade ». C'est correct, mais c'est de l'oral.* (You wrote "peut-être qu'il est malade". It's correct, but it's spoken French.)
- *Alors j'écris « peut-être est-il malade » ?* (So I write "peut-être est-il malade"?)
- *Oui, c'est plus soutenu. Mais jamais « peut-être qu'est-il ».* (Yes, it's more formal. But never "peut-être qu'est-il".)
- *Et « toujours est-il que » ? Je l'ai vu dans un roman.* (And "toujours est-il que"? I saw it in a novel.)
- *Ça veut dire « en tout cas ». Par exemple : ton histoire a des erreurs, toujours est-il qu'elle me plaît !* (It means "anyway". For example: your story has mistakes, but the fact remains that I like it!)
- *Merci ! Je change les dialogues ce soir, du moins j'essaie.* (Thanks! I'll change the dialogues tonight, or at least I'll try.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Dit Paul, not Paul dit-il">
  Learners often use complex inversion in an incise because they know it from questions. After a quotation, a noun subject simply follows the verb: **« Je pars », dit Paul**, not *« Je pars », Paul dit-il*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Collect reporting verbs">
  When you read a novel, note every reporting verb you find in an incise, with its sentence: *murmura-t-elle*, *rétorqua le témoin*. After a few chapters, you'll have a list that lets your own dialogues go far beyond *dit-il*.
</Callout>

## En résumé

L'inversion stylistique met le verbe avant le sujet dans la langue écrite soignée. Avec un pronom, on écrit dit-il ou demanda-t-elle. Avec un nom, on garde le nom devant et on ajoute un pronom après le verbe : sans doute le témoin s'est-il trompé. Dans une incise, après une citation, l'inversion est obligatoire, et un nom sujet suit le verbe sans pronom : « Je pars », dit Paul. L'inversion est aussi obligatoire après encore au début de la phrase, quand il ajoute une condition, et dans toujours est-il que. Après à peine, peut-être et sans doute au début de la phrase, elle est la règle à l'écrit soigné. Après ainsi et du moins, elle est possible. Dans une relative, un nom sujet peut suivre le verbe, mais pas un pronom : le livre qu'il lit. Après peut-être que, on garde l'ordre normal.
