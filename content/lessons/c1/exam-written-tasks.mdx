By the end of this lesson you'll be able to plan and write a synthesis of two documents and an argued essay, and check your own text with a list before you hand it in.

## What you already know

You know how to build a synthesis by idea, with *confronter*, *un point commun*, *une divergence* and *s'accorder sur*, from <LessonLink id="c1-synthesis" text="the lesson on synthesis" />, and the dissertation's plans and problématique from <LessonLink id="c1-dissertation" text="the lesson on the dissertation" />. You know *quand bien même* from <LessonLink id="c1-concession-and-hypothesis" text="the lesson on concession and hypothesis" /> and connectors such as *dès lors* and *à cet égard* from <LessonLink id="c1-advanced-connectors" text="the lesson on advanced connectors" />. This practice lesson trains the two long written tasks, without any claim about an exam's rules.

## Two different tasks

<VocabTable group="task" />

**A synthesis** brings together the ideas of several documents in a new, shorter text. It is *objectif* (objective), a word you know from <LessonLink id="b2-summarizing-an-article" text="the lesson on summarizing an article" />: you give no personal opinion, you attribute every view to its source, and you organize the text by idea, not document by document. A good synthesis is *équilibré* (balanced): each document gets its fair place.

**An argued essay** answers a question with your own reasoning. Here you do take a position, but you show that you've considered the other side, usually with a concession (*certes…, mais…*, *quand bien même…*).

In both tasks, read the instructions twice before you start. The first rule is to *respecter la consigne* (follow the instructions): the subject, the length, the kind of text. A text that doesn't answer the question asked *est hors sujet* (is off topic). In a synthesis, the most serious mistake is *un contresens* (a misreading): making a document say the opposite of what it says.

<Example fr="Une synthèse doit rester objective : on ne donne pas son avis." en="A synthesis must stay objective: you don't give your opinion." />
<Example fr="Avant d'écrire, relisez la consigne pour ne pas être hors sujet." en="Before writing, reread the instructions so you're not off topic." />
<Example fr="Faire dire à un auteur le contraire de ce qu'il dit, c'est un contresens." en="Making an author say the opposite of what they say is a misreading." />

## Planning a synthesis

As a rule of thumb, work in four steps:

1. **Read each document** and note its main idea and its two or three arguments.
2. **Compare**: find the points the documents share and the points where they diverge.
3. **Build *un plan détaillé* (a detailed outline) by idea**: one part per question the documents answer, each mixing both documents.
4. **Write** an introduction that presents the documents and the shared question, the parts with linking sentences, and a short conclusion that sums up, still without your opinion.

<Example fr="Mon plan détaillé compte deux parties, organisées par idées." en="My detailed outline has two parts, organized by idea." />

## Planning an essay

For an essay, start from the question, turn it into a problématique, and choose a plan: dialectical (for, against, beyond), analytical or thematic, as you saw in the lesson on the dissertation. Place your concession where it helps your argument, and finish each part with *une phrase de transition* (a linking sentence) to the next.

<Example fr="Quand bien même les devoirs seraient supprimés, les inégalités resteraient." en="Even if homework were abolished, the inequalities would remain." />
<Example fr="Une phrase de transition annonce la partie suivante." en="A linking sentence announces the next part." />

## Two documents and a model synthesis

<VocabTable group="topic" />

The two documents and the synthesis below are original texts written for this lesson. The people are invented.

*Document 1 : le point de vue d'une enseignante*

*« Les devoirs à la maison permettent aux élèves de revoir seuls ce qu'ils ont appris en classe. Ils développent l'autonomie et l'habitude de travailler chaque jour. Sans devoirs, beaucoup d'élèves oublieraient vite la leçon du jour. Bien sûr, ils doivent être courts et adaptés à l'âge des élèves. » (Claire Durand, professeure de collège)*

*Document 2 : la position d'une association de parents d'élèves*

*« Tous les enfants n'ont pas les mêmes chances devant les devoirs. Certains sont aidés par leurs parents, d'autres travaillent seuls dans une maison où il n'y a pas de calme. Les parents qui travaillent tard ne peuvent pas toujours aider leurs enfants, même s'ils le veulent. Les devoirs augmentent donc les inégalités. Nous demandons qu'ils soient faits à l'école, avec un adulte, et adaptés à l'âge de chacun. »*

*Synthèse*

*Les deux documents s'interrogent sur la place des devoirs à la maison. Si l'enseignante y voit un moyen de développer l'autonomie des élèves et de ne pas oublier la leçon du jour, l'association de parents estime au contraire qu'ils augmentent les inégalités, puisque tous les enfants ne sont pas aidés de la même façon. Les deux sources s'accordent cependant sur un point : les devoirs doivent être adaptés à l'âge des élèves. Elles divergent en revanche sur le lieu : l'enseignante défend le travail à la maison, tandis que l'association demande qu'il soit fait à l'école, avec un adulte. Ainsi, les deux textes répondent à la même question, mais ils ne proposent pas la même solution.*

(Document 1: a teacher's point of view. "Homework allows pupils to go over on their own what they've learned in class. It develops independence and the habit of working every day. Without homework, many pupils would quickly forget the day's lesson. Of course, it must be short and suited to the pupils' age." (Claire Durand, collège teacher) Document 2: the position of a parents' association. "Not all children have the same chances when it comes to homework. Some are helped by their parents, others work alone in a home where there is no quiet. Parents who work late can't always help their children, even if they want to. Homework therefore increases inequalities. We ask that it be done at school, with an adult, and suited to each child's age." Synthesis. Both documents examine the place of homework. While the teacher sees it as a way to develop pupils' independence and not forget the day's lesson, the parents' association believes, on the contrary, that it increases inequalities, since not all children get the same help. The two sources agree, however, on one point: homework must be suited to the pupils' age. They differ, on the other hand, on the place: the teacher defends work at home, whereas the association asks for it to be done at school, with an adult. So the two texts answer the same question, but they don't propose the same solution.)

## How the synthesis works

- **By idea, not by document**: the first sentence states the shared question; then each sentence confronts the two sources.
- **Every view attributed**: *l'enseignante y voit…*, *l'association estime…*; no *je*, no *à mon avis*.
- **A point in common and a divergence**: *s'accordent sur un point* (the pupils' age), *divergent sur le lieu* (home or school).
- **Connectors**: a concessive *si*, *au contraire*, *puisque*, *cependant*, *en revanche*, *tandis que*.

## Checking your text

<VocabTable group="check" />

Keep *une grille de relecture* (a checklist for rereading) and use it on every text:

- **The task**: does the text answer the question asked, at the right length, in the right form?
- **The method**: in a synthesis, is every view attributed and is there no personal opinion? In an essay, is the plan announced and followed?
- **The links**: does each part end with a linking sentence, and are the connectors varied?
- **The language**: check *l'orthographe* (spelling), *la ponctuation* (punctuation), each *faute d'accord* (agreement mistake), especially past participles, and each *répétition* (repeated word).

<Example fr="Je relis mon texte trois fois : pour le sens, pour les accords, puis pour l'orthographe et la ponctuation." en="I reread my text three times: for the meaning, for the agreements, then for spelling and punctuation." />
<Example fr="Une faute d'accord sur un participe passé est une erreur qu'on fait souvent." en="An agreement mistake on a past participle is an error people often make." />
<Example fr="Pour éviter une répétition, on peut utiliser ces derniers ou un synonyme." en="To avoid a repetition, you can use ces derniers or a synonym." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Selon l'association, not à mon avis, in a synthesis">
  Learners often slip their own opinion into a synthesis. In a synthesis, every judgment belongs to a source: **selon l'association, les devoirs augmentent les inégalités**, not *à mon avis, les devoirs augmentent les inégalités*. Keep your opinion for the essay.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Reread for one thing at a time">
  Don't reread a text once for everything. Read it once for the meaning, once only for the agreements, once only for spelling and punctuation. On each reading, you'll see mistakes that you missed when you were looking for everything at once.
</Callout>
