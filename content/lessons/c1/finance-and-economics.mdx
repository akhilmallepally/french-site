By the end of this lesson you'll be able to follow economic news and discuss public finance, economic policy, savings and markets with precise vocabulary.

## What you already know

You know *la croissance*, *la récession*, *la conjoncture*, *le PIB*, *l'inflation* and *investir* from <LessonLink id="b2-economy-and-business" text="the lesson on the economy and business" />, and *un taux* and *pour cent* from <LessonLink id="b2-presenting-data" text="the lesson on presenting data" />. *Emprunter*, *un prêt* and *rembourser* come from <LessonLink id="b1-money-and-banking" text="the lesson on money and banking" />. This lesson moves from companies to the State and the markets.

A note on figures: the examples here describe mechanisms, not the real state of any economy. For real figures, check an official source on the day you need them.

## Public finance

<VocabTable group="public" />

*Le budget de l'État* sets what the State will spend, *les dépenses publiques*, and what it will receive, *les recettes*: mostly *les impôts*. *La fiscalité* is the tax system as a whole. When spending is higher than revenue, the budget shows *un déficit*. To cover it, the State borrows, and the total it owes is *la dette publique*. *S'endetter* is to get into debt, for a State, a company or a household.

<Example fr="Cette année, les dépenses publiques ont été plus élevées que les recettes." en="This year, public spending was higher than revenue." />
<Example fr="Pour financer son déficit, l'État s'endette." en="To fund its deficit, the State borrows." />
<Example fr="Le gouvernement veut réformer la fiscalité des petites entreprises." en="The government wants to reform taxation for small businesses." />

## Economic policy: relance or rigueur

<VocabTable group="policy" />

Economic debates often oppose two policies. *La relance* means spending more, or taxing less, to *relancer l'économie* (boost it). *La rigueur budgétaire* means controlling or cutting public spending to reduce the deficit. Each side has its arguments; the vocabulary itself is neutral, but the choice of word can show a point of view, so a careful writer names both.

*Un ménage*, in economics, is a household: the people who share a home and a budget. Economists talk about *la consommation des ménages* and *l'épargne des ménages*.

<Example fr="Le plan de relance doit aider les ménages et les entreprises." en="The stimulus plan is meant to help households and businesses." />
<Example fr="Certains économistes défendent la rigueur budgétaire, d'autres la relance." en="Some economists support budgetary restraint, others a stimulus." />

## Rates, savings and markets

<VocabTable group="markets" />

*Un taux d'intérêt* is the price of borrowing. *La banque centrale* can *relever* or *baisser ses taux*: when they go up, borrowing costs more; when they go down, it costs less. The rates themselves *montent* or *baissent*, but the bank *relève* them: *monter* isn't used with an object here.

*L'épargne* is the money people put aside, and *épargner* is to save. That money can go into *un placement* (an investment), which *rapporte* (yields) more or less, with more or less risk. *La Bourse* is the stock exchange, where *les actions* you know from B2 are bought and sold. *Une obligation*, in finance, is a bond: a loan to a State or a company that pays interest. Elsewhere, it keeps its everyday meaning of a duty.

<Example fr="La banque centrale a relevé ses taux pour faire baisser l'inflation." en="The central bank raised its rates to bring inflation down." />
<Example fr="Les ménages épargnent plus quand l'avenir est incertain." en="Households save more when the future is uncertain." />
<Example fr="Un placement sans risque rapporte en général moins." en="A risk-free investment usually yields less." />
<Example fr="Les marchés financiers ont réagi à la décision." en="The financial markets reacted to the decision." />

## Words that look familiar

Several words in this field are false friends, or have a second meaning that changes everything.

- **La Bourse**, with a capital, is the stock exchange; **une bourse** is a student grant.
- **Les recettes** of the State are its revenue; in a kitchen, *une recette* is a recipe.
- **Un placement** is an investment of savings, not a work placement, which is *un stage*.
- **Une obligation** is a bond in finance and a duty elsewhere.

<Example fr="Elle a obtenu une bourse pour ses études." en="She got a grant for her studies." />
<Example fr="Les recettes de l'État ont augmenté grâce à la croissance." en="The State's revenue rose thanks to growth." />

When a word seems odd in an economic article, check whether it has a financial meaning before you translate it.

## Reading economic news

Economic articles combine this vocabulary with the trend verbs you know from B2 (*augmenter*, *chuter*, *se stabiliser*) and with hedging. A careful reader notices who is speaking: a forecast is *une prévision*, not a fact, and journalists often use the conditionnel for it.

<Example fr="Selon le ministère, le déficit devrait baisser l'an prochain." en="According to the ministry, the deficit should fall next year." />

As with law, keep the words and the opinions apart. *La dette publique augmente* is a fact you can check; *la dette est trop élevée* is a judgement someone makes.

## En français

Lucas is reading an economic article. He asks his sister Emma, who works in a bank, to explain it.

- *Emma, ici ils disent que la banque centrale a relevé ses taux. Ça veut dire quoi ?* (Emma, here they say the central bank raised its rates. What does that mean? (spoken))
- *Que les taux d'intérêt montent : emprunter coûte plus cher.* (That interest rates are going up: borrowing costs more.)
- *Alors c'est le moment d'épargner, pas d'acheter un appartement ?* (So it's the time to save, not to buy a flat?)
- *Pour beaucoup de ménages, oui. L'épargne rapporte un peu plus.* (For many households, yes. Savings bring in a bit more.)
- *Et l'article parle aussi du déficit public.* (And the article also talks about the public deficit.)
- *C'est quand l'État dépense plus que ses recettes.* (That's when the State spends more than its revenue.)
- *Et pour le payer, il s'endette ?* (And to pay for it, it borrows?)
- *Exactement. C'est comme ça que la dette publique augmente.* (Exactly. That's how the public debt grows.)
- *Et une obligation, c'est quoi ? Un devoir ?* (And what's an obligation? A duty? (spoken))
- *Pas ici. En finance, c'est un prêt à l'État ou à une entreprise.* (Not here. In finance, it's a loan to the State or a company.)
- *Tu en as acheté, toi ?* (Have you bought any? (spoken))
- *Je ne parle pas de mes placements, même à mon frère !* (I don't talk about my investments, even to my brother!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une obligation: a bond in finance">
  Learners often read *une obligation* in an economic article as "an obligation". In finance, it's a bond: **acheter des obligations de l'État** means buying government bonds, not taking on duties.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Fact or judgement?">
  When you read an economic article, mark each sentence F (a fact you could check) or J (a judgement). *Le déficit a baissé* is F; *la rigueur est nécessaire* is J. It trains you to report economic debates neutrally, which the synthesis and the essay both require.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le budget de l'État fixe les dépenses publiques et les recettes, qui viennent surtout des impôts. La fiscalité est l'ensemble du système des impôts. Quand les dépenses sont plus élevées que les recettes, il y a un déficit public, et l'État s'endette. La dette publique est le total de ce que l'État doit. Pour aider l'économie, un gouvernement peut choisir la relance, c'est-à-dire dépenser plus, ou la rigueur budgétaire, c'est-à-dire réduire les dépenses. La banque centrale peut relever ou baisser ses taux d'intérêt. Les ménages épargnent et choisissent des placements, qui rapportent plus ou moins. À la Bourse, on achète des actions, et une obligation est un prêt à l'État ou à une entreprise. Il faut séparer les faits, comme une hausse de la dette, et les jugements.
