By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read and use the vocabulary of French law and institutions: how a text becomes law, which courts judge what, and the words of a court case.

## What you already know

You know *une loi*, *l'État* and *un citoyen* from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-french-republic" text="the lesson on the French Republic" />, and *le Parlement*, *un projet de loi* and *adopter une loi* from <LessonLink id="b2-politics-and-society" text="the lesson on politics and institutions" />. You also know *porter plainte* from <LessonLink id="b1-making-a-complaint" text="the lesson on making a complaint" />. This lesson adds the words you need to read official texts and news about the courts.

A note before you start: this lesson explains words, not anyone's rights. For a real legal question, ask a lawyer or an official advice service.

## From a bill to a text in force

<VocabTable group="texts" />

French law rests on a hierarchy of texts. At the top is *la Constitution*. Below it are *les lois*, voted by the Parliament. Below the laws are *les décrets*, adopted by the government, often to say how a law will be applied. Each level must respect the one above it.

When the Parliament has adopted a law, the President of the Republic *promulgue* it, and it's published in *le Journal officiel*. It then *entre en vigueur* (comes into force), sometimes on a date the text itself sets. A text can later be *abrogé* (repealed) by a new text.

<Example fr="La loi a été promulguée puis publiée au Journal officiel." en="The law was promulgated and then published in the official journal." />
<Example fr="Cette règle entrera en vigueur le 1er janvier." en="This rule will come into force on 1 January." />
<Example fr="Le gouvernement a abrogé l'ancien décret." en="The government repealed the old decree." />

*Une disposition* is one provision of a text, and *en vertu de* introduces the text that justifies a decision: *en vertu de cette disposition* (under this provision).

<Example fr="En vertu de cette loi, les entreprises sont tenues d'informer leurs clients." en="Under this law, companies are required to inform their customers." />

## Two orders of courts

<VocabTable group="courts" />

France has two separate orders of courts. *La justice* in the ordinary sense (disputes between people, companies, and criminal cases) starts in *un tribunal*, goes on appeal to *une cour d'appel*, and ends at *la Cour de cassation*. Disputes with public bodies (a town hall, a ministry, a public service) go to the administrative courts: *le tribunal administratif*, then the administrative court of appeal, and at the top *le Conseil d'État*.

*Le Conseil d'État* has two roles: it's the highest administrative court, and it advises the government on its draft laws and decrees. *Le Conseil constitutionnel* is not an ordinary court: it checks that laws respect the Constitution.

*Une juridiction* is the general word for any court. To take a case to a court is *saisir* it.

<Example fr="Elle travaille dans la justice depuis dix ans." en="She has worked in the courts for ten years." />
<Example fr="L'association a saisi le tribunal administratif." en="The association took the case to the administrative court." />
<Example fr="Le Conseil constitutionnel vérifie que les lois respectent la Constitution." en="The Conseil constitutionnel checks that laws respect the Constitution." />

## The words of a case

<VocabTable group="case" />

*Un procès* is a trial or a court case, where *un juge* or *une juge* decides and each side is helped by *un avocat* or *une avocate*. The decision is *un jugement*. A side that disagrees can *faire appel* (appeal). Against a decision of an administration, you take legal action: in formal writing, *former un recours*; *déposer un recours* is also standard, and everyday administrative French often says *faire un recours*.

<Example fr="Elle a perdu son procès, mais elle a fait appel du jugement." en="She lost her case, but she appealed against the ruling." />
<Example fr="Plusieurs habitants ont formé un recours contre ce projet." en="Several residents have taken legal action against this project." />

## Rights and duties

<VocabTable group="rights" />

*Un droit* is a right, and *le droit* is law as a subject: *elle fait des études de droit*. *Être tenu de* + infinitive is the formal way to say that the law requires something; the participle agrees with the subject. *Juridique* means "relating to law", like a legal question or a legal text.

<Example fr="Les propriétaires sont tenus de faire ces travaux." en="Owners are required to carry out this work." />
<Example fr="C'est une question juridique difficile." en="It's a difficult legal question." />

## Reading news about the law

News reports use this vocabulary in set patterns, often in the passive. Here are a few to recognize.

<Example fr="Le Conseil d'État a été saisi." en="The case has been taken to the Conseil d'État." />
<Example fr="Le jugement a été confirmé en appel." en="The ruling was upheld on appeal." />
<Example fr="Le texte entre en vigueur aujourd'hui, mais un recours a déjà été formé." en="The text comes into force today, but legal action has already been taken against it." />

Keep two things apart when you read: the word for a court tells you which order it belongs to, and the verb tells you what happened (*saisir*, *juger*, *faire appel*, *abroger*). Many articles mix them in one sentence.

## The whole list

| Step or body | French |
|---|---|
| top of the hierarchy | *la Constitution* |
| voted by the Parliament | *une loi* |
| adopted by the government | *un décret* |
| signed into law, published | *promulguer*, *le Journal officiel* |
| come into force, repeal | *entrer en vigueur*, *abroger* |
| ordinary courts | *un tribunal*, *une cour d'appel*, *la Cour de cassation* |
| administrative courts | *le tribunal administratif*, …, *le Conseil d'État* |
| constitutional check | *le Conseil constitutionnel* |
| a case | *un procès*, *un juge*, *un avocat*, *un jugement*, *faire appel*, *former un recours* |

## En français

Sarah tells her friend Lucas, a law student, about something she read in the paper.

- *Tu as lu le journal ? Un nouveau décret entre en vigueur lundi.* (Have you read the paper? A new decree comes into force on Monday.)
- *Sur quoi ?* (About what?)
- *Sur les horaires des magasins le dimanche. Une association veut former un recours.* (About shop opening hours on Sundays. An association wants to take legal action.)
- *Contre un décret, c'est en général le Conseil d'État qui juge.* (Against a decree, it's generally the Conseil d'État that rules.)
- *Pas le tribunal administratif ?* (Not the administrative court?)
- *Non, pour un décret, on saisit le plus souvent tout de suite le Conseil d'État.* (No, for a decree, you usually go straight to the Conseil d'État. (spoken))
- *Et si l'association gagne ?* (And if the association wins?)
- *Le Conseil d'État peut annuler le décret. Mais le gouvernement peut aussi l'abroger lui-même.* (The Conseil d'État can cancel the decree. But the government can also repeal it itself.)
- *Tu veux devenir avocat ?* (Do you want to become a lawyer?)
- *Peut-être, ou juge. Mais attention, je ne donne pas de conseils juridiques !* (Maybe, or a judge. But careful, I don't give legal advice!)
- *Rassure-toi, je n'ai pas de procès en cours.* (Don't worry, I don't have a court case going on.)
- *Tant mieux. Un procès, ça peut durer longtemps.* (Just as well. A court case can last a long time. (spoken))

<Callout type="warning" title="Former un recours in formal writing">
  Learners often use *faire* with every legal noun, and sometimes drop the article. In formal writing, choose the established collocation: **former un recours** (against a decision; everyday French also says *faire un recours*), **faire appel** (against a ruling), **porter plainte** (with the police or a court).
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Name the order of the court">
  When you read about a case, decide first whether the court is ordinary (*tribunal*, *cour d'appel*, *Cour de cassation*) or administrative (*tribunal administratif*, *Conseil d'État*). It tells you who the other side is: a person or company, or a public body.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le droit français repose sur une hiérarchie de textes. La Constitution est au-dessus de tout, puis viennent les lois, votées par le Parlement, et les décrets, adoptés par le gouvernement. Le président de la République promulgue la loi, qui est publiée au Journal officiel, puis elle entre en vigueur. Un nouveau texte peut l'abroger. En France, il existe deux ordres de juridictions. Les tribunaux, les cours d'appel et la Cour de cassation jugent les conflits entre personnes ou entreprises. Le tribunal administratif et le Conseil d'État jugent les conflits avec une mairie, un ministère ou un service public. Le Conseil constitutionnel vérifie que les lois respectent la Constitution. Dans un procès, un juge décide et les avocats défendent chaque partie. Contre un jugement, on fait appel, et contre une décision publique, on forme un recours.
