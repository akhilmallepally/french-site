By the end of this lesson you'll be able to describe the main French traditions of satire and humour, recognize the kinds of wordplay French loves, and explain why French humour is hard to translate.

In <LessonLink id="c1-irony-and-understatement" text="the lesson on irony and understatement" />, you learned to read irony. This lesson places it in a long tradition.

## A long tradition

<VocabTable group="tradition" />

France has a long tradition of *la satire*: humour that *tourne en ridicule* the powerful, the pretentious and the hypocritical. *Le rire* is seen as a way to criticize without giving a lecture.

**Les fables.** Jean de La Fontaine (1621–1695) published his *Fables* from 1668. In short stories in verse, animals behave like people, and each fable ends with *une morale*. In *Le Loup et l'Agneau*, a wolf finds a pretext to eat a lamb; the moral is famous.

<Example fr="« La raison du plus fort est toujours la meilleure. » (La Fontaine)" en="\"The argument of the strongest is always the best.\" (our translation)" />

Behind the animals, readers recognize the court and the powerful of the time.

**La comédie.** Molière (1622–1673) wrote comedies that laugh at *l'hypocrisie*, false learning and social pretensions: the religious hypocrite in *Tartuffe*, the doctors who don't cure anybody, the snobs who imitate the nobility.

<Example fr="Molière tourne en ridicule l'hypocrisie de son temps." en="Molière holds up the hypocrisy of his time to ridicule." />

**Le conte philosophique.** In the eighteenth century, Voltaire used the *conte philosophique* to mock war, fanaticism and blind optimism. *Candide* (1759) is the best-known example: a naive young man discovers that the world is far from "the best of all possible worlds".

## Satire today

<VocabTable group="modern" />

**La presse satirique.** You met it at B2. *Le Canard enchaîné*, a satirical weekly founded in 1915, combines investigation and humour. Satirical papers rely on *le dessin de presse*, where a single cartoon comments on the news.

**Le sketch et le stand-up.** Comedians write *sketches*, short comic scenes, for the stage, the radio and television. Since the early 2000s, *le stand-up*, imported from English-speaking countries, has become very popular, especially among young audiences. Many comedians use *l'autodérision*: they laugh at themselves before they laugh at others.

<Example fr="Cet humoriste pratique beaucoup l'autodérision." en="This comedian uses a lot of self-mockery." />

As at B2, this lesson describes the tradition; it doesn't judge any particular paper or artist.

## Playing with words

<VocabTable group="words" />

French humour loves *les jeux de mots*. *Un calembour* plays on two meanings of the same sound: many French words sound alike, which makes puns easy. *Une contrepèterie* swaps sounds between two words to create a second meaning, often cheeky; the reader has to find it. *Un sous-entendu* suggests something without saying it.

<Example fr="Ce calembour repose sur deux mots qui se prononcent de la même façon." en="This pun relies on two words that are pronounced the same way." />
<Example fr="Le sous-entendu est clair, même si personne ne le dit." en="The implication is clear, even though nobody says it." />

## Why humour is hard to translate

Puns and spoonerisms depend on the sounds of one language: they're often *intraduisibles*. Satire depends on references a foreigner may not share: a politician's habits, an advertisement, a regional accent. Irony depends on tone. When you watch French comedy, expect to miss some jokes at first; it's a sign of how much of humour is culture, not language.

<Example fr="Ce jeu de mots est intraduisible : il n'existe qu'en français." en="This play on words is untranslatable: it only exists in French." />

## Fables in everyday French

La Fontaine's morals are still part of everyday French. People quote them, or half-quote them, without always knowing where they come from. *La raison du plus fort* is used to criticize any abuse of power; other fables gave French its images of the clever fox and the proud crow.

<Example fr="C'est encore la raison du plus fort : la grande entreprise gagne toujours." en="It's the law of the strongest again: the big company always wins." />

Knowing the best-known fables helps you catch these references in the news and in conversation, where a single word can call up a whole story.

## Humour and register

French humour moves easily between registers. A satirical paper can use very formal French to describe something absurd, and the contrast itself is funny. A sketch can put the language of an official letter into the mouth of a child. When you read satire, ask which register the writer is imitating, and why: the joke is often there.

<Example fr="Le journal décrit une simple panne de café avec le ton d'une crise d'État." en="The paper describes a simple coffee-machine breakdown in the tone of a national crisis." />

As a learner, enjoy satire as a reader, and be careful as a writer: satire that targets a group of people, rather than the powerful, quickly stops being funny for the people concerned.

## A short article

The article below is an original text written for this lesson.

*Rire du pouvoir : une tradition française*

*En France, on aime rire des puissants. Cette tradition est vieille. Il y a plus de trois cents ans, La Fontaine faisait parler des animaux : derrière le loup, le lion ou le renard, ses lecteurs reconnaissaient la cour et ses mœurs. On lit encore ses fables à l'école aujourd'hui. À la même époque, Molière tournait en ridicule l'hypocrisie et les médecins de son temps, et le roi lui-même assistait à ses comédies. Le public s'amusait, mais il réfléchissait aussi.*

*Au siècle suivant, Voltaire choisit le conte philosophique. Sous une forme légère, il critique la guerre, le fanatisme et ceux qui pensent que tout va pour le mieux.*

*Aujourd'hui, la tradition continue sous d'autres formes. La presse satirique commente l'actualité avec des dessins et des jeux de mots. Chaque semaine, les lecteurs retrouvent ainsi des personnages qu'ils connaissent bien, toujours un peu ridicules. À la radio et à la télévision, les humoristes imitent les responsables politiques et se moquent des petites habitudes de chacun. Sur scène, le stand-up a trouvé un jeune public, qui apprécie l'autodérision autant que la critique.*

*Ce rire a ses règles. Il vise plutôt les puissants que les faibles, et il suppose un public complice, qui comprend les sous-entendus. C'est pourquoi l'humour voyage mal : un calembour ou une contrepèterie reste souvent intraduisible, et une allusion à l'actualité peut tomber à plat dans un autre pays.*

*Rire du pouvoir n'est pas toujours facile. Mais en France, on pense souvent que la liberté de rire fait partie de la liberté d'expression.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Laughing at power: a French tradition. In France, people like to laugh at the powerful. This tradition is old. More than three hundred years ago, La Fontaine made animals speak: behind the wolf, the lion or the fox, his readers recognized the court and its customs. His fables are still read at school today. At the same time, Molière made fun of hypocrisy and the doctors of his time, and the king himself attended his comedies. The audience enjoyed itself, but it also thought. In the following century, Voltaire chose the philosophical tale. In a light form, he criticizes war, fanaticism and those who think that all is for the best. Today, the tradition continues in other forms. The satirical press comments on the news with cartoons and wordplay. Every week, readers find characters they know well, always a little ridiculous. On radio and television, comedians imitate politicians and make fun of everyone's little habits. On stage, stand-up has found a young audience, which enjoys self-mockery as much as criticism. This laughter has its rules. It targets the powerful rather than the weak, and it assumes an audience in on the joke, who understands the innuendo. That's why humour travels badly: a pun or a spoonerism often remains untranslatable, and a reference to the news can fall flat in another country. Laughing at power isn't always easy. But in France, people often think that the freedom to laugh is part of freedom of expression.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une morale, not un moral">
  Learners often confuse the two nouns. The lesson at the end of a fable is feminine: **la morale de la fable**. *Le moral* (masculine) is your mood: *avoir le moral*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with La Fontaine">
  If you want to understand French humour from its roots, read two or three of La Fontaine's *Fables* in a modern edition with notes. They're short, they're quoted constantly in everyday French, and each one shows how a story can criticize without naming anyone.
</Callout>
