## Three registers, one message

A proverb, an idiom, a slang word and a formal formula are all correct French, but each one has its place. As a rule of thumb, write the three versions in this order:

1. **Start in the middle**, with the everyday standard version. It holds all the facts: what, when, where, who.
2. **Go down** for a friend: cut the formulas, shorten the sentences, add one or two familiar words, and keep only the facts your friend needs.
3. **Go up** for an institution: replace every familiar word, use the precise nouns, add the request formula and the full closing formula, and check that every fact matches the standard version.

If a fact changes in one version, change it in the other two.

## Where proverbs and idioms fit

Not every register welcomes a proverb or an idiom. As a rule of thumb:

- **In a text to a friend**: idioms are natural, often in their most familiar form (*faut mettre la main à la pâte*, with the *il* dropped, as people say it).
- **In a message to neighbours or colleagues**: one proverb, introduced by *comme dit le proverbe*, works well as a friendly conclusion.
- **In an administrative letter**: leave them out. A letter to an institution states facts and makes a request; a proverb there sounds like a joke.

Use one at a time: two proverbs in a short message sound like a lesson.

## Regional words across registers

Regional words don't belong to a register. For a writer from a Swiss canton where *huitante* is used, *huitante* and *le souper* are the normal words, in a text to a friend as much as in a letter to the town council. That's why, in the model below, they appear in all three versions.

*Ça craint* is familiar everywhere; *huitante* is standard in some Swiss cantons and not used in others. So the question to ask isn't "is this word formal?" but "will my reader know it?". As the lesson on regional languages advised, recognize these words, and, as a learner writing to a reader in France, keep to *quatre-vingts* and *le dîner*. For a Swiss reader, the regional words are the right choice.

## A model message

These messages are original texts written for this lesson. The town, the association and the people are invented. The writer, Léa, lives in a Swiss town where *huitante* is used.

*1. Texto à une amie*

*Salut Chloé ! Le souper du quartier, c'est mort pour samedi : il va pleuvoir toute la journée. On le fait samedi prochain, même heure. On est déjà huitante, t'imagines ? Du coup, faut que tout le monde mette la main à la pâte pour les tables. Tu viens nous aider ? Bises !*

*2. Message aux voisins*

*Chers voisins, à cause de la pluie annoncée pour samedi, le souper du quartier est reporté au samedi suivant, à la même heure, sur la place du Marché. Huitante personnes ont déjà répondu oui : merci à tous ! Nous cherchons encore quelques personnes pour installer les tables à partir de dix-sept heures. Si vous pouvez aider, répondez à ce message. Comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps. À samedi prochain ! Léa Favre, pour l'association de quartier.*

*3. Lettre à la commune*

*Léa Favre, pour l'association de quartier, 5 chemin du Lac, Montvert*

*Administration communale de Montvert*

*Montvert, le 2 juin*

*Objet : demande d'autorisation pour notre souper de quartier*

*Madame, Monsieur,*

*En raison des fortes pluies annoncées pour samedi, l'association de quartier que je représente a décidé de reporter son souper au samedi suivant. Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir nous autoriser à organiser notre souper sur la place du Marché ce jour-là, aux mêmes horaires, de dix-huit heures à vingt-trois heures. Huitante habitants ont déjà annoncé qu'ils seraient présents. Nous installerons les tables nous-mêmes à partir de dix-sept heures, et nous nous engageons à libérer et à nettoyer la place à la fin de la soirée. Je reste à votre disposition pour toute information.*

*Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*

*Léa Favre, présidente de l'association de quartier*

## How the message uses the unit

- **Layout**: only the letter has the full layout of <LessonLink id="c1-formal-correspondence" text="a formal letter" />: sender, recipient, place and date, *objet*, salutation and closing formula.
- **Registers**: *c'est mort*, *t'imagines*, *du coup* and *faut que* with the *il* dropped (1); the neutral passive *est reporté* and *chers voisins* (2); *en raison de*, *je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir*, *nous nous engageons à* and the full closing formula (3).
- **One proverb and one idiom**: *comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps* in the message to the neighbours, where it is both literal and figurative; *mettre la main à la pâte* in the text to a friend. The letter has neither.
- **Two regional words**, labelled: *huitante* and *le souper* appear in all three versions, because they are Léa's normal words.
- **The same facts**: the supper moves to the following Saturday, at the same time, on the same square, with eighty people already coming; the tables go up from five o'clock.

## Your turn

Choose one small event: a postponed party, a cancelled class, a change of address. Write it three times, in about sixty words each: a text to a friend, a message to neighbours or colleagues, and a letter to an institution. Use one proverb and one idiom, each in a version where it fits, and keep both out of the letter. If you use a regional word, write for a reader from that region, and label it.

## Check your work

- Do the three versions give the same facts, with no detail changed between them?
- Does each version keep one register from start to finish, with no familiar word in the letter and no formal formula in the text?
- Is the proverb introduced naturally, and is it in the right version?
- Does the letter have the full layout, from the sender to the closing formula?
- Is every regional word labelled, and would your reader know it?

<Callout type="warning" title="No proverb in an administrative letter">
  Learners who have just learned a proverb often want to use it everywhere. In a letter to an institution, it sounds like a joke. Write **en raison des fortes pluies annoncées**, not *comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a list of local words">
  When you watch a film or read a newspaper from Belgium, Switzerland, Québec or Africa, note each word you don't know from France in a separate list, with the country beside it. Over a few months, that list will help you understand speakers from many places, not only from Paris.
</Callout>
