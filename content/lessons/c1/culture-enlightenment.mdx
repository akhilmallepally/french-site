## The century of light

<VocabTable group="age" />

*Les Lumières* (the Enlightenment) is the name given to a movement of ideas in eighteenth-century Europe. The image is light against darkness: the light of reason against *les préjugés* (prejudices), superstition and blind obedience. In France, its writers called themselves *philosophes*, but they weren't only academic philosophers: they wrote novels, plays, tales, letters, dictionaries and pamphlets.

Their shared ideas, as a rule of thumb: reason should judge every claim, whoever makes it; *la tolérance* (tolerance) should replace religious persecution; knowledge should be shared; and *le progrès* (progress) of science and education can make people happier. Many of their books faced *la censure* (censorship) from the Church or the royal authorities.

<Example fr="Les philosophes des Lumières voulaient remplacer les préjugés par la raison." en="The Enlightenment philosophers wanted to replace prejudice with reason." />
<Example fr="Au dix-huitième siècle, beaucoup croyaient au progrès des sciences." en="In the eighteenth century, many believed in the progress of science." />

## Four writers and a dictionary

<VocabTable group="thinkers" />

**Montesquieu** (1689–1755) first mocked French society through the eyes of two Persian visitors in his *Lettres persanes* (1721). In *De l'esprit des lois* (1748), he compared political systems and argued for *la séparation des pouvoirs*: the power to make laws (*le pouvoir législatif*), to apply them (*le pouvoir exécutif*) and to judge (*le pouvoir judiciaire*) should be in different hands, so that no one can abuse them.

<Example fr="Montesquieu défend la séparation des pouvoirs." en="Montesquieu argues for the separation of powers." />
<Example fr="« […] il faut que, par la disposition des choses, le pouvoir arrête le pouvoir. » (Montesquieu, De l'esprit des lois)" en="&quot;[…] things must be arranged so that power checks power.&quot; (our translation)" />

**Voltaire** (1694–1778) fought for tolerance and freedom of thought with humour and energy. When Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant from Toulouse, was executed in 1762 after an unfair trial, Voltaire campaigned for years to have him cleared, and wrote his *Traité sur la tolérance* (1763). His tale *Candide* (1759) ends with a modest piece of advice:

<Example fr="« Il faut cultiver notre jardin. » (Voltaire, Candide)" en="&quot;We must cultivate our garden.&quot; (our translation)" />

A famous sentence about defending to the death someone's right to speak is often attributed to Voltaire, but it isn't his: it was written by an English biographer at the start of the twentieth century to sum up his attitude. Check a quotation before you use it.

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** (1712–1778), born in Geneva, argued in *Du contrat social* (1762) for *la souveraineté du peuple* (the sovereignty of the people): legitimate laws express *la volonté générale* (the general will), not the will of a king. The same year, *Émile* set out his ideas on education.

<Example fr="« L'homme est né libre, et partout il est dans les fers. » (Rousseau, Du contrat social)" en="&quot;Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains.&quot; (our translation)" />

**Denis Diderot** (1713–1784) directed, with the mathematician d'Alembert, *l'Encyclopédie* (published from 1751 to 1772). This huge work wanted to *rassembler les savoirs* (bring knowledge together), from philosophy to the techniques of crafts and trades, and it was banned more than once.

<Example fr="L'Encyclopédie voulait rassembler les savoirs de son temps." en="The Encyclopédie wanted to bring together the knowledge of its time." />

Women played a real part too. **Émilie du Châtelet** (1706–1749), a scientist, translated Newton's major work into French. In Paris, women such as Madame Geoffrin held *salons*, regular gatherings where writers, scientists and artists met and discussed new ideas.

<Example fr="Dans les salons, on parlait de science, de politique et de religion." en="In the salons, people talked about science, politics and religion." />
<Example fr="Pour Rousseau, la loi doit exprimer la volonté générale." en="For Rousseau, the law must express the general will." />

## From ideas to the Revolution

<VocabTable group="legacy" />

In August 1789, a few weeks after the storming of the Bastille, the revolutionaries adopted *la Déclaration des droits de l'homme et du citoyen*. Its text echoes Enlightenment ideas: freedom, equality before the law, *la liberté d'expression* (freedom of expression), the separation of powers and the sovereignty of the nation.

<Example fr="Les députés ont rédigé la Déclaration en quelques semaines." en="The deputies drafted the Declaration in a few weeks." />
<Example fr="La Déclaration des droits de l'homme et du citoyen défend la liberté d'expression." en="The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen defends freedom of expression." />

How far the Enlightenment caused the Revolution is a historians' debate. For some, the writers of the Enlightenment prepared the ground by teaching people to question authority. For others, the causes were first of all economic and political, such as the state's debts and the cost of bread, and the ideas served to express a crisis that had other roots.

The legacy is debated too. For many, the Enlightenment is the source of human rights, democracy and secular institutions. Others point out its limits: many of its writers didn't extend the equality they defended to women. And although Montesquieu (in book XV of *De l'esprit des lois*), Voltaire (in *Candide*) and the *Encyclopédie* attacked *l'esclavage* (slavery), this criticism rarely became a campaign for abolition before the 1780s. Present both views, and attribute them.

<Example fr="Pour certains spécialistes, les Lumières ont des limites : beaucoup d'auteurs ont critiqué l'esclavage, mais peu ont demandé sa fin avant les années 1780." en="For some specialists, the Enlightenment has limits: many writers criticized slavery, but few called for its end before the 1780s." />

## A short article

The article below is an original text written for this lesson.

*Comment les idées des Lumières ont voyagé*

*Au dix-huitième siècle, un livre qui critiquait le roi ou l'Église pouvait être censuré. Beaucoup d'auteurs publiaient donc sans leur nom, ou faisaient imprimer leurs œuvres à l'étranger, en Hollande ou à Genève, avant de les faire entrer en France en secret.*

*Les idées passaient aussi par la parole. À Paris, des femmes invitaient chez elles, dans leur salon, des écrivains, des savants et des artistes. On y lisait des textes nouveaux, on y parlait de science, de politique et de religion. Dans les cafés, on lisait les journaux et on commentait les nouvelles.*

*Les lettres jouaient aussi un grand rôle, et Voltaire écrivait sans cesse à des amis dans toute l'Europe. Une idée lancée à Paris pouvait ainsi arriver à Berlin ou à Saint-Pétersbourg quelques semaines plus tard. Écrire pouvait pourtant coûter cher : Voltaire a passé du temps à la Bastille quand il était jeune, et Diderot a passé quelques mois en prison à Vincennes.*

*Enfin, l'Encyclopédie de Diderot et d'Alembert voulait rassembler les savoirs de son temps, des sciences aux métiers. Elle a été interdite plusieurs fois, mais sa publication a continué.*

*Les spécialistes ne sont pas tous d'accord sur le rôle de ces idées dans la Révolution. Pour certains, les Lumières l'ont préparée ; pour d'autres, ses causes sont d'abord économiques et politiques. Beaucoup pensent cependant que ce siècle a changé la façon de débattre : une idée devait désormais être jugée par la raison, et non par l'autorité de la personne qui la défend.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(How the ideas of the Enlightenment travelled. In the eighteenth century, a book that criticized the king or the Church could be censored. Many authors therefore published without their name, or had their works printed abroad, in Holland or in Geneva, before bringing them into France in secret. Ideas also travelled by word of mouth. In Paris, women invited writers, scientists and artists to their homes, to their salon. People read new texts there and talked about science, politics and religion. In the cafés, people read the newspapers and commented on the news. Letters also played a large part, and Voltaire wrote constantly to friends all over Europe. An idea launched in Paris could thus reach Berlin or Saint Petersburg a few weeks later. Writing could still cost a writer dearly, though: Voltaire spent time in the Bastille when he was young, and Diderot spent a few months in prison at Vincennes. Finally, Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie wanted to bring together the knowledge of its time, from the sciences to the trades. It was banned several times, but its publication went on. Specialists don't all agree on the role of these ideas in the Revolution. For some, the Enlightenment prepared it; for others, its causes were first of all economic and political. Many think, however, that this century changed the way people debate: from then on, an idea had to be judged by reason, and not by the authority of the person defending it.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Le siècle des Lumières, not la Lumière">
  Learners often write the name in the singular. The movement is always plural, with a capital: **les Lumières**, **le siècle des Lumières**. *La lumière*, in the singular, is just light.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Check the source of a quotation">
  Before you quote a famous thinker, look for the work and, if you can, the chapter. Many sentences that circulate online are wrongly attributed, as the "Voltaire" sentence above shows. A quotation with its source is also more convincing in an essay.
</Callout>
