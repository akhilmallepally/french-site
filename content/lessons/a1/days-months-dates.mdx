By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name the days and months, say what the date is, and tell someone when your birthday is.

## The days of the week

The French week starts on Monday, and that's how calendars are printed in France.

<VocabTable group="days" />

All the days are masculine, and all of them take a **small letter**, unless they start a sentence. You can use them with no article at all to mean one particular day, as English does. Add **le** and the day becomes a habit:

- *Lundi* means this coming or most recent Monday.
- *Le lundi* means on Mondays, every Monday.

<Example fr="Le marché, c'est le mercredi." en="The market is on Wednesdays." />
<Example fr="Aujourd'hui, c'est vendredi." en="Today is Friday." />

## The months

Months take small letters as well, and many of them look a lot like the English words, so spelling is the main thing to watch. *Février* and *décembre* have an acute accent, and *août* has a circumflex.

<VocabTable group="months" />

To say "in" a month, use **en**: *en janvier*, *en juin*, *en octobre*. You don't need an article.

<Example fr="Noël, c'est en décembre." en="Christmas is in December." />

## Giving the date

A French date is built in a fixed order: **le**, then the number, then the month. You're already able to say all the numbers, thanks to <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-21-100" text="the lesson on numbers 21 to 100" />.

The words below will help. French gives you a few fixed phrases for this, and you can learn them as they stand.

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

- *le 14 juillet* (the 14th of July)
- *le 25 décembre* (the 25th of December)
- *le 31 octobre* (the 31st of October)

English uses "first", "second", "third" and so on. French uses plain numbers, with one exception: the first of the month is **le premier**, often written *le 1er*.

<Example fr="On est le premier avril." en="It's the first of April." />
<Example fr="Nous sommes le 22 septembre." en="It's the 22nd of September." />

If you include the day of the week, it goes first: *lundi 3 mai*, or *le lundi 3 mai* on more formal occasions.

*Le 14 juillet* is one date every French learner meets early, because it's the French national holiday. People simply call it *le 14 juillet*, much as Americans say "the Fourth of July".

## Birthdays

Birthdays use these four phrases. Learn the whole sentences as fixed phrases for now. *Quand* (when) gets its proper lesson in <LessonLink id="a1-question-words" text="the lesson on question words" />.

<VocabTable group="birthdays" />

<Example fr="C'est quand, ton anniversaire ? — C'est le 8 novembre." en="When's your birthday? — It's the 8th of November." />

## Putting it together

Here's how the words work in a short exchange at the start of a class:

- *On est quel jour, aujourd'hui ?* (What day is it today? A fixed phrase for now.)
- *On est jeudi.* (It's Thursday.)
- *Et la date ?* (And the date?)
- *On est le 30 janvier.* (It's the 30th of January.)
- *Ah ! C'est mon anniversaire !* (Oh! It's my birthday!)
- *Bon anniversaire !* (Happy birthday!)

<Callout type="warning" title="The day comes first on paper">
  French writes dates day first, so **04/07** is the 4th of July, and not April 7th. If you're used to month-first dates, check twice when you book a train or fill in a form. On a form, you'll also often see your date of birth asked for in this order.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The week is full of planets">
  Most French days are named after the sky. *Lundi* comes from the Moon (*la lune*), *mardi* from Mars, *mercredi* from Mercury, *jeudi* from Jupiter and *vendredi* from Venus. If you can picture the planets in that order, the first five days fall into place. *Samedi* and *dimanche* have other origins, but they're the two you'll be looking forward to.
</Callout>
