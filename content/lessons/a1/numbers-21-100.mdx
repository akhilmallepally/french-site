By the end of this lesson you'll be able to count from 21 to 100, say the numbers French builds in an unusual way, and recognize the words used for them in Belgium and Switzerland.

## The tens

You already know everything up to twenty from <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-0-20" text="the first numbers lesson" />. From here, you only need a few new words.

<VocabTable group="tens" />

## 21 to 69: a simple pattern

From 21 to 69, put the ten first and then the unit, joined with a hyphen, just as English does: *vingt-deux*, *quarante-sept*, *soixante-huit*.

There's one exception: numbers ending in **1** use **et** (and) with no hyphens: *vingt et un*, *trente et un*, *cinquante et un*, *soixante et un*.

<VocabTable group="joining" />

In these numbers you can hear the t of *vingt*: *vingt-deux*, *vingt et un*.

<Example fr="J'ai vingt-trois ans." en="I'm twenty-three." />
<Example fr="Elle a quarante-deux ans." en="She's forty-two." />

## 70 to 79: sixty and ten

Standard French doesn't have its own word for 70. It says **sixty-ten**, and carries on counting: sixty-eleven, sixty-twelve, up to sixty-nineteen.

<VocabTable group="seventies" />

So after *soixante-neuf* comes *soixante-dix*. The pattern carries on with the numbers from 11 to 19 that you already know: *soixante et onze*, *soixante-douze*, *soixante-treize*, *soixante-quatorze*, *soixante-quinze*, *soixante-seize*, *soixante-dix-sept*, *soixante-dix-huit*, *soixante-dix-neuf*.

Notice that 71 still uses *et*.

<Example fr="Il y a soixante-dix personnes." en="There are seventy people." />
<Example fr="C'est soixante-quinze euros." en="That's seventy-five euros." />

## 80 to 99: four twenties

Now French goes one step further. 80 is **four twenties**, and the 90s are four-twenties-ten and onwards.

<VocabTable group="eighties" />

Three things to look out for:

- **Quatre-vingts** takes an -s when it's exactly 80. Once another number follows, the -s is dropped: *quatre-vingt-trois*.
- The t of *vingt* is silent throughout the 80s and 90s, unlike in *vingt-deux*.
- **There's no *et*** in 81 or 91: *quatre-vingt-un*, *quatre-vingt-onze*.

Then come the last numbers before you reach 100: *quatre-vingt-dix-huit*, *quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*, *cent*. The 90s use the numbers from 11 to 19 again.

<Example fr="Madame Roche a quatre-vingt-un ans." en="Mrs Roche is eighty-one." />

French phone numbers, which are read in pairs, are good practice for all these. 06 37 72 81 99 is *zéro six, trente-sept, soixante-douze, quatre-vingt-un, quatre-vingt-dix-neuf*.

## Belgium and Switzerland

French speakers in Belgium and Switzerland mostly use simpler words for two of these numbers.

<VocabTable group="regional" />

For 80, Belgium uses *quatre-vingts*, like France. In some parts of Switzerland you may also come across *huitante*. If you're learning for France, you only need to recognize these words.

## Putting it together

To read any number up to 100, split it into the part you know and the part that's added: 67 is *soixante* and *sept*, 74 is *soixante* and *quatorze*, and 96 is *quatre-vingt* and *seize*.

<Callout type="warning" title="Et stops at 71">
  Learners tend to carry the *et* pattern all the way up. It applies only to 21, 31, 41, 51, 61 and 71. It's **quatre-vingt-un** and **quatre-vingt-onze**, with hyphens instead. And keep the -s for exactly 80: **quatre-vingts**, but **quatre-vingt-cinq**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Don't write the first digit too soon">
  When you're taking down a number by ear, wait until the end. *Soixante…* might turn out to be 60-something or 70-something: it depends on what comes next. *Quatre-vingt…* could be in the 80s or the 90s. Write down the whole number once you've heard all of it, and do the sum then.
</Callout>
