By the end of this lesson you'll be able to give instructions, tell people what to do and what not to do, and suggest doing something together.

## Three forms, no subject

The **imperative** is the form you use for commands, instructions and suggestions. You've already used it in <LessonLink id="a1-asking-directions" text="the lesson on asking for directions" />: *Tournez à droite*, *Allez tout droit*.

It has only three forms, one each for *tu*, *nous* and *vous*. They come from the present tense, but you leave out the subject.

- **Tu** form: telling one person you call *tu*.
- **Vous** form: telling one person you call *vous*, or more than one person.
- **Nous** form: suggesting something, meaning "let's".

## Regular verbs

There's one change to watch for. **-er verbs drop the final -s of the *tu* form**: *tu parles* becomes *Parle !* The -ir and -re verbs keep theirs: *Finis !*, *Attends !*

<Example fr="Regardez !" en="Look!" />
<Example fr="Choisis un livre." en="Choose a book." />
<Example fr="Attendons Paul." en="Let's wait for Paul." />

## Aller and faire

These two use forms you've met in <LessonLink id="a1-faire-and-aller" text="the lesson on faire and aller" />. *Aller* behaves like an -er verb, so *tu vas* becomes **Va !**, with no -s. *Faire* keeps *fais*, and the *vous* form is *faites*, as it always is.

## Saying don't

To tell someone not to do something, put *ne…pas* around the verb, just as you do in a statement.

<Example fr="Ne tourne pas ici !" en="Don't turn here!" />
<Example fr="N'allez pas à la gare." en="Don't go to the station." />
<Example fr="Ne faisons pas les courses." en="Let's not do the food shopping." />

## Being polite

A command on its own can sound abrupt. Add **s'il te plaît** with someone you call *tu*, or **s'il vous plaît** with someone you call *vous*.

- *Attends, s'il te plaît.* (Wait, please.)
- *Tournez à droite, s'il vous plaît.* (Turn right, please.)

Now you can see why directions sound the way they do. A passer-by says *Prenez la première rue à gauche* with the *vous* form, because you're a stranger. A friend would use the *tu* form instead.

## Putting it together

Here's someone giving a friend a lift, with instructions from the passenger seat.

- *Va tout droit, et tourne à gauche.* (Go straight on, and turn left.)
- *Ici ?* (Here?)
- *Non, attends ! Ne tourne pas ici. Va au feu.* (No, wait! Don't turn here. Go to the lights.)
- *Allons au marché.* (Let's go to the market.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep the -s off -er commands">
  The *tu* form of an -er verb ends in -es in a statement, so it's easy to carry that over: *Parles !* or *Vas à la gare !* In a command, the -s disappears: **Parle !**, **Va à la gare !** -er verbs and *aller* do this. A few -ir verbs like *ouvrir* do too, but not the ones in this course. It's still **Finis !** and **Attends !**
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Why the door says Pousser">
  On signs, doors and recipes, French often skips the imperative and uses the plain infinitive. *Pousser* on a door means "push", and *Tirer* means "pull". You'll also see *Ne pas toucher* ("Do not touch"). You don't have to produce this form yet, but once you know it, French doors will be a lot less confusing.
</Callout>
