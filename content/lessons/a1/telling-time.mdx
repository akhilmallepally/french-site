By the end of this lesson you'll be able to ask what time it is, tell the time in everyday French, understand the 24-hour clock, and say what time something happens.

## Asking and answering

For telling the time, French uses **il est**, and here *il* doesn't refer to anyone. It works just like "it" in "it's five o'clock". The word **heure** (hour) is feminine, and it takes a plural -s from two o'clock onward. You'll use the numbers from <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-21-100" text="the lesson on numbers 21 to 100" />, along with those you learned first.

<VocabTable group="basics" />

You must say the word *heures*. In English you can stop at "It's five", but French can't. The only times without it are **midi** and **minuit**.

<Example fr="Il est trois heures." en="It's three o'clock." />
<Example fr="Il est minuit !" en="It's midnight!" />

Because *heures* starts with a silent h, numbers link to it: you'll hear a z sound after *deux*, *trois*, *six* and *dix*, a v sound after *neuf*, a t sound after *huit* and *sept*, and a k sound after *cinq*.

## Past and to the hour

To add minutes, just say them after *heures*: *Il est sept heures dix* (7:10), *Il est neuf heures vingt-cinq* (9:25).

For the past half of the hour, you can use the words below. For minutes before the next hour, French counts backward from the coming hour using **moins** (minus).

<VocabTable group="parts" />

<Example fr="Il est dix heures et quart." en="It's quarter past ten." />
<Example fr="Il est onze heures moins le quart." en="It's quarter to eleven." />
<Example fr="Il est quatre heures moins vingt." en="It's twenty to four." />

Spelling note: after *heures* it's **et demie**, with an -e, because *heure* is feminine. After *midi* and *minuit*, which are masculine, it's **et demi**: *Il est midi et demi*.

## The time of day

To make it clear which part of the day you mean, add one of these. You'll understand how they're built in <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" />. For now, learn them as fixed phrases.

<VocabTable group="day-parts" />

<Example fr="Il est six heures du matin." en="It's six in the morning." />

## The 24-hour clock

In France, timetables, tickets and official schedules use the 24-hour clock, and you'll hear it in everyday speech too. Times are written with **h**: *14h*, *20h30*.

This style is simpler, since you say the hours and then the minutes as plain numbers. You don't use *quart*, *demie* or *moins*.

- *14h15*: *quatorze heures quinze*
- *18h30*: *dix-huit heures trente*
- *21h45*: *vingt et une heures quarante-cinq*

Notice that 21 hours is *vingt et une heures*, with *une* because *heure* is feminine.

## At what time?

To say when something happens, add **à** before the time.

<Example fr="Le film est à vingt heures trente." en="The film is at 8:30 p.m." />
<Example fr="À quelle heure ? — À midi." en="At what time? — At noon." />

## Putting it together

Here's a quick exchange at a train station:

- *Excusez-moi, il est quelle heure ?* (Excuse me, what time is it?)
- *Il est cinq heures moins dix.* (It's ten to five.)
- *Et le train, c'est à quelle heure ?* (And the train, what time is it?)
- *À dix-sept heures vingt.* (At 5:20 p.m.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't stop before heures">
  English lets you say "It's five" or "at ten thirty". French needs *heures* in the middle: **Il est cinq heures**, **à dix heures trente**. A bare *Il est cinq* sounds incomplete. And don't mix the two systems: *quinze heures et demie* isn't what you'd see on a timetable. There it's **quinze heures trente**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Subtract twelve">
  You'll see the 24-hour clock everywhere in France, but you can reply in the way you find easiest. When you see a time above 12:00, subtract twelve to get the familiar number: 19h30 is half past seven in the evening. A train departure board is good reading practice for this, and it's free.
</Callout>
