By the end of this lesson you'll be able to ask what someone is like, describe their height, hair and eyes, and say something about their personality.

## Two verbs do the work

Describing a person in French takes two verbs you already know. Use <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="être" /> for what someone **is**: tall, funny, shy. Use <LessonLink id="a1-avoir-present" text="avoir" /> for what someone **has**: blue eyes, long hair.

To ask for a description, you need just one short question.

<VocabTable group="question" />

## Height

<VocabTable group="height" />

With words like these, a woman's form usually adds an -e in writing, and you can often hear the difference, because the final consonant is pronounced: *grande*, *petite*. For now, learn both forms as a pair. <LessonLink id="a1-adjective-agreement" text="The lesson on adjective agreement" /> explains the rules.

<Example fr="Thomas est grand, mais Inès est petite." en="Thomas is tall, but Inès is short." />
<Example fr="Clara est de taille moyenne." en="Clara is of medium height." />

## Hair

For hair, French uses **avoir** and **les**, followed by *cheveux* and then the description.

<VocabTable group="hair" />

All these words are shown with their plural ending because *cheveux* is plural. You don't need any other form to describe hair, and the words come after *cheveux*, unlike in English. You can use two at once by joining them with *et*: *les cheveux longs et noirs*, or simply *les cheveux noirs et longs*.

<Example fr="Karim a les cheveux noirs et courts." en="Karim has short black hair." />

If you want to talk about the person instead, you can use *être*: *Elle est blonde*, *Il est brun*. That's the same idea as "she's a blonde" in English, with the form matching the person.

## Eyes

Eyes work exactly the same way.

<VocabTable group="eyes" />

<Example fr="Léa a les yeux verts." en="Léa has green eyes." />

*Marron* is the odd one out. It's the name of a brown nut (a chestnut), and it never takes a plural ending.

## Personality

For character, go back to **être**. Some words change for a woman, and some don't. If a word already ends in -e, like *drôle*, *timide* and *calme*, it's the same for everyone. *Sympa* doesn't change either.

<VocabTable group="personality" />

These three small words make your descriptions more exact. They go right before the describing word.

<VocabTable group="degree" />

<Example fr="Hugo est un peu timide." en="Hugo is a little shy." />
<Example fr="Nora est très drôle et assez sérieuse." en="Nora is very funny and quite reliable." />

## Putting it together

Here's a complete description of someone. Notice how *être* and *avoir* take turns.

- *Elle est comment, Sophie ?* (What's Sophie like?)
- *Elle est grande. Elle a les cheveux roux et les yeux bleus.* (She's tall. She has red hair and blue eyes.)
- *Elle est sympa ?* (Is she nice?)
- *Oui, très sympa, et très gentille !* (Yes, very nice, and very kind!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Hair is plural, and you have it">
  English says "He *is* blue-eyed" or "His hair *is* short", and treats hair as one thing. French says **Il a les yeux bleus** and **Il a les cheveux courts**: he "has the eyes blue" and "has the hairs short". Don't write *Il est les yeux bleus*, and don't write *le cheveux*. *Un cheveu*, in the singular, means one single strand of hair.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Sympa isn't sympathetic">
  *Sympa* looks like "sympathetic", but it means pleasant and easy to get on with. It's short for *sympathique*, and it's one of the most useful words in casual French. You can use it for people and for places or evenings too: *C'est sympa ici !* If someone feels sorry for you, that's a different word.
</Callout>
