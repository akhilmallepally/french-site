By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use *faire* (to do, to make) and *aller* (to go) with every pronoun, talk about jobs around the house, and say where you're going and how you are.

## Two verbs you can't avoid

*Faire* and *aller* are two of the most common verbs in French. Neither follows the pattern from <LessonLink id="a1-er-verbs-present" text="the lesson on regular -er verbs" />. *Aller* ends in -er, but it's irregular. The only way is to learn the forms, and because you'll use them constantly, they'll stick quickly.

## Faire: to do, to make

*Faire* covers both "do" and "make" in English. So *Je fais un gâteau* means "I'm making a cake", and *Je fais la vaisselle* means "I'm doing the dishes".

<VocabTable group="faire" />

Look out for two forms:

- **Nous faisons** is spelled with *ai*, but that *ai* is pronounced like the *e* of *le*.
- **Vous faites** has its own ending. Don't use the normal -ez.

## Using faire around the house

French uses *faire* for many everyday tasks. These are useful in the form of whole phrases. The article is part of the phrase, so learn it too.

<VocabTable group="faire-uses" />

<Example fr="Je fais le ménage le samedi." en="I do the housework on Saturdays." />
<Example fr="Tu fais les courses aujourd'hui ?" en="Are you doing the food shopping today?" />
<Example fr="Ils font la cuisine le soir." en="They do the cooking in the evening." />

*Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?* is a very common question, and it's a fixed phrase for now. You'll see how French question words work in <LessonLink id="a1-question-words" text="the lesson on question words" />.

## Aller: to go

*Aller* is the verb for going somewhere.

<VocabTable group="aller" />

Notice that the *nous* and *vous* forms start with *all-*, but the others start with *v-*. *Nous allons* and *vous allez* link with a z sound.

To go to a town or city, use **à**:

<Example fr="Je vais à Toulouse." en="I'm going to Toulouse." />
<Example fr="Elles vont à Bruxelles." en="They're going to Brussels." />

When you want to say you're going to a place like the cinema or the park, you'll need a small change to *à*. That comes in the next lesson, so for now, stick to towns and cities.

## Aller: how you are

French also uses *aller* for how someone is feeling. You've been using it since <LessonLink id="a1-greetings" text="the greetings lesson" />: *Ça va ?* is literally "it goes?"

<VocabTable group="aller-uses" />

<Example fr="Comment allez-vous, madame ? — Je vais bien, merci." en="How are you, madam? — I'm well, thank you." />

So "how are you?" is a question about how you're *going*, a bit like the English "how's it going?" Use *Comment allez-vous ?* in formal situations, and *Ça va ?* with friends.

## Putting it together

Here's a short exchange between two neighbours. Notice how each verb does its own job.

- *Bonjour, ça va ?* (Hello, how are you?)
- *Oui, je vais bien. Et vous ? Qu'est-ce que vous faites ?* (Yes, I'm well. And you? What are you doing?)
- *Je fais les courses, et je vais à Nantes.* (I'm doing the food shopping, and I'm going to Nantes.)

Be careful with translating. "I'm going" is simply **je vais**, and "I'm making" is simply **je fais**, with no extra word for "am".

<Callout type="warning" title="Vous faites, never vous faisez">
  Nearly every *vous* form you've learned ends in -ez, so *vous faisez* looks right. It isn't. The correct form is **vous faites**. Only a few verbs break the -ez rule, and you already know the most famous one: *vous êtes*. Both end in *-tes*, so remember them as a pair.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The -ont club">
  Four of the most common verbs in French share the same plural ending: **ils sont** (être), **ils ont** (avoir), **ils font** (faire) and **ils vont** (aller). Regular verbs use a silent *-ent*, but these four use a clearly audible *-ont*. Learn them as one group, and you've got the *ils* form of the four verbs you'll need most.
</Callout>
