By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say what you can do, what you want to do and what you have to do, and ask for permission politely.

## Three verbs that lean on another verb

*Pouvoir* (can), *vouloir* (to want) and *devoir* (must, to have to) are very common, and they all work the same way. Like *aller* in <LessonLink id="a1-near-future" text="the lesson on the near future" />, the first verb changes to match the subject, and a second verb follows it in the **infinitive**. Nothing goes between them.

All three are irregular, but they do have a pattern. The *je* and *tu* forms sound identical, and so does the *il/elle* form. *Nous* and *vous* have the usual -ons and -ez.

## Pouvoir: can

<VocabTable group="pouvoir" />

<Example fr="Tu peux aider Marc ?" en="Can you help Marc?" />
<Example fr="Nous pouvons rentrer à Lille." en="We can go back to Lille." />

*Pouvoir* is the verb for asking permission and making requests.

- **Je peux… ?** asks if you may do something: *Je peux payer ici ?*
- **Vous pouvez… ?** or **Tu peux… ?** asks someone to do something: *Vous pouvez répéter, s'il vous plaît ?*

## Vouloir: to want

<VocabTable group="vouloir" />

*Vouloir* can go before an infinitive, or before a noun.

<Example fr="Je veux voyager." en="I want to travel." />
<Example fr="Elles veulent un sandwich." en="They want a sandwich." />
<Example fr="Tu veux aller au cinéma ?" en="Do you want to go to the cinema?" />

## Je voudrais, revisited

In <LessonLink id="a1-ordering-at-a-cafe" text="the lesson on ordering at a café" />, you learned **je voudrais** as a fixed phrase. Now you can see where it comes from: it's a polite form of *vouloir*, and it means "I would like". *Je veux* is direct. It's fine with friends, but with staff or strangers it can sound demanding. So use **je voudrais** with people you call *vous*.

It works before a noun, and it also works before an infinitive, just as *je veux* does: *Je voudrais payer, s'il vous plaît*.

## Devoir: must, have to

<VocabTable group="devoir" />

<Example fr="Je dois rentrer." en="I have to go home." />
<Example fr="Vous devez tourner à droite." en="You have to turn right." />

## Making them negative

As with the near future, put *ne…pas* around the first verb, and leave the infinitive at the end.

<Example fr="Je ne veux pas manger de viande." en="I don't want to eat meat." />
<Example fr="Il ne peut pas payer par carte." en="He can't pay by card." />

Note that *répéter* follows the -er pattern, with a small change to its accent in some forms: *je répète*. For now, you'll mostly use the infinitive.

## Putting it together

Here's a short exchange between two friends making plans.

- *Tu veux manger au restaurant ce soir ?* (Do you want to eat at the restaurant tonight?)
- *Je ne peux pas. Je dois travailler.* (I can't. I have to work.)
- *Et demain ?* (And tomorrow?)
- *Oui, je peux demain. Je voudrais aller au cinéma.* (Yes, I can tomorrow. I'd like to go to the cinema.)

<Callout type="warning" title="The x in peux and veux">
  You might expect *je* and *tu* to end in -s, and with *devoir* they do: *je dois*. But *pouvoir* and *vouloir* end in **-x**: **je peux**, **tu peux**, **je veux**, **tu veux**. You can't hear the difference, so it's purely something to check in writing. And don't add a word before the second verb: it's **Je dois travailler**, not *Je dois à travailler*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Two words that ask for anything">
  You don't always need a verb after *Je peux ?* Point at a free chair, a phone charger or the last biscuit, and say **Je peux ?** with a smile. Anyone will understand that you're asking "May I?" It's one of the shortest and most useful polite phrases in French.
</Callout>
