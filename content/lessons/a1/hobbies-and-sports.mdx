By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about the sports you play, the instruments you play, and how you like to spend your free time.

## What do you do in your free time?

French has two main verbs for free-time activities: **jouer** (to play), a regular -er verb, and **faire** (to do). Each one comes with **à** or **de**, so you'll put <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" /> to work straight away.

The tricky part is that the preposition changes with the kind of activity.

## Jouer à: games and ball sports

As a rough guide, games and ball sports take **jouer à**. Remember that *à* melts together with *le* and *les*.

<VocabTable group="jouer-a" />

<Example fr="Je joue au tennis le dimanche." en="I play tennis on Sundays." />
<Example fr="Ils jouent aux jeux vidéo le soir." en="They play video games in the evening." />

*Le foot* is the everyday word for football (soccer in American English). *Le football américain* is the other kind.

## Jouer de: musical instruments

For musical instruments, use **jouer de**. English says "play *the* piano", and French literally says "play *of the* piano".

<VocabTable group="jouer-de" />

<Example fr="Lucas joue du violon." en="Lucas plays the violin." />

So the rule is: **à for games, de for music**.

## Faire de: many other activities

Other sports and activities, the ones without a ball, generally take **faire de**. This is a rough guide rather than a law, so learn each phrase as you meet it.

<VocabTable group="faire" />

<Example fr="Nous faisons du yoga le matin." en="We do yoga in the morning." />
<Example fr="Elle fait de la randonnée." en="She goes hiking." />

English often uses a single verb for these ("to cycle", "to ski", "to swim"), but French likes to say "do some cycling". For now, just learn each phrase as a whole. The form of *faire* comes from <LessonLink id="a1-faire-and-aller" text="the lesson on faire and aller" />.

## Music, reading and going out

Some free-time activities don't need either verb.

<VocabTable group="other" />

*Lire* (to read) is an irregular verb, so for now, use it only as an infinitive after *j'aime* or *j'adore*.

## Talking about what you like

All of this works together with what you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-likes-and-dislikes" text="the lesson on likes and dislikes" />. Put *jouer* or *faire* in the infinitive straight after *aimer*, and keep the preposition.

<Example fr="J'aime jouer aux échecs." en="I like playing chess." />
<Example fr="Tu aimes faire du ski ?" en="Do you like skiing?" />
<Example fr="Je déteste faire de la natation." en="I hate swimming." />

You can also talk about a sport as a general thing, without a verb, and then it takes *le* or *la*: *J'adore le tennis*, *Je déteste le ski*.

## Putting it together

Here's a short chat about free time. Look at how the verbs change with the activity.

- *Tu fais du sport ?* (Do you do any sport?)
- *Oui, je joue au foot, et je fais du vélo.* (Yes, I play football and I cycle.)
- *Moi, je joue de la guitare, et j'aime lire.* (Me, I play the guitar, and I like reading.)

<Callout type="warning" title="À for games, de for music">
  It's easy to mix these up, since English says "play" for both. You can't say *Je joue au piano* in standard French, or *Je joue du tennis*. It's **Je joue du piano** and **Je joue au tennis**. Ask yourself first: is this a game or an instrument?
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="French loves a short word">
  French people clip long words all the time. *Le football* is *le foot*, *le basketball* is *le basket*, *le cinéma* is *le ciné*, *la télévision* is *la télé*, and *le restaurant* is *le resto*. Use the short form with friends and you'll sound relaxed. Keep the gender when you shorten it.
</Callout>
