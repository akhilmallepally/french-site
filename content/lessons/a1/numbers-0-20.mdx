By the end of this lesson you'll be able to count from zero to twenty, understand and read out a French phone number, and say how old you are.

## Zero to ten

<VocabTable group="zero-to-ten" />

**Un or une?** *Un* is the number you use when counting. Before a noun, "one" has to match the noun's gender: *un livre* (one book, masculine) but *une table* (one table, feminine). You'll learn about gender in <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on gender and articles" />.

## Eleven to twenty

<VocabTable group="eleven-to-twenty" />

There's a pattern here. The numbers **11 to 16** each have their own word, all ending in **-ze**: *onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize*. From **17**, French switches to "ten-seven", "ten-eight", "ten-nine", written with a hyphen: *dix-sept, dix-huit, dix-neuf*. Then comes *vingt*.

## Final consonants: the tricky part

Several numbers change their last sound depending on what comes after them. Learn the "counting" pronunciation in the table first, then notice these patterns as you meet them:

- **On their own** (counting, or at the end of a sentence), *six*, *huit* and *dix* pronounce their final letter. The x of *six* and *dix* sounds like s.
- **Before a consonant**, *six*, *huit* and *dix* usually drop their final sound: *six livres* (six books), *huit jours* (eight days), *dix minutes*.
- **Before a vowel**, the final letter links to the next word. *Deux*, *trois*, *six* and *dix* link with a z sound: *deux ans*, *trois ans*, *six ans*, *dix ans*. *Neuf* links with a v sound in *neuf ans* (nine years) and *neuf heures* (nine o'clock).

*Sept* always pronounces its t, and its p is always silent. *Vingt* is said without its g and t when it's on its own, but the t is heard in *vingt ans*.

## Phone numbers

French phone numbers have ten digits and are written and read **in pairs**: 06 12 15 18 02 is *zéro six, douze, quinze, dix-huit, zéro deux*. Mobile numbers start with 06 or 07. Many pairs are above twenty, so you'll need <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-21-100" text="the next numbers lesson" /> to read most numbers. Until then, saying digits one by one will be understood.

## Saying your age

To give your age, French uses *avoir* (to have), not *être*: **J'ai dix-huit ans**, literally "I have eighteen years". You'll learn *avoir* fully in <LessonLink id="a1-avoir-present" text="the lesson on avoir" />. For now, learn *J'ai … ans* and *Tu as quel âge ?* as fixed phrases. In a full sentence, keep *ans* (years): *J'ai dix-huit ans*, not *J'ai dix-huit*. Answering with just the number, *Dix-huit.*, is fine.

<Example fr="Tu as quel âge ? — J'ai dix-neuf ans." en="How old are you? — I'm nineteen." />
<Example fr="Mon numéro, c'est le zéro six, douze, quinze, dix-huit, zéro deux." en="My number is 06 12 15 18 02." />
<Example fr="Un café et deux croissants, s'il vous plaît." en="One coffee and two croissants, please." />
<Example fr="Elle a six ans." en="She's six (years old)." />
<Example fr="Il est huit heures." en="It's eight o'clock." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't pronounce every final letter">
  English speakers tend to say *six*, *huit* and *dix* the same way everywhere. In French the end of the number changes with the next word: the full sound when counting, usually no final sound before a consonant (*six livres*), and a linking sound before a vowel (*six ans*, *dix ans*). Getting this right is one of the quickest ways to sound less like a beginner, so don't worry if it takes a while.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The -ze family">
  To remember 11 to 16, think of them as a family that all end in **-ze**: *onze, douze, treize, quatorze, quinze, seize*. Each one is related to its small number: *douze* and *deux*, *treize* and *trois*, *quatorze* and *quatre*, *quinze* and *cinq*, *seize* and *six*. After the family ends, French just counts "ten-seven, ten-eight, ten-nine". Belgium and Switzerland count the same way up to twenty. Their famous differences start at seventy.
</Callout>
