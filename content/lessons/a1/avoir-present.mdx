By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use *avoir* (to have) with every subject pronoun, give your age, say you're hungry or cold, and say what there is somewhere with *il y a*.

## The second big verb

*Avoir* means "to have". Along with *être*, it's one of the two most important verbs in French, and it's irregular, so you learn the forms by heart. You'll use the pronouns from <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the subject pronouns lesson" />.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

A few things to notice:

- **J'ai** always shortens, because *ai* starts with a vowel.
- **Nous avons**, **vous avez**, **ils ont** and **elles ont** all link. The normally silent s is pronounced as a z sound, joining the two words.
- **Tu as** and **il a** sound alike at the end, since the s of *as* is silent.

## Having things and people

*Avoir* usually comes before *un*, *une* or *des*, which you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-indefinite-articles" text="the lesson on un, une and des" />, or before a number.

<Example fr="J'ai une voiture." en="I have a car." />
<Example fr="Tu as un stylo ?" en="Do you have a pen?" />
<Example fr="Elle a deux enfants." en="She has two children." />

This is also the verb behind the phrase you used in <LessonLink id="a1-family" text="the family lesson" />: *J'ai un frère et une sœur*.

## Giving your age

As <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-0-20" text="the numbers lesson" /> showed in passing, French uses *avoir* for age. You "have" your years, and you always need the word *ans*.

<VocabTable group="age" />

<Example fr="J'ai vingt ans. Et toi ?" en="I'm twenty. And you?" />
<Example fr="Le fils de Marie a six ans." en="Marie's son is six." />

The usual link applies between a number and *ans*, so the end of the number joins on to the vowel. *Tu as quel âge ?* is what you'll hear most in conversation. *Quel âge as-tu ?* is a little more formal.

## Hungry, thirsty, hot and cold

For a few physical feelings, French uses *avoir* with a noun where English uses "to be" with an adjective. You literally "have hunger".

<VocabTable group="feelings" />

<Example fr="J'ai faim !" en="I'm hungry!" />
<Example fr="Vous avez froid ?" en="Are you cold?" />

*Faim*, *soif*, *chaud* and *froid* stay exactly as they are, whoever is speaking: *Elle a faim*, *Nous avons froid*.

## There is, there are: il y a

**Il y a** is one fixed phrase meaning "there is" or "there are". Learn it as a single piece. It never changes, even with a plural.

<VocabTable group="there" />

<Example fr="Il y a un livre ici." en="There's a book here." />
<Example fr="Il y a deux chaises." en="There are two chairs." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Ils ont or ils sont?">
  These two look and sound close, and they mean very different things. **Ils ont** means "they have", and the link is a z sound. **Ils sont** means "they are", and you hear an s at the start of *sont*. Listen for that sound: z for having, s for being. The same goes for *elles ont* and *elles sont*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Does 'has' fit?">
  *A* and *à* sound the same, but they're different words. When you're writing, try "has" in its place. *Elle a un frère*: "she has a brother" works, so no accent. *Elle est à Paris*: "she is has Paris" makes no sense, so it's *à*.
</Callout>

## Putting it together

Here's a short chat between two students who have just met. Notice that they use *tu* with each other, and that *avoir* handles the family, the age and the hunger.

- *Tu as des frères et sœurs ?* (Do you have brothers and sisters?)
- *Oui, j'ai une sœur. Elle a dix ans. Et toi ?* (Yes, I have a sister. She's ten. And you?)
- *J'ai deux frères. Tu as faim ?* (I have two brothers. Are you hungry?)
- *Oui ! Il y a un café ici ?* (Yes! Is there a café here?)
