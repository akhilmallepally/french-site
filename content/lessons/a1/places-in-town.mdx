By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name the shops, buildings and transport stops in a town, and say what there is and what there isn't.

## Your town

First, some words for a town itself.

<VocabTable group="town" />

## Shops

Learn each of these with its article, because you'll need the gender to say where you're going.

<VocabTable group="shops" />

You know how to go to places from <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" />. It works the same way here: the article decides what happens.

<Example fr="Je vais à la pharmacie." en="I'm going to the chemist's." />
<Example fr="On va au marché ?" en="Shall we go to the market?" />

## Buildings

<VocabTable group="buildings" />

*La poste* is usually a building with a yellow sign. *La mairie* is the town hall, which in France is one of the most important buildings in any town or village, and it's where weddings take place.

<Example fr="La mairie est ici." en="The town hall is here." />

## Getting around

<VocabTable group="transport" />

Notice that French has different words for different kinds of station. **La gare** is for trains. **La station** is for the metro, and **l'arrêt** is a stop for buses or trams.

<Example fr="Voici l'arrêt de bus." en="Here's the bus stop." />

## What there is and what there isn't

To say what you can find in a place, you need *il y a*. To say something's missing, use the negative you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-negation-ne-pas" text="the lesson on negation" />.

<VocabTable group="there" />

*Il y a* never changes, whether what follows is singular or plural. After it, use *un*, *une* or *des*.

After the negative, those three all change to **de**, or **d'** before a vowel or silent *h*.

<Example fr="Il y a une librairie et une boucherie." en="There's a bookshop and a butcher's." />
<Example fr="Il y a des magasins ici." en="There are some shops here." />
<Example fr="Il n'y a pas d'hôpital ici." en="There's no hospital here." />
<Example fr="Il n'y a pas de gare à Sarlat." en="There's no train station in Sarlat." />

To ask if something's there, make it a question, as you've done before: *Il y a une banque ici ?* or *Est-ce qu'il y a une banque ici ?*

## Putting it together

Here's someone describing a small town to a friend. Notice how *il y a* and *il n'y a pas de* take turns.

- *Il y a un supermarché ?* (Is there a supermarket?)
- *Oui, et il y a une boulangerie et une pharmacie.* (Yes, and there's a bakery and a chemist's.)
- *Il y a une gare ?* (Is there a station?)
- *Non, il n'y a pas de gare, mais il y a un arrêt de bus.* (No, there's no station, but there's a bus stop.)

For practice, try describing your own town or neighbourhood in the same way. Start with three things there are, and finish with one that there isn't. You can use the same pattern for any place you know: your street, your school or the place where you work.

<Callout type="warning" title="A librairie isn't a library">
  The word looks exactly like "library", but **la librairie** is a shop where you buy books. If you want to borrow a book, you need **la bibliothèque**. Similarly, **la gare** is only for trains. Asking for *la gare* when you want the bus stop will send you to the wrong place.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A green cross means a chemist">
  Some French shops can be recognized from far away. A flashing green cross outside a building means *une pharmacie*, and you can usually find one even in a small town. A tall red sign shaped like a long diamond marks a *tabac*, which sells stamps and bus tickets as well as cigarettes.
</Callout>
