By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say your nationality, recognise the names of common countries, ask someone where they live, and say which city you live in.

## Saying your nationality

To give your nationality, use *être* (see <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="the verb être" />) plus a nationality word: *Je suis canadien*, *Elle est française*. There's no word for "a" and no extra verb. It works just like *Je suis fatigué*.

French nationality words change depending on who they describe. There's a basic form for a man, and a feminine form for a woman. Most follow one of three patterns.

## Pattern 1: add -e

Most nationalities simply add **-e** for a woman.

<VocabTable group="add-e" />

The added -e often changes what you hear. In the basic form the last consonant is usually silent. In the feminine form it's pronounced: you can't hear the s of *français*, but you can hear a z sound in *française*. The d of *allemande* is sounded in the same way, and it isn't in *allemand*. The ending of *américain* is nasal, and *américaine* loses that nasal sound.

## Pattern 2: -ien becomes -ienne

Words ending in **-ien** double the n before adding -e.

<VocabTable group="double-n" />

Here too the sound changes: the nasal ending of *canadien* becomes a clear n sound in *canadienne*.

## Pattern 3: already ends in -e

If the word already ends in **-e**, nothing changes. A man and a woman can both be *belge*, *suisse* or *britannique*.

<VocabTable group="same" />

One note for people from the UK: *anglais* means English, specifically. If you're Scottish, Welsh or from Northern Ireland, *britannique* is a safe choice.

## Countries

Countries in French usually come with a word for "the": *la France*, *le Canada*, *les États-Unis*, or *l'Italie* before a vowel. You'll learn how these work in <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on gender and articles" />. For now, learn each country with its little word in front.

<VocabTable group="countries" />

Notice how close many countries and nationalities look: *le Japon* and *japonais*, *l'Italie* and *italien*. But they're different words, so *Je suis Japon* doesn't work.

## Where you live

The phrase **J'habite à…** works with any city or town: *J'habite à Paris*, *J'habite à Montréal*. Learn it as a set phrase. The h of *habite* is silent, which is why *je* becomes *j'*.

For countries, French uses other small words, such as *en France* and *au Canada*. They have their own lesson later, <LessonLink id="a2-prepositions-with-places" text="on prepositions with countries and cities" />. Until then, name your city.

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

<Example fr="Je suis canadienne, mais j'habite à Lyon." en="I'm Canadian, but I live in Lyon. (said by a woman)" />
<Example fr="Tu habites où ? — J'habite à Bruxelles." en="Where do you live? — I live in Brussels." />
<Example fr="Karim est marocain." en="Karim is Moroccan." />
<Example fr="Vous êtes suisse, madame ?" en="Are you Swiss, madam?" />
<Example fr="Yuki est japonaise." en="Yuki is Japanese." />

<Callout type="warning" title="No capital letter after être">
  English always capitalises nationalities, so *Je suis Français* looks right to English speakers. In French, a nationality that describes someone normally takes a small letter: **Je suis français**, **Elle est anglaise**. The capital is for a noun meaning the person, as in *un Français* (a Frenchman). Country names do take a capital: *la France*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The -e wakes up the consonant">
  Picture the last consonant of *français* or *allemand* as asleep. When the feminine -e arrives, it wakes up and gets pronounced. That one picture covers most of the pairs in the first table, so you can work out how a feminine form sounds without memorizing it separately.
</Callout>
