By the end of this lesson you'll be able to make any sentence negative, say what you don't like and don't do, and use *pas de* to say you don't have something.

## Two words around the verb

English makes a sentence negative with "not" or "don't". French uses two small words that wrap around the verb like a sandwich: **ne** before it and **pas** after it.

<VocabTable group="pattern" />

Take any verb you know, such as those in <LessonLink id="a1-er-verbs-present" text="the lesson on regular -er verbs" />, and add the two words. There's no separate word for "do".

<Example fr="Nous ne regardons pas la télé." en="We're not watching TV." />
<Example fr="Je ne travaille pas à Bordeaux." en="I don't work in Bordeaux." />

Like *je*, the word *ne* shortens before a vowel or silent *h*. It becomes **n'**.

<Example fr="Elle n'habite pas ici." en="She doesn't live here." />

## With the verbs you use most

*Être* and *avoir* work in exactly the same way. Here are the negative forms you'll use constantly, including the ones you were missing in <LessonLink id="a1-likes-and-dislikes" text="the lesson on likes and dislikes" />.

<VocabTable group="useful" />

Remember that *ai*, *as*, *a*, *avons*, *avez* and *ont* are forms of *avoir*, and they all start with a vowel, so *ne* always becomes **n'** with this verb: *tu n'as pas*, *nous n'avons pas*.

When there's a second verb, the negative goes around the first one, and the infinitive comes after: *Je n'aime pas cuisiner* (I don't like cooking).

<Example fr="Je n'aime pas la pizza." en="I don't like pizza." />

## Un, une, des become de

Here's the one change that makes negatives tricky. You know *un*, *une* and *des* from <LessonLink id="a1-indefinite-articles" text="the lesson on un, une and des" />. After a negative verb, all three usually change to **de**, or **d'** before a vowel or silent *h*. The noun itself doesn't change.

- *J'ai un sac.* → *Je n'ai pas de sac.* (I don't have a bag.)
- *Tu as une clé ?* → *Tu n'as pas de clé ?* (You don't have a key?)
- *Il y a des chaises.* → *Il n'y a pas de chaises.* (There aren't any chairs.)
- *Elle a des amis.* → *Elle n'a pas d'amis.* (She doesn't have any friends.)

This is the same thing as saying "no" or "not any" in English. If you're saying that something doesn't exist or isn't there, use *pas de*.

<Example fr="Il n'y a pas de téléphone ici." en="There's no phone here." />
<Example fr="Nous n'avons pas d'ordinateur." en="We don't have a computer." />

## Le, la, les and être don't change

Two things stay as they are.

**Le, la, l' and les never change.** That's why you say *Je n'aime pas le café*, keeping the same article as *J'aime le café*.

**After être, un, une and des don't change.** You're saying what something isn't, not that it's missing: *C'est un stylo* becomes *Ce n'est pas un stylo*.

<Example fr="Ce n'est pas une maison, c'est un café !" en="It's not a house, it's a café!" />

## Putting it together

Here's a person answering questions about themselves. Notice how each answer follows one of the patterns.

- *Tu parles italien ?* — *Non, je ne parle pas italien.* (Do you speak Italian? — No, I don't speak Italian.)
- *Tu aimes les chats ?* — *Non, je n'aime pas les chats.* (Do you like cats? — No, I don't like cats.)
- *Tu as une voiture ?* — *Non, je n'ai pas de voiture.* (Do you have a car? — No, I don't have a car.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Not every article becomes de">
  It's easy to learn "negative means de" and apply it everywhere. Only **un, une and des** change, and not after *être*. So it's **Je n'aime pas le thé**, never *Je n'aime pas de thé* for tea in general, and **Ce n'est pas un chat**, not *Ce n'est pas de chat*. Check which article you started with.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen for pas">
  In everyday speech, people very often drop the *ne*. You'll hear *Je parle pas anglais* and *J'aime pas ça*. So when you're trying to follow a conversation, don't wait for *ne*: *pas* is the word that tells you it's negative. Keep the *ne* when you write, and in careful speech.
</Callout>
