By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say what you love, like and hate, ask someone else what they enjoy, and talk about activities as well as things.

## Three verbs, one pattern

To talk about what you enjoy, you need three verbs. All of them are regular, so you already know how to use them from <LessonLink id="a1-er-verbs-present" text="the lesson on regular -er verbs" />. *Aimer* and *adorer* start with a vowel, so *je* shortens: **j'aime**, **j'adore**. *Détester* starts with a consonant: **je déteste**.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

From strongest to weakest, you can rank them like this: *j'adore*, *j'aime*, *j'aime bien*. *Je déteste* sits at the far end, and it's strong, like "hate" in English.

Asking is simple. Turn the statement into a question with your voice, or a question mark when writing: *Tu aimes le café ?* You can then return the question with *Et toi ?* or *Et vous ?*

## Liking things: use le, la, les

After these verbs, French nouns take a **definite article**, the *le*, *la*, *l'* or *les* that you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on le, la and les" />. It's there even when English has nothing at all.

When you're talking about something in general, this article carries the meaning "in general". *J'aime le chocolat* means you like chocolate as a thing, not one particular bar.

With things you can count, like animals or films, use the plural: *J'adore les chiens*.

<VocabTable group="things" />

<Example fr="J'aime le cinéma." en="I like films." />
<Example fr="Tu aimes la pizza ?" en="Do you like pizza?" />
<Example fr="Je déteste les chiens." en="I hate dogs." />
<Example fr="Elles adorent le thé." en="They love tea." />

## Liking activities: use the infinitive

To say you like doing something, put a second verb straight after. The first verb changes to match the subject, but the second one stays in the **infinitive**, the form ending in -er. English sometimes uses "-ing" here and sometimes "to". French has just this one way.

<VocabTable group="activities" />

<Example fr="Elle adore cuisiner." en="She loves cooking." />
<Example fr="Vous aimez voyager ?" en="Do you like travelling?" />
<Example fr="Je déteste travailler le soir." en="I hate working in the evening." />

So the first verb changes, and the second doesn't: **J'aime danser**, **Nous aimons danser**, **Ils aiment danser**. You also don't add any little word between them.

## Putting it together

Here's a short exchange between two new friends. See how they move between things and activities.

- *Tu aimes le sport ?* (Do you like sport?)
- *Non, je déteste le sport. J'adore la musique. Et toi ?* (No, I hate sport. I love music. And you?)
- *Moi, j'aime bien chanter, et j'adore voyager.* (Me, I quite like singing, and I love travelling.)

There's one thing you can't say yet: "I don't like". That's coming straight away in <LessonLink id="a1-negation-ne-pas" text="the lesson on negation" />. Until then, *je déteste* will have to do, though it's rather strong.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't leave out the article">
  English says "I like coffee" with no article, so it's easy to write *J'aime café*. French nearly always needs one here. Say **J'aime le café**. It's also not *un*: *J'aime un café* doesn't mean you like coffee in general. Keep *le*, *la*, *l'* or *les* after these verbs when you're talking about things in general.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Bien turns love down">
  You'd expect *bien* (well, good) to make a verb stronger, but with *aimer* it does the opposite when you're talking about people. *J'aime Paul* can mean you're in love with Paul. *J'aime bien Paul* means you simply like Paul. Use *aimer bien* for friends and colleagues, and you'll avoid some awkward moments.
</Callout>
