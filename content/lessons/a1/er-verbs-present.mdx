By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use any regular *-er* verb with every subject pronoun, say what you do and where you live and work, and know which endings you can hear.

## A pattern, at last

So far, you've met verbs one at a time. Here's a whole family that follows one pattern. In the dictionary, French verbs appear in a basic form called the **infinitive**, like "to speak" in English. A very large group of them have infinitives ending in **-er**: *parler*, *habiter*, *travailler*.

To use one of these verbs, remove the *-er* to get the **stem**, then add the ending that matches the subject. You'll use the pronouns from <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the subject pronouns lesson" />.

| Subject | Ending | parler → parl- |
|---|---|---|
| je | -e | parle |
| tu | -es | parles |
| il / elle / on | -e | parle |
| nous | -ons | parlons |
| vous | -ez | parlez |
| ils / elles | -ent | parlent |

## Which endings you can hear

This is the best news about -er verbs. Three of the endings are **completely silent**: -e, -es and -ent. So *parle*, *parles* and *parlent* all sound the same. In speech you only have to get the stem right, except with *nous* and *vous*.

The ending **-ons** sounds nasal, and **-ez** sounds just like the end of the infinitive, so *parlez* and *parler* sound the same.

You still have to write the endings, though. *Ils parlent* needs its -ent even though nobody hears it.

## Verbs starting with a vowel

Some verbs start with a vowel or a silent *h*. Two familiar things happen. *Je* shortens to **j'**, as in *j'habite*, and the final s of *nous*, *vous*, *ils* and *elles* links with a z sound, as in *nous habitons*.

## One form, several meanings

English has several ways of saying the present: "I work", "I'm working", "I do work". French uses a single form for all of them: **je travaille**. The context tells the listener which you mean.

If you use the verb *manger* (to eat), you'll spot a small spelling change with *nous*: **nous mangeons**. The extra e keeps the g soft. Every other form follows the pattern.

## Putting it together

Here's a quick exchange between two people at a conference. They use *vous* because they've just met.

- *Bonjour ! Vous habitez à Paris ?* (Hello! Do you live in Paris?)
- *Non, j'habite à Rouen, et je travaille à Caen. Et vous ?* (No, I live in Rouen, and I work in Caen. And you?)
- *Moi, j'étudie à Strasbourg.* (Me, I study in Strasbourg.)

<Callout type="warning" title="No 'am' in front of the verb">
  English says "I *am* working", so it's tempting to write *Je suis travaille*. French doesn't build its present that way. Drop the "am" and use the verb on its own: **Je travaille**. The same goes for "I *do* work" or "Do you work?": there's no separate word for "do" in these sentences.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A pattern worth thousands of verbs">
  The great majority of French verbs end in -er, and nearly all of them follow this pattern. New verbs often join the family too, as in *télécharger* (to download). So every time you meet a new -er verb, you already know how to use it with all the pronouns, with no extra work.
</Callout>
