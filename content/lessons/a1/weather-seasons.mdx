By the end of this lesson you'll be able to ask about the weather, describe it, and name the four seasons.

## Faire does the weather

In <LessonLink id="a1-faire-and-aller" text="the lesson on faire and aller" /> you learned the forms of *faire*. Most descriptions of the weather start with **il fait**. As with telling the time, *il* doesn't refer to anyone here. It means "it".

To ask about it, you need only one question.

<VocabTable group="question" />

<VocabTable group="faire" />

Describing words after *il fait* don't change. They're always in the basic masculine form, because they aren't describing anybody.

<Example fr="Il fait mauvais à Brest." en="The weather's bad in Brest." />
<Example fr="Aujourd'hui, il fait trente degrés !" en="It's thirty degrees today!" />

## Rain and snow

Two kinds of weather have verbs of their own, so you don't use *fait* with them.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

*Il pleut* and *il neige* can mean "it's raining" or "it rains", just as other French verbs cover both.

<Example fr="Il pleut en novembre." en="It rains in November." />

## Sun, wind and cloud

For these, French uses *il y a* (there is, there are) with a noun. For now, learn them as whole phrases. You'll see why it's *du* and *des* in <LessonLink id="a1-partitive-articles" text="the lesson on du, de la and des" />.

<VocabTable group="il-y-a" />

<Example fr="Il y a du soleil, mais il fait froid." en="It's sunny, but it's cold." />

## The four seasons

<VocabTable group="seasons" />

To say "in" a season, use **en**, as you do with months from <LessonLink id="a1-days-months-dates" text="the lesson on days, months and dates" />: *en été*, *en automne*, *en hiver*. The one exception is spring, which takes **au**: *au printemps*. *Été*, *automne* and *hiver* all begin with a vowel sound, and *printemps* begins with a consonant.

The four seasons are all masculine.

<Example fr="Au printemps, il fait beau à Paris." en="In spring, the weather's nice in Paris." />
<Example fr="En hiver, il neige à Chamonix." en="In winter, it snows in Chamonix." />

## Weather or you?

*Il fait chaud* describes the weather outside. If you want to say how **you** feel, use *avoir*, as you may remember from <LessonLink id="a1-avoir-present" text="the lesson on avoir" />.

## Putting it together

Here's a short phone call between two friends in different towns.

- *Allô ! Il fait quel temps à Nice ?* (Hello! What's the weather like in Nice?)
- *Il fait beau, il y a du soleil. Et à Lille ?* (It's nice, it's sunny. And in Lille?)
- *Il pleut, et il fait froid.* (It's raining, and it's cold.)

Now try matching the weather to the months you already know. Pick a month and describe a typical day where you live: *En juillet, il fait chaud* or *En décembre, il pleut*. If you can, add a temperature. French weather reports use degrees Celsius, so twenty degrees is pleasantly warm, and thirty degrees is hot. Talking about the weather is an easy way to start a conversation, and now you have the words for it.

<Callout type="warning" title="Je suis chaud isn't about the weather">
  English says "I am cold" and "It is cold", using "to be" for both. French separates them: **Il fait froid** for the weather, and **J'ai froid** for you. Don't say *Je suis froid* or *Je suis chaud* to mean you feel cold or hot.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A verb with only one person">
  *Pleuvoir* (to rain) and *neiger* (to snow) are used almost only with *il*, because nobody else does the raining. So while other verbs have six forms to learn, for these you only need *il pleut* and *il neige*. They're two of the easiest verbs in French.
</Callout>
