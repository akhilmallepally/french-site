By the end of this lesson you'll know when French people usually eat, how a full meal is organized, what happens with the bread, and what to expect if you're invited to someone's home.

## Three meals, and a snack

You know the names of the meals from <LessonLink id="a1-food-and-drink" text="the lesson on food and drink" />. Here's roughly how they work in France. Habits vary from one family to another, but these are the broad patterns.

**Le petit déjeuner** is usually light and often sweet: coffee, tea or hot chocolate, with bread and butter or jam, or cereal. A big cooked breakfast is unusual.

**Le déjeuner** is usually eaten somewhere between noon and two o'clock. Many schools and workplaces have a proper lunch break, and many people still sit down to a real meal.

**Le dîner** is usually eaten between about half past seven and nine.

Children often have a fourth moment in the day, **le goûter**: an afternoon snack after school, often bread with chocolate, or a biscuit and some fruit.

## The order of a meal

On a normal weekday, a family dinner can be a single dish. But a full French meal, at a restaurant or on a special occasion, follows a set order.

<VocabTable group="courses" />

The order is usually: *apéro*, *entrée*, *plat principal*, cheese, *dessert*, and sometimes a coffee at the end. Notice that cheese comes **before** dessert, and after the main course.

<Example fr="Voici l'entrée, et voici le plat principal." en="Here's the starter, and here's the main course." />
<Example fr="Le fromage, c'est avant le dessert." en="Cheese comes before dessert." />

## The apéro

**L'apéro**, short for *l'apéritif*, is a French institution. Before a meal with friends, people have a drink, often with olives, crisps or small snacks. It can last a good while, and it's a social event in its own right. If you're invited *pour l'apéro*, you may be there only for drinks, not a full meal.

## Bread

Bread is on the table at almost every meal, and the classic loaf is **la baguette**. Many people still buy it fresh from **la boulangerie**, often on the way home.

<VocabTable group="words" />

At the table, your piece of bread normally sits **directly on the table**, next to your plate. There's usually no side plate. Break off a piece with your hands instead of cutting it with a knife.

<Example fr="La baguette, c'est à la boulangerie." en="You get baguettes at the bakery." />

## At the table

If you're eating with French people, a few small habits are useful. Keep your hands visible, with your wrists resting on the edge of the table, not in your lap. Wait until everyone is served before you begin.

The two phrases you'll hear most are these:

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

<Example fr="À table ! C'est le dîner !" en="Come and sit down! It's dinner time!" />
<Example fr="Merci, et bon appétit !" en="Thank you, and enjoy your meal!" />

A meal is also a moment for talking, so don't be surprised if it takes a long time. Getting up and leaving as soon as your plate is empty can seem rude. If you're invited, you might see <LessonLink id="a1-culture-politeness" text="the lesson on politeness and la bise" /> for how to arrive and greet people.

<Callout type="warning" title="An entrée isn't a main course">
  In American English, an "entrée" is a main dish. In France, **l'entrée** is the starter, the dish that "enters" first. If you order one and expect a full plate, you'll get a small surprise. The main course is **le plat principal**, or often just **le plat**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Plan around kitchen hours">
  Many traditional restaurants serve lunch from about noon to two, and open again for dinner at about seven. If you're hungry at four in the afternoon, you may find the kitchen closed. Look for a *boulangerie* or a place that advertises *service continu*, which means food all day.
</Callout>
