By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say you're going to the cinema, the park or the beach, and join *à* and *de* correctly to the words for "the".

## Two words that melt together

You've used **à** with towns and cities, as in <LessonLink id="a1-faire-and-aller" text="the lesson on faire and aller" />: *Je vais à Nantes*. To go to a place like the cinema, you need *à* and also a word for "the", from <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on le, la and les" />.

The trouble is that French doesn't allow *à le* or *à les*. Those pairs always melt into a single word. The feminine *la* and the shortened *l'* don't cause any change, so they stay separate.

## À with the article

<VocabTable group="a" />

Only two forms are new: **à + le = au**, and **à + les = aux**. *Au* and *aux* sound the same, and the x of *aux* links with a z sound before a vowel, as in *aux États-Unis*.

Here are some places to practise with. Pay attention to the articles, which decide what happens.

<VocabTable group="places" />

<Example fr="Elle va au musée." en="She's going to the museum." />
<Example fr="On va à la piscine ?" en="Shall we go to the pool?" />
<Example fr="Je vais à l'hôtel." en="I'm going to the hotel." />
<Example fr="Vous allez aux États-Unis ?" en="Are you going to the United States?" />

*À* also means "at" or "in" a place, so the same forms work for where you are: *Il travaille au restaurant* (He works at the restaurant).

## De with the article

**De** can mean "of", "from" or "about". It works just like *à*: it melts together with *le* and *les*, and leaves *la* and *l'* alone.

<VocabTable group="de" />

Only two forms are new here too: **de + le = du**, and **de + les = des**.

<Example fr="Je rentre du travail." en="I'm coming home from work." />
<Example fr="C'est la porte de la maison." en="That's the door of the house." />
<Example fr="Nous parlons de l'école." en="We're talking about school." />
<Example fr="Le prix des billets, c'est trente euros." en="The price of the tickets is thirty euros." />

## Which des is this?

You already know a *des* that means "some", as in *des livres*, and it looks exactly like this one. The context tells you which is which. After a word like *le prix* or *parler*, it's normally **de + les**. In front of a noun you're just introducing, it means "some". You don't need to think much about it, because they're spelled the same way.

## No contraction with people's names

These changes only happen with the articles *le* and *les*. Names don't have an article, so nothing changes: *Je parle à Paul*, *le livre de Marc*.

## Putting it together

Here's an exchange about weekend plans. Every sentence uses a form from this lesson.

- *Tu vas à la plage ?* (Are you going to the beach?)
- *Non, je vais au cinéma. Et toi ?* (No, I'm going to the cinema. And you?)
- *Je rentre de l'école, et je vais au parc.* (I'm coming back from school, and I'm going to the park.)

To choose the right form, ask one question: what would the article be? If you'd say *le cinéma*, it's **au cinéma**. If you'd say *la plage*, it's **à la plage**.

<Callout type="warning" title="À le and de le don't exist">
  The forms *à le*, *à les*, *de le* and *de les* look logical, and they're very common beginner mistakes. In standard French these are always combined: **au**, **aux**, **du**, **des**. Before you finish a sentence, check any *à* or *de* that's followed by *le* or *les*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="You've been saying au since day one">
  You met this contraction long ago without noticing it. *Au revoir* is *à* + *le* + *revoir*: literally "to the seeing again". Whenever you're not sure how *au* works, think of the first goodbye you ever learned. It's the same *au* as in *au cinéma*.
</Callout>
