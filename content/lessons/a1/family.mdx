By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name the people in your family, say whose mother or brother someone is, and tell someone how many brothers, sisters or children you have.

## Your close family

Family words are good nouns to learn first, because their gender is easy: nearly all of them match the person they describe. As you saw in <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on le, la and les" />, it's best to learn each noun with its article.

<VocabTable group="close" />

Notice the accent in *père*, *mère* and *frère*. It's always *è*, the grave accent. *Les parents* looks like the English word, and it's plural, so it takes *les*.

## Children

<VocabTable group="children" />

Be careful with two of these words:

- **La fille** means both "the girl" and "the daughter". *Une fille* usually means "a girl", and *la fille de Paul* means "Paul's daughter".
- **Le fils** looks as if it should rhyme with *fille*, but it doesn't. The l is silent and the final s is pronounced.

*L'enfant* works for boys and girls, so the article shows which you mean when it matters: *un enfant* or *une enfant*. With *l'enfant* you can't tell, because the article shortens before the vowel.

## Grandparents and grandchildren

<VocabTable group="generations" />

These are all written with a hyphen. Look closely at the spelling of the feminine forms: *la grand-mère* doesn't add an -e to *grand*, but *la petite-fille* does add one to *petit*.

## Husbands, wives and partners

<VocabTable group="partners" />

**La femme** means both "the woman" and "the wife", just as *la fille* does two jobs. *La femme de Paul* can only mean "Paul's wife".

For someone you're not married to, you can use *le compagnon* or *la compagne* for a long-term partner. *Le copain* and *la copine* are more casual and common among younger people. They can also just mean a friend, so the context matters.

## Saying whose relative

To say who someone is, combine *c'est* with a family word and **de**. Use the same method for a question: *C'est la sœur de qui ?* (Whose sister is she?).

<Example fr="C'est le père de Julie." en="That's Julie's father." />
<Example fr="C'est la femme de Paul." en="That's Paul's wife." />
<Example fr="C'est la grand-mère d'Anne." en="That's Anne's grandmother." />
<Example fr="Voici les enfants de Marie." en="Here are Marie's children." />

*De* shortens to *d'* before a vowel or silent *h*, just like *je* and *le*.

To talk about your own family, one short phrase will do for now: **J'ai** (I have), followed by *un*, *une* or a number. You'll learn the whole verb in the next lesson.

<Example fr="J'ai un frère et une sœur." en="I have a brother and a sister." />
<Example fr="J'ai deux enfants, un fils et une fille." en="I have two children, a son and a daughter." />

<Callout type="warning" title="French has no 's">
  English says "Paul's sister". There's no way to copy that in French. You turn the phrase around and use *de*: **la sœur de Paul**, literally "the sister of Paul". Word for word, the person comes first and the owner comes second.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Grand goes up, petit goes down">
  A generation above you starts with **grand**: *grand-père*, *grand-mère*. A generation below starts with **petit**: *petit-fils*, *petite-fille*. Picture the family tree: "big" toward the roots, "small" toward the new branches.
</Callout>

## Putting it together

Here's someone talking through a family photo. Every sentence uses a pattern from this lesson.

- *Voici la famille de Julie.* (Here's Julie's family.)
- *C'est le mari de Julie, et ce sont les enfants.* (That's Julie's husband, and those are the children.)
