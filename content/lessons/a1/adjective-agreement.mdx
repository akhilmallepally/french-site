By the end of this lesson you'll be able to make an adjective match the noun it describes, and decide whether it goes before or after that noun.

## Adjectives match their nouns

An adjective is a word that describes a noun. In <LessonLink id="a1-describing-people" text="the lesson on describing people" /> you saw pairs like *grand / grande*. Now here's the system behind them. A French adjective takes its form from the noun it describes: masculine or feminine, singular or plural. This is called **agreement**.

The usual endings are simple. Start from the masculine singular, the form you'll find in a dictionary.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| **Masculine** | grand | grands |
| **Feminine** | grande | grandes |

The plural follows the same logic as nouns in <LessonLink id="a1-plural-nouns" text="the lesson on plural nouns" />, and its -s is just as silent. Add -e for feminine, add -s for plural, and for feminine plural add both.

<Example fr="Mon frère est content." en="My brother is happy." />
<Example fr="Mes sœurs sont contentes." en="My sisters are happy." />

Three more details:

- If the masculine already ends in **-e**, the feminine is the same: *un homme timide*, *une femme timide*.
- If the masculine already ends in **-s** or **-x**, the masculine plural is the same: *il est français*, *ils sont français*.
- A group that includes even one masculine noun takes the **masculine plural**: *Paul et Marie sont contents*.

## Where the adjective goes

Here French is the opposite of English. **Most adjectives go after the noun.** That includes colours, nationalities and most descriptions.

<Example fr="un film français" en="a French film" />
<Example fr="une table rouge et moderne" en="a modern red table" />

A small group of very common adjectives goes **before** the noun. English-speaking learners often remember them as **BAGS**:

- **B**eauty: *beau*, *joli*
- **A**ge: *jeune*, *vieux*, *nouveau*
- **G**oodness: *bon*, *mauvais*
- **S**ize: *grand*, *petit*, *gros*

<Example fr="une jolie robe" en="a pretty dress" />
<Example fr="un gros sac" en="a big bag" />

Agreement still applies: *un petit livre*, *une petite table*. BAGS tells you the position, and the noun tells you the ending. Note, too, that *beau*, *nouveau* and *vieux* have a special form before a masculine noun beginning with a vowel: *un bel ami*, *un nouvel ordinateur*, *un vieil homme*. You can recognize these for now.

## Putting it together

To describe something, ask two questions in order. **Which ending?** Look at the noun: masculine or feminine, singular or plural. **Which side?** If it's a BAGS word, put it before the noun. Otherwise, put it after.

For example, for "the pretty red cars": *voitures* is feminine plural, so you need *jolies* and *rouges*. *Jolies* is a BAGS word, and colours go after: *les jolies voitures rouges*.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't keep the English word order">
  English nearly always puts adjectives first: "a red car", "an interesting film". It's tempting to write *une rouge voiture* or *un intéressant film*. Unless the adjective is one of the BAGS group, it follows the noun: **une voiture rouge**, **un film intéressant**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Adjectives you can't get wrong">
  When you're writing quickly, some adjectives give you less to worry about. Words already ending in -e, such as *jeune*, *timide*, *drôle*, *calme*, *moderne* and *rouge*, stay the same for masculine and feminine, so you only have to think about the plural -s. Reach for them when you're not sure of a harder form.
</Callout>
