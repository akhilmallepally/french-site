By the end of this lesson you'll be able to send a friend a text, make a simple plan, and write a short postcard.

## Short messages have their own style

A text or a postcard is short, so it can be built from a few standard pieces: an opening, one or two ideas, and a closing. What you choose depends on who you're writing to, in the same way that *tu* and *vous* do.

<VocabTable group="closings" />

Use *Coucou*, *Salut*, *Bisous* and *Grosses bises* only with friends and family. For someone you call *vous*, such as a teacher or a landlord, open with *Bonjour* and sign off with *Cordialement*.

*Cher* matches the person you're writing to: **Cher Marc**, **Chère Julie**.

<Example fr="Pas de problème. À samedi ! Bisous" en="No problem. See you on Saturday! Love" />

To end with a day, use **À** with the day: *À samedi !*, *À lundi !* It works just like *À demain !*

## Your turn

Write these two on paper or in a notes app.

1. A text exchange of four to six messages, making a plan for the weekend with a friend. Include a suggestion, a problem and a solution.
2. A postcard from a town you'd like to visit, to someone you know well. Include the weather and one plan.

Read through each one, then ask yourself: does the opening suit the reader, and does the closing match it?

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't send bisous to your landlord">
  In English, it's easy to overlook how friendly a sign-off is. In French, **Bisous** and **Grosses bises** are for people you'd kiss on both cheeks, or close to it. To someone you call *vous*, write **Cordialement**. And check who's receiving your *Cher*: it's **Chère** for a woman, with the accent and the final -e.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read shorthand out loud">
  If you can't work out a French text abbreviation, say it aloud letter by letter or as a sound. *A+* is said *à plus*, meaning "see you later". *Bjr* is *bonjour* with the vowels taken out. Many abbreviations are built from how the words sound, so saying them often reveals the meaning.
</Callout>
