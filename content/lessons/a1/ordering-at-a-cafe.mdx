By the end of this lesson you'll be able to order food and drink in a French café, deal with the usual questions from the staff, and ask for the bill.

## Start with bonjour

A café order starts the way all French encounters do: with a greeting, as in <LessonLink id="a1-greetings" text="the greetings lesson" />. Say **bonjour** when the waiter arrives, and use *vous*. Finish each request with **s'il vous plaît**, and say **merci** when your order comes.

## Saying what you want

Two phrases will do almost everything you need.

<VocabTable group="ordering" />

*Je voudrais* is the polite way of saying what you'd like. *Je vais prendre* means "I'll have", and you'll hear it all the time. Learn both as fixed phrases for now. You'll see how they work in <LessonLink id="a1-modal-verbs" text="the lesson on pouvoir, vouloir and devoir" /> and <LessonLink id="a1-near-future" text="the lesson on the near future" />.

<Example fr="Bonjour, je voudrais un thé, s'il vous plaît." en="Hello, I'd like a tea, please." />
<Example fr="Pour moi, un café crème." en="For me, a coffee with milk." />

## What's on the menu

You know many food and drink words already. Here are some that are particular to cafés.

<VocabTable group="menu" />

The word *café* can cause surprise. **Un café** on its own means a small espresso, and that's what you'll be brought. If you want milk, ask for **un café crème**.

## Un or du?

In a café, you usually order one of something, so you use *un* or *une*: *un café*, *une bière*. If you want an amount of something rather than one portion, use the words from <LessonLink id="a1-partitive-articles" text="the lesson on du, de la and des" />.

<Example fr="Je voudrais de l'eau, s'il vous plaît." en="I'd like some water, please." />
<Example fr="Je vais prendre du pain." en="I'll have some bread." />

The phrases with *un verre de*, *une carafe d'* and *une bouteille d'* use plain **de**, with no *le* or *la*. Learn them as fixed phrases for now.

## What the staff will say

Look out for these phrases, since the server's questions tend to follow the same pattern.

<VocabTable group="staff" />

To get someone's attention, make eye contact, or say **S'il vous plaît !**, or **Monsieur !** or **Madame !**

In France, the bill doesn't usually come until you ask for it. Staff leave you alone so that you can stay at your table as long as you like, and that's considered good service, not bad.

## Putting it together

Here's a complete order. Notice how polite and short each line is.

- *Bonjour, monsieur. Vous désirez ?* (Hello, sir. What would you like?)
- *Bonjour. Je vais prendre un sandwich et un jus d'orange, s'il vous plaît.* (Hello. I'll have a sandwich and an orange juice, please.)
- *Et avec ça ?* (Anything else?)
- *C'est tout, merci.* (That's all, thank you.)

Later:

- *L'addition, s'il vous plaît.* (The bill, please.)
- *Oui, tout de suite.* (Yes, right away.)
- *Merci, bonne journée !* (Thank you, have a good day!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't call out garçon">
  You may have seen it in old films, but calling *Garçon !* to a waiter is outdated and can sound rude. Use **S'il vous plaît !**, or **Monsieur !** or **Madame !** instead. And order with **Je voudrais** or **Je vais prendre**, the forms that staff expect.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask for a carafe">
  If you ask for *de l'eau* at a restaurant, you may be brought a bottle that you'll have to pay for. Ask for **une carafe d'eau** and you'll normally get tap water for free with a meal. It's a perfectly normal thing to order.
</Callout>
