By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say where things and places are: on, under, in front of, behind, between, next to, opposite, near and far.

## Two kinds of preposition

A preposition is a small word that shows where something is. French has two kinds, and the difference matters when you put them before *le* and *les*.

To say where something is, you'll use *est* for one thing and *sont* for several. You've seen them both already.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

## The simple ones

These five go directly before the article, and the article doesn't change.

<VocabTable group="simple" />

<Example fr="Le stylo est sur le livre." en="The pen is on the book." />
<Example fr="Les clés sont sous le journal." en="The keys are under the newspaper." />
<Example fr="L'arrêt de bus est devant la mairie." en="The bus stop is in front of the town hall." />
<Example fr="La librairie est derrière l'église." en="The bookshop is behind the church." />

**Entre** needs two things, joined with *et*: *entre la poste et la gare* (between the post office and the station).

## The ones ending in de

These four all end in **de**. That means they follow the rule from <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" />: *de* melts together with *le* and *les*, and it stays separate from *la* and *l'*.

<VocabTable group="with-de" />

| Before | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| le | du | *à côté du marché* |
| la | de la | *à côté de la mairie* |
| l' | de l' | *à côté de l'hôpital* |
| les | des | *à côté des magasins* |

Use the places from <LessonLink id="a1-places-in-town" text="the lesson on places in town" /> to practise.

<Example fr="La banque est en face de la poste." en="The bank is opposite the post office." />
<Example fr="L'hôtel est près du parc." en="The hotel is near the park." />
<Example fr="Le supermarché est loin de l'école." en="The supermarket is far from the school." />

With a person's or a town's name, there's no article, so nothing changes: *près de Paul*, *loin de Paris*. And *de* becomes *d'* before a vowel: *près d'Orléans*.

## Asking where

To ask where something is, the simplest question is **Où est… ?** For more than one thing, say **Où sont… ?** You can answer with just the place, which is what most people do.

- *Où est la pharmacie ?* — *À côté de la banque.* (Where's the chemist's? — Next to the bank.)
- *Où sont les clés ?* — *Sur la table.* (Where are the keys? — On the table.)

## Opposites

Several of these prepositions come in natural pairs of opposites, so it helps to look at them side by side.

- *sur* and *sous*
- *devant* and *derrière*
- *près de* and *loin de*

Be careful with *sur* and *sous*. They look similar but mean the opposite, and the vowel is different. For *sur*, your lips are rounded and pushed forward. For *sous*, the sound is the same as in *vous*.

## Putting it together

Here's someone explaining the layout of a small town square.

- *La boulangerie est à côté de la pharmacie.* (The bakery is next to the chemist's.)
- *La mairie est entre la poste et l'église.* (The town hall is between the post office and the church.)
- *Le parking est derrière la mairie.* (The car park is behind the town hall.)
- *Et l'arrêt de bus est en face du marché.* (And the bus stop is opposite the market.)

<Callout type="warning" title="De only goes with its own group">
  English prepositions don't give you a clue here. In French, only *à côté de*, *en face de*, *près de* and *loin de* take **de**. It's **devant la gare**, not *devant de la gare*, and **sur la table**, not *sur de la table*. If the preposition isn't in the *de* group, the article follows it directly.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Près de gets you there">
  When you can't remember exactly how two places are arranged, you don't need the perfect word. *Près de* (near) will always give your listener the general idea. It's better to say *La banque est près de la gare* than to stop in the middle of a sentence hunting for *en face de*. You can be more exact when the words come to you.
</Callout>
