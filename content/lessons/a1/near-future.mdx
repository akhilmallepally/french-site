By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about your plans: what you're going to do tomorrow, this weekend and next week.

## Going to do something

English has an easy way to talk about plans: "I'm going to visit Paris". French does exactly the same thing. Take the present of *aller*, which you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-faire-and-aller" text="the lesson on faire and aller" />, and add a verb in the infinitive. That's the **near future**.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

Only *aller* changes to match the subject. The second verb stays in its infinitive form, the one ending in -er, -ir or -re, however many people are involved.

<Example fr="Je vais visiter Bordeaux." en="I'm going to visit Bordeaux." />
<Example fr="Elle va arriver bientôt." en="She's going to arrive soon." />
<Example fr="Nous allons faire la cuisine." en="We're going to do the cooking." />
<Example fr="Ils vont chercher Marc." en="They're going to look for Marc." />

*Aller* now has two jobs. On its own, with a place, it means going somewhere: *Je vais à Lille*. With an infinitive, it talks about the future: *Je vais travailler*. Both can appear in one sentence: *Je vais aller à Lille* (I'm going to go to Lille).

## When is it happening?

The near future is usually joined by a word that says when. These are the most useful, and they often go at the beginning or end of the sentence.

<VocabTable group="time" />

You can add any day or date from <LessonLink id="a1-days-months-dates" text="the lesson on days, months and dates" />: *vendredi*, *en août*, *le 3 mai*.

<Example fr="Demain, je vais travailler à Rennes." en="Tomorrow I'm going to work in Rennes." />
<Example fr="On va faire les courses ce soir." en="We're going to do the shopping tonight." />

*Prochain* is an adjective, and it matches its noun: *lundi prochain*, but *la semaine prochaine*.

You can also use the near future to say what's about to happen soon, or to talk about plans further ahead. It's very common in everyday speech. French has another future tense, which you'll meet later on, but this one will carry you a long way.

## Making it negative

Put *ne…pas* around **aller**, and leave the infinitive outside it, at the end.

<Example fr="Je ne vais pas regarder le match." en="I'm not going to watch the match." />
<Example fr="Elles ne vont pas travailler lundi." en="They're not going to work on Monday." />

The *ne* shortens to *n'* before *allons* and *allez*: *Nous n'allons pas visiter Nice*.

## Asking about plans

To ask what someone is going to do, you can use the phrase below. For a yes/no question, simply raise your voice at the end, as usual: *Tu vas travailler demain ?*

## Putting it together

Here are two friends talking about the weekend.

- *Qu'est-ce que tu vas faire ce week-end ?* (What are you going to do this weekend?)
- *Samedi, je vais visiter Dijon.* (On Saturday, I'm going to visit Dijon.)
- *Et dimanche ?* (And Sunday?)
- *Dimanche, je ne vais pas travailler. Je vais faire la cuisine !* (On Sunday, I'm not going to work. I'm going to cook!)

Notice that *samedi* and *dimanche* have no *le*, because they mean this coming Saturday and Sunday, and not every week.

<Callout type="warning" title="Nothing between aller and the verb">
  English needs "to" in "going *to* visit", so it's tempting to add a word in French: *Je vais à visiter*. There's nothing there in French: **Je vais visiter**. And only *aller* changes, so don't conjugate the second verb: it's **Elle va parler**, and not *Elle va parle*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A future with nothing new to learn">
  You already know all the pieces. If you can say *je vais* and you know a verb's infinitive, you can talk about the future with no new endings to memorize. Every infinitive you've learned so far is now also a plan: *voyager*, *cuisiner*, *finir*, *attendre*.
</Callout>
