By the end of this lesson you'll be able to make French nouns plural, spot the ones that change in a special way, and know why two forms that look different can sound exactly alike.

## The basic rule: add -s

Most French nouns form the plural just as most English nouns do, by adding **-s**. The article changes too, as you saw with *les* and with *des* in <LessonLink id="a1-indefinite-articles" text="the lesson on un, une and des" />.

<VocabTable group="regular" />

Here's the big difference from English: **the plural -s is silent**. *Livre* and *livres* sound identical. *Les tables* differs from *la table* only in the sound of the article.

When the next word begins with a vowel or silent *h*, the s of *les* and *des* links to it with a z sound, as in *les amis*. That's the s of the article. The s at the end of the noun normally stays quiet.

<Example fr="un livre, deux livres" en="one book, two books" />
<Example fr="Voici les amis de Paul." en="Here are Paul's friends." />

## Nouns that don't change

If a noun already ends in **-s**, **-x** or **-z** in the singular, the plural looks just the same. Only the article shows you there's more than one.

<VocabTable group="same" />

<Example fr="Ce sont des bus." en="They're buses." />

## Nouns that take -x

Nouns ending in **-eau**, **-au** or **-eu** usually add **-x** instead of -s. This x is silent too.

<VocabTable group="x" />

There are a few exceptions, such as *le pneu* (the tyre), which becomes *les pneus*. Most nouns ending in *-ou* add a normal -s, but a small group take -x, such as *le bijou* (the jewel), which becomes *les bijoux*. You can pick these up as you meet them.

<Example fr="Ce sont des gâteaux." en="They're cakes." />

## Nouns in -al become -aux

Most nouns ending in **-al** change that ending to **-aux** in the plural. This is one of the rare plurals you can actually hear, because the end of the word changes sound.

<VocabTable group="aux" />

<Example fr="Voici les journaux." en="Here are the newspapers." />

A few common -al nouns don't follow the pattern and simply add -s, such as *le festival*, which becomes *les festivals*.

One noun is a complete oddity, and you'll need it soon for describing people: *l'œil* (the eye) becomes *les yeux* (the eyes).

## Choosing the right ending

When you need a plural, run through these questions in order:

1. Does the noun already end in -s, -x or -z? Change nothing.
2. Does it end in -al? It's probably -aux.
3. Does it end in -eau, -au or -eu? It's probably -x.
4. Anything else? Add -s.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't sound out the plural s">
  English speakers are used to hearing the plural: "book" and "books" sound different. It's natural to pronounce the s in *livres*, but it should stay silent. The listener knows it's plural from the small word in front, so make that vowel clear: *le* and *les* are different sounds.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Spot the article, check the ending">
  Plural endings are the easiest thing to forget when you write, because you can't hear them. When you read over your French, stop at every *les*, *des* or number above *un*, then look at the noun that follows. Does it have its -s or -x? This takes seconds and catches most slips.
</Callout>
