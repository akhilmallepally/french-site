By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name the three daily meals, and talk about everyday foods, drinks, fruit and vegetables.

## Meals

French has a word for each meal of the day. Here they're nouns, so each one has its article.

<VocabTable group="meals" />

*Le petit déjeuner* is literally "the little lunch". You can learn more about when and how these meals are eaten in <LessonLink id="a1-culture-french-meals" text="the lesson on French meals and mealtimes" />.

## Everyday foods

As you saw in <LessonLink id="a1-indefinite-articles" text="the lesson on un, une and des" />, a French noun usually needs a small word in front of it. These tables list each food with *le*, *la*, *l'* or *les*. Learn them that way, because the article shows you the gender, except with *l'*, where a note tells you.

<VocabTable group="foods" />

Two of these are worth a second look. *Les pâtes* is always plural, unlike "pasta" in English. *L'œuf* has a pronounced f in the singular, but in *les œufs* the f and the s are both silent.

<Example fr="C'est un œuf." en="It's an egg." />
<Example fr="Le poulet et le riz, c'est le dîner." en="Chicken and rice, that's dinner." />

<Example fr="Voici une banane et une fraise." en="Here's a banana and a strawberry." />

## Vegetables

<VocabTable group="vegetables" />

*La salade* can mean a green lettuce as well as a mixed salad. You'll usually be able to tell from the context.

*Le haricot* is worth noticing, too. Most words that begin with *h* shorten the article, as in *l'hôtel*. This one doesn't. A small group of French words behave this way, and you learn them one at a time.

<Example fr="C'est la soupe de carottes." en="It's the carrot soup." />
<Example fr="Une tomate, un oignon et une pomme de terre." en="A tomato, an onion and a potato." />

## Putting it together

These lines use only what you've learned so far. Imagine someone unpacking the shopping:

- *Voici le lait.* (Here's the milk.)
- *Et le beurre ?* (And the butter?)
- *Voici le beurre, et voici une pomme.* (Here's the butter, and here's an apple.)

To say that you're eating "some bread" or drinking "some water", French needs a different set of small words. They're the subject of <LessonLink id="a1-partitive-articles" text="the lesson on du, de la and des" />, so for now, stick to naming things with *le*, *la*, *un* and *une*.

The names of foods are also a great place to practise your spelling. Many of them have accents or double letters that matter, so take a moment to look carefully at *pâtes*, *bière*, *pomme* and *carotte* before you move on.

<Callout type="warning" title="Fish or poison?">
  A double s and a single s can change the meaning completely. **Le poisson**, with *ss*, has an s sound, and it's fish. **Le poison**, with one *s*, has a z sound, and it's poison. Look closely when you write it, and take care when you say it aloud. The s sound is the one you want at dinner.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A word can be a little picture">
  Some French food words are small pictures. *La pomme de terre* is an "apple of the earth", and *le petit déjeuner* is a "little lunch". When a word is made of parts you know, translate it literally in your head. A strange image sticks to your memory far better than a plain list does.
</Callout>
