By the end of this lesson you'll be able to stop someone in the street, ask how to get somewhere, and understand a simple set of directions.

## Starting politely

As with all French encounters, you start with a polite greeting. Say **Excusez-moi**, then *madame* or *monsieur*, and use *vous*. It helps to say *bonjour* as well.

## Asking the way

There are several ways of asking. These all do the same job.

<VocabTable group="asking" />

**Pour aller à… ?** is the phrase you'll use most. Remember that *à* melts together with *le* and *les*: *Pour aller au musée ?*, *Pour aller à la gare ?*, *Pour aller à l'hôtel ?*

You can also use *où* from <LessonLink id="a1-question-words" text="the lesson on question words" />, along with what you've learned in <LessonLink id="a1-prepositions-of-place" text="the lesson on prepositions of place" />.

<Example fr="Excusez-moi, madame. Pour aller à la mairie, s'il vous plaît ?" en="Excuse me, madam. How do I get to the town hall, please?" />
<Example fr="Bonjour, monsieur. Où est la station de métro ?" en="Hello, sir. Where's the metro station?" />
<Example fr="Il y a une pharmacie près d'ici ?" en="Is there a chemist's near here?" />

## Understanding the answer

The answers follow a pattern, and most directions use only a few phrases.

<VocabTable group="directions" />

These instructions use the *vous* form of the verb without a subject, which is how French gives instructions. You'll learn how that works in <LessonLink id="a1-imperative" text="the lesson on the imperative" />. For now, learn these as fixed phrases, so you recognize them when you hear them.

Directions often mention landmarks too.

<VocabTable group="landmarks" />

*Le feu* is literally "the fire", and it's a single word for a set of traffic lights.

<Example fr="Allez tout droit, et au feu, tournez à droite." en="Go straight on, and at the traffic lights, turn right." />
<Example fr="Au carrefour, prenez la deuxième rue à gauche." en="At the crossroads, take the second street on the left." />
<Example fr="La poste est au bout de la rue, en face de l'église." en="The post office is at the end of the street, opposite the church." />

## Is it far?

Before you set off, it's worth asking how far it is. You can use **C'est loin ?** The answer may be **C'est tout près** or a number of minutes.

<Example fr="C'est loin ? — Non, c'est à cinq minutes à pied." en="Is it far? — No, it's five minutes on foot." />

## Putting it together

Here's a complete exchange in the street.

- *Excusez-moi, monsieur. Pour aller à la gare, s'il vous plaît ?* (Excuse me, sir. How do I get to the station, please?)
- *Allez tout droit, et prenez la deuxième rue à gauche.* (Go straight on, and take the second street on the left.)
- *C'est loin ?* (Is it far?)
- *Non, c'est à dix minutes à pied.* (No, it's ten minutes on foot.)
- *Merci beaucoup, monsieur. Bonne journée !* (Thank you very much, sir. Have a good day!)

If you didn't understand all of it, it's fine to ask for it again. You can say *Pardon ?* or *Comment ?*, as you learned with the question words, and the speaker will usually repeat it more slowly.

Don't worry if you only catch a few words. Listen for *gauche*, *droite* and *tout droit*, and follow those one at a time. When you get to the next corner, you can always ask someone else.

<Callout type="warning" title="Tout droit isn't à droite">
  These two sound alike and look alike, but they send you in different directions. **Tout droit** means "straight on". **À droite** means "to the right". The difference is in the final sound: you can hear a t in *droite*, but *droit* ends in a vowel sound. If you're not sure which you heard, it's worth checking.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Say it back">
  When someone gives you directions, repeat the key words back as a question: *À gauche ? Et tout droit ?* The other person will nod or correct you straight away. It takes a few seconds, it confirms that you understood, and it's much better than finding out three streets later that you didn't.
</Callout>
