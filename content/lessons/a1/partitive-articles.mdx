By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say "some bread" and "some water", choose between *le pain* and *du pain*, and say you don't have or eat something.

## Some of something

In <LessonLink id="a1-food-and-drink" text="the lesson on food and drink" /> you learned the names of foods with *le*, *la* and *les*. Now you need to say that you're eating **some** of them. French has a set of words for this called **partitive articles**, and they mean "some", an amount you aren't specifying.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

The forms look just like the ones in <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" />: *de* + *le* melts into *du*, and *de la* and *de l'* stay as they are. Here, though, they don't mean "of the". They mean "some".

English can often leave out "some": "I eat bread", "There's water". French can't. When you mean an amount of something, the word has to be there.

These are the verbs and phrases you'll use most with partitives.

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

<Example fr="Je mange du fromage." en="I eat cheese. / I'm eating some cheese." />
<Example fr="Il y a de la bière." en="There's some beer." />
<Example fr="J'ai de l'eau." en="I have some water." />
<Example fr="Nous mangeons des fraises." en="We're eating some strawberries." />

*Des* is the same word as the plural of *un* and *une*, so you've used it already.

## Le pain or du pain?

This is the key idea in this lesson. The two sets of articles do different jobs.

- **Le, la, les** talk about a thing **in general**, or a particular one. *Le pain, c'est bon.* (Bread is good.)
- **Du, de la, des** talk about **some** of it, an amount. *Je mange du pain.* (I'm eating some bread.)

So verbs of liking normally take *le*, *la* or *les*, because you like bread as a whole. That's what you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-likes-and-dislikes" text="the lesson on likes and dislikes" />. But *manger*, *il y a* and *j'ai* normally take *du*, *de la* or *des*, because you're dealing with an amount.

Compare the two:

<Example fr="J'aime le poulet, et je mange du poulet." en="I like chicken, and I eat chicken." />

If you do mean a particular one, the definite article is right after *manger* as well: *Je mange le gâteau de Marie* (I'm eating Marie's cake).

## After a negative verb

You know from <LessonLink id="a1-negation-ne-pas" text="the lesson on negation" /> that *un*, *une* and *des* change to **de** after a negative verb. The partitives do the same. *Du*, *de la* and *de l'* all become **de**, or **d'** before a vowel or silent *h*.

- *Je mange du pain.* → *Je ne mange pas de pain.*
- *Il y a de la soupe.* → *Il n'y a pas de soupe.*
- *J'ai de l'eau.* → *Je n'ai pas d'eau.*

*Le*, *la* and *les* don't change, so verbs of liking keep theirs: *Je n'aime pas le café*. The partitive also stays after *être*, just as *un* and *une* do: *Ce n'est pas du vin*.

<Example fr="Il n'y a pas de lait." en="There's no milk." />

## Putting it together

Here's a short exchange in a kitchen.

- *Il y a du pain ?* (Is there any bread?)
- *Oui, et il y a du beurre.* (Yes, and there's some butter.)
- *Et de l'eau ?* (And any water?)
- *Non, il n'y a pas d'eau.* (No, there's no water.)

<Callout type="warning" title="You like le pain, not du pain">
  Once you've learned *du*, it's tempting to use it everywhere, including after *aimer*. Keep them apart: **J'aime le fromage**, and **Je mange du fromage**. *J'aime du fromage* doesn't work for saying you like cheese in general. The verb tells you which set of articles to reach for.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Try the word 'some'">
  When you can't decide between *le* and *du*, put the sentence into English and add the word "some". "I'm eating some bread" works, so it's *du pain*. "I like some bread" sounds wrong if you mean bread in general, so it's *le pain*. It's a quick test that gets most everyday sentences right.
</Callout>
