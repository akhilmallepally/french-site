By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use *être* (to be) with every subject pronoun to say who you are, where you are, and how you feel. You'll also be able to use *c'est* to point things and people out.

## The most useful verb in French

*Être* means "to be". It's the most common verb in the language, and like "to be" in English, it's irregular: its forms don't follow a pattern, so you learn them by heart. You'll use the pronouns from <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the subject pronouns lesson" /> with every form.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

A few things to notice:

- The verb forms **es** and **est** sound the same, because both have a silent final consonant. As phrases, *tu es* and *il est* still sound different because of the pronoun.
- **Ils sont** and **elles sont** sound like *il* and *elle* plus *sont*: the s of *ils* and *elles* is silent before a consonant.
- **Vous êtes** links: the normally silent s of *vous* is pronounced, with a z sound, because *êtes* starts with a vowel. This linking is called *liaison*.
- **On est** uses the *il/elle* form, even though it means "we".

## What you can say with être

**Who you are.** In <LessonLink id="a1-introducing-yourself" text="the introductions lesson" /> you saw that *Je suis Paul* is another way to give your name. *Être* also gives your job or status. Notice there's no word for "a": *Je suis étudiant* (I'm a student), not *je suis un étudiant*.

**Where you are.** *Je suis à Paris* (I'm in Paris). *Tu es où ?* (Where are you?) is very common in texts and on the phone.

**How you are.** *Je suis fatigué* (I'm tired), *Je suis content* (I'm happy). Describing words like these add an -e in writing when they describe a woman: *Elle est fatiguée*. You'll see why in later lessons.

## C'est

**C'est** means "it is", "this is" or "that's". It's *ce* (this, that, it) plus *est*. You use it to point out or identify a person or thing: *C'est Paul* (That's Paul), *C'est Paris !* (This is Paris!), *C'est ici* (It's here).

The apostrophe is another case of elision, which you met with *je* → *j'*. Small words like *ce* and *je* drop their final e before a vowel: *ce* + *est* becomes *c'est*, and *je* + *habite* becomes *j'habite*. With *je suis* nothing changes, because *suis* starts with a consonant.

*C'est* is also how French reacts to things: *C'est bon !* (That's good! / That's fine!), *C'est super !* (That's great!), *C'est loin ?* (Is it far?). In these quick comments the describing word stays in its basic form, so you don't need to worry about adding an -e.

## Putting it together

Here's a short exchange at a language class, using several forms of *être*. Notice how *tu es* and *vous êtes* follow the same tu/vous rules you already know: the two students use *tu* with each other, and *vous* with the teacher.

- *Salut, tu es étudiante ici ?* (Hi, are you a student here?)
- *Oui ! Et toi ?* (Yes! And you?)
- *Moi aussi. On est en retard, non ?* (Me too. We're late, aren't we?)
- *Bonjour monsieur. Vous êtes le professeur ?* (Hello, sir. Are you the teacher?)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't use être for age">
  English says "I *am* twenty". French says "I *have* twenty years": **J'ai vingt ans**, using *avoir* (to have). *Je suis vingt ans* is one of the most common beginner mistakes. The same goes for being hungry or cold, which also use *avoir* in French. You'll learn it in <LessonLink id="a1-avoir-present" text="the lesson on avoir" />.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A hidden s in êtes">
  Remember the circumflex from <LessonLink id="a1-alphabet-and-accents" text="the alphabet lesson" />, the one that often marks a lost s? *Êtes* is an example: older French wrote it *estes*. If you can picture the s, you'll remember the accent.
</Callout>
