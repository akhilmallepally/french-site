By the end of this lesson you'll know all nine French subject pronouns (the words for I, you, he, she, it, we and they) and you'll be able to choose confidently between *tu* and *vous*.

## What a subject pronoun does

A subject pronoun stands in for the person or thing doing the action. In "**She** lives in Paris", *she* is the subject pronoun. You've already used two French ones without noticing: *je* in *je m'appelle*, and *tu* in *tu t'appelles comment ?* from <LessonLink id="a1-introducing-yourself" />.

<VocabTable />

## Je: I

*Je* is never written with a capital letter unless it starts a sentence. Before a vowel or a silent h, it shortens to **j'**: *j'habite* (I live), *j'aime* (I like). This shortening is called *elision*, and French does it with several short words.

## Tu or vous: you

English has one "you". French has two, and choosing between them is part of being polite.

**Tu** is for one person you know well: friends, family, children, classmates, and people your own age in relaxed settings.

**Vous** has two jobs:
1. The **polite** "you" for one person: strangers, older people, shop staff, your doctor, and anyone in a professional setting.
2. The **plural** "you" for more than one person, whoever they are. Talking to two close friends at once? That's *vous*.

This is why you learned both *Et toi ?* and *Et vous ?* in the last lesson.

## Il and elle: he, she, and it

*Il* means "he" and *elle* means "she". But French has no separate word for "it". Every noun in French is either masculine or feminine (you'll learn how to tell in <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" />), and you use *il* for masculine things and *elle* for feminine ones. A table (*la table*, feminine) is *elle*. A book (*le livre*, masculine) is *il*.

## Ils and elles: they

*Ils* is for a group of men, a group of masculine things, or any **mixed** group. *Elles* is only for a group that is entirely women or entirely feminine things. One man in a group of ten is enough to make it *ils*.

In most sentences *ils* sounds exactly like *il*, and *elles* like *elle*. You'll tell them apart from the verb and the context.

## On: the everyday "we"

*On* originally means "one" or "people in general": *On parle français ici* is "French is spoken here". In everyday speech, though, French speakers use *on* far more often than *nous* to mean "we". *Nous* is still correct, and you'll see it in writing and formal speech, but *on* is what you'll hear most among friends.

One useful detail: *on* always takes the same verb form as *il* and *elle*, even when it means "we".

<Example fr="Vous vous appelez comment, monsieur ?" en="What's your name, sir? (polite)" />
<Example fr="Salut, vous deux !" en="Hi, you two! (vous for more than one person)" />

<Callout type="warning" title="Using tu with strangers">
  Because English has only one "you", English speakers tend to pick *tu* and stick with it. Using *tu* with a shopkeeper, a waiter, or someone older you've just met can sound rude or overly familiar. Start with **vous** with adults you don't know. Also stay consistent within a conversation: *Tu t'appelles comment ? Et vous ?* mixes the two.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Let them offer tu">
  French even has verbs for this: **tutoyer** (to use *tu*) and **vouvoyer** (to use *vous*). Moving from *vous* to *tu* is a small social step, and it's usually offered by the older or more senior person: *On peut se tutoyer ?* ("Shall we use tu?"). Young people often use *tu* with each other straight away. *Tu* is used more freely in Québec.
</Callout>
