By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say the French alphabet, recognise the five accents and what they do, spell a word or a name aloud, and read the pronunciation hints used throughout this course.

## Why learn the alphabet?

Spelling comes up all the time in real life. You'll spell your name at a hotel desk, give your email address, or ask how a word you've just heard is written. French letters look the same as English ones, but most of their *names* are different, and a few are genuinely confusing.

## The letters

<VocabTable group="letters" />

Most names are easy if you read the hints carefully. The ones that trip people up are the vowels and two consonants:

- **E** and **I** are easy to swap. The French E is close to the a in "about", and the French I is close to the ee in "see".
- **G** and **J** are swapped compared with English. That's the big one; see the warning below.
- **H** has a name of its own, but inside words it's almost always silent: *hôtel* starts with an o sound.
- **U** is a sound English doesn't have. Start with the vowel of "see", keep your tongue where it is, and round your lips as if to whistle.
- **W** is "double V" and **Y** is "Greek I". They're rare in native French words.

## The accents

French has five accent marks. They aren't decoration: an accent is part of the spelling, and leaving one out is a spelling mistake.

<VocabTable group="accents" />

**Accent aigu (é).** Only ever on e. It gives a sound like the vowel in "day", but shorter and without the glide at the end: *café*, *été* (summer), *étudiant* (student).

**Accent grave (è, à, ù).** On e, it gives an open sound like the e in "bed": *très* (very), *mère* (mother). On a and u it usually doesn't change the sound at all. It tells two words apart instead: *a* (has) and *à* (at, to), *ou* (or) and *où* (where).

**Accent circonflexe (ê, â, î, ô, û).** It can sit on any vowel. Very often it marks a spot where an s used to be written, and English sometimes kept that s: *forêt* (forest), *hôpital* (hospital), *fête* (feast). Spotting this can help you guess a word's meaning.

**Cédille (ç).** Only under c, and only before a, o or u. A plain c before those letters sounds like k (*café*). The cédille makes it an s sound: *ça* (that), *garçon* (boy), *français* (French).

**Tréma (ë, ï).** Two dots telling you to pronounce the vowel separately instead of blending it with the one before: *Noël* (Christmas), *naïf* (naive).

## Spelling aloud

To spell a word, say each letter's name. For accents, say the letter and then the accent: "e accent aigu", "c cédille". For a double letter, French counts: *deux L*, *deux S*.

<Callout type="warning" title="G and J are the wrong way round">
  The French name for **G** (*gé*) sounds like the English name for J, and the French name for **J** (*ji*) sounds like the English name for G. Mixing them up is the most common spelling mistake English speakers make, and it's easy to miss because each name sounds like a real English letter. Practise names that have both letters, like *Georges* or *Jérôme*.
</Callout>

## How to read the pronunciation hints

Every vocabulary table in this course has a pronunciation column. The hints use English spelling with a few conventions:

- **CAPITALS** mark the syllable with a little more weight. In French that's normally the *last* syllable of a word or short phrase, not the first as in many English words.
- **zh** is the sound in the middle of "measure".
- **uh** is the soft sound at the start of "about".
- **ew** is the French u: say "ee" with rounded lips.
- **ahn, ohn, an, uhn**: when a vowel is followed by n in a hint, the vowel is usually *nasal*. Let the sound go through your nose and don't fully close the n.
- **r** is the French r, made at the back of the throat, close to a light gargle.

The hints are a starting point, not an exact recording. They work best alongside the spelling, which you'll learn to read more and more directly.

<Callout type="tip" title="Spelling over the phone">
  When a line is bad, French speakers often spell with names: *B comme Bernard, D comme Daniel*. If someone does this to you, they're not being strange. They're making sure B and D don't get confused. You can do it too.
</Callout>
