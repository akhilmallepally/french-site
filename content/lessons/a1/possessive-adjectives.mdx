By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say "my", "your", "his", "her", "our" and "their" in French, talk about your family, and avoid the mistake English speakers make most often with *son* and *sa*.

## One word for each kind of noun

So far you've said whose relative someone is with *de*, in <LessonLink id="a1-family" text="the family lesson" />: *la sœur de Paul*. Possessive adjectives give you a shorter way. Like *le*, *la* and *les*, they change to match the noun that comes after them.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

Read the table across. Each row is one owner, and the form depends on the noun:

- *mon père* (masculine), *ma mère* (feminine), *mes parents* (plural)
- *ton frère*, *ta sœur*, *tes enfants*
- *son fils*, *sa fille*, *ses grands-parents*

The bottom three rows are simpler, because they have only one singular form for both genders: *notre père*, *notre mère*, *nos parents*.

The plural forms all work like the plural article from <LessonLink id="a1-plural-nouns" text="the lesson on plural nouns" />. The noun still takes its -s or -x: *mes livres*, *nos gâteaux*.

In the sentences below and in the practice, *avec* means "with".

<Example fr="C'est mon frère." en="That's my brother." />
<Example fr="Tu es avec ta mère ?" en="Are you with your mother?" />
<Example fr="Ce sont nos enfants." en="They're our children." />
<Example fr="Madame, voici votre clé." en="Here's your key, madam." />

## Your: ton or votre?

French has two sets of words for "your", and they follow the tu/vous choice you already know. Use **ton, ta, tes** with someone you call *tu*. Use **votre, vos** with someone you call *vous*, or with more than one person.

Keep the choice consistent. If you've said *Vous êtes*, it should be *votre mari*, not *ton mari*.

## Mon before a vowel

*Ma*, *ta* and *sa* can't sit directly before a vowel or silent *h*. Instead of shortening, as *la* does, they switch to **mon, ton, son**, even though the noun is feminine.

<VocabTable group="vowels" />

<Example fr="C'est mon amie Claire." en="That's my friend Claire." />

The noun stays feminine, so anything else that describes it will still be feminine. The n of *mon* links to the vowel. The final s of *mes*, *tes*, *ses*, *nos*, *vos* and *leurs* also links, with a z sound: *mes amis*, *leurs enfants*.

## Leur or leurs?

*Leur* goes with one thing that a group shares, and *leurs* goes with several. The two sound the same.

- *Les enfants sont avec leur mère.* (one mother)
- *Les enfants sont avec leurs parents.* (two parents)

Don't confuse *leurs* with *ses*. *Ses* means that one person has several: *Paul et ses enfants*. *Leurs* means that several people have several: *Paul et Marie et leurs enfants*.

## If it isn't clear

Because *son* and *sa* can mean "his" or "her", a sentence may be unclear. Usually the context settles it. If it doesn't, name the person with *de*: *la mère de Paul*, *la mère de Marie*.

<Callout type="warning" title="The noun chooses, not the owner">
  In English, the owner picks the word: "his" for a man, "her" for a woman. In French, the **noun** picks it. Marie's brother is **son frère**, and Paul's sister is **sa sœur**. It's tempting to say *sa frère* about a woman, but the owner's gender plays no part. Look only at the word that follows.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Move one person around the table">
  Choose one real person from your family and say the same relationship from different points of view. *Ma sœur* is *ta sœur* if you're talking to your brother, *notre sœur* when you're together, and *leur sœur* for your other siblings. Moving one person around the table practises all the forms at once.
</Callout>
