By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say "a", "an" and "some" in French, choose between *un livre* and *le livre*, and name the things around you with *c'est un…* and *ce sont des…*.

## Three words for "a" and "some"

In <LessonLink id="a1-definite-articles" text="the lesson on le, la and les" /> you learned how to say "the". The words in this lesson do a different job. They introduce something without saying exactly which one.

<VocabTable group="articles" />

As with *le* and *la*, the choice between *un* and *une* depends on the gender of the noun. *Des* is the plural for both genders: *des livres*, *des tables*.

Two details are different from *le* and *la*:

- **Un and une never shorten.** You write *l'ami* but **un ami**, and *l'école* but **une école**.
- **The n of un links to a vowel.** On its own, *un* ends in a nasal sound. Before a vowel, as in *un ami*, you also hear an n that joins the two words. The s of *des* links in the same way, with a z sound: *des amis*.

## A book, or the book?

French and English mostly agree on this. Use *un*, *une* or *des* for any one of something, or something you're mentioning for the first time. Use *le*, *la* or *les* when you and your listener know exactly which one you mean.

- *Voici un stylo.* (Here's a pen. Any pen, new to the conversation.)
- *Le stylo est ici.* (The pen is here. The one we were just talking about.)
- *C'est le sac de Paul.* (It's Paul's bag. A particular bag.)

<Example fr="Voici une clé." en="Here's a key." />
<Example fr="La clé est ici." en="The key is here." />

## Naming things

Now you can point at something and say what it is. Use **c'est** for one thing and **ce sont** for several. *C'est* comes from <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="the lesson on être" />, and *sont* is the *ils/elles* form of the same verb.

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

<Example fr="Qu'est-ce que c'est ? — C'est un ordinateur." en="What is it? — It's a computer." />
<Example fr="C'est une maison." en="It's a house." />
<Example fr="Ce sont des chaises." en="They're chairs." />
<Example fr="C'est un ami." en="He's a friend." />

In relaxed conversation you'll also hear *c'est des livres*. It's fine to recognize, but use *ce sont des livres* when you write.

You may remember that *être* gives a job or status with no article: *Je suis étudiant*. After *c'est*, the article comes back: **C'est un étudiant** (He's a student).

<VocabTable group="objects" />

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't drop des">
  English often uses no word at all before a plural: "They're books." It's tempting to write *Ce sont livres*, but a French noun nearly always needs something in front of it. When English has no article and you mean "some", French uses **des**: *Ce sont des livres*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="A word that's also a number">
  *Un* and *une* also mean "one". So *un livre* can mean "a book" or "one book", and French doesn't need a separate word. When you count, you already use it: *un*, *deux*, *trois*. If you're asked how many, *une clé* is a complete answer.
</Callout>

Now try it for real: look around the room, point at three things, and name each one with *c'est un…* or *c'est une…*. If you don't know the gender, look it up and say the whole phrase again.
