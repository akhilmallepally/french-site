By the end of this lesson you'll know how to walk into a French shop and leave it politely, how to address people with *monsieur* and *madame*, what to do when someone leans in for *la bise*, and when it's time to move from *vous* to *tu*.

## Why this matters

In <LessonLink id="a1-greetings" text="the greetings lesson" /> you learned the words. This lesson is about how they're used. In France, polite routines aren't optional extras. They're how you show respect to someone before you ask them for anything. Getting them right will change how people respond to you, even if your French is still basic.

## Monsieur and madame

The polite greeting in France is often *bonjour* plus a title: *Bonjour, monsieur* or *Bonjour, madame*. You don't need a surname. In English, "Hello, sir" can sound stiff or very formal, but in French it's just ordinary courtesy.

<VocabTable group="titles" />

With a surname, it's *Madame Martin* or *Monsieur Dubois*, shortened in writing to *Mme Martin* and *M. Dubois*. *Mademoiselle* was once used for unmarried women, but it's now old-fashioned for adults, and French government forms dropped it in 2012. Use *madame* for any adult woman.

## The shop routine

Small French shops have a little script, and nearly everyone follows it.

1. **Coming in:** say *Bonjour, madame* (or *monsieur*), even if you're only looking. In the evening it's *Bonsoir*.
2. **Asking:** start with *s'il vous plaît* or *excusez-moi* if you need to get someone's attention.
3. **Leaving:** say *Merci, bonne journée !* or *Merci, au revoir !*, even if you didn't buy anything.

The same routine applies at the pharmacy, at the doctor's, on a small bus, and when you enter a waiting room or a lift with a few other people.

<VocabTable group="shops" />

When someone wishes you a good day, *À vous aussi !* is the easy answer. When someone thanks you, say *De rien*, or the more formal *Je vous en prie*.

## La bise

*La bise* is a greeting where two people touch cheeks and make a small kissing sound. You don't actually kiss the other person's face.

It's for friends, family, and friends of friends at a casual get-together. Colleagues in some workplaces do it too. Women often greet each other and men this way, while men more often shake hands unless they're close friends or relatives. How many kisses depends on where you are: two is the most common, but some regions do three or even four. If you're not sure, follow the other person's lead.

In formal situations, and when you first meet someone in a professional setting, shake hands instead.

## From vous to tu

You saw how *tu* and *vous* work in <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the subject pronouns lesson" />. In practice, start with *vous* with adults you don't know. Young people often use *tu* with each other straight away, and some workplaces use *tu* from the first day, while others keep *vous* for years.

Don't switch without an invitation. The change is usually marked by a simple question: *On se tutoie ?* Once you've moved to *tu* with someone, you normally stay there.

<Example fr="Merci, bonne journée ! — À vous aussi !" en="Thank you, have a good day! — You too!" />
<Example fr="Excusez-moi, monsieur." en="Excuse me, sir." />

<Example fr="Bonjour, Madame Martin." en="Hello, Mrs Martin." />

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't skip straight to your question">
  English speakers often walk up to someone and begin with the request: "Where's the station?" or *Un café, s'il vous plaît*. That's normal in many English-speaking places, but in France it can come across as cold or rude. *Excusez-moi, …* is polite for stopping someone, and adding **bonjour** first makes you sound warmer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Only one bonjour a day">
  French people generally say *bonjour* to a given person once a day. If you run into the same colleague again that afternoon, a second *bonjour* can get a playful response. People say *rebonjour* instead, or just smile.
</Callout>
