By the end of this lesson you'll be able to present a simple argument for or against an idea, in speech or in writing, with a clear beginning, middle and end.

## The shape of an argument

A short French argument usually has three parts. In **the introduction**, you present the question. In **the middle**, you give your arguments one by one, and you answer the other side. In **the conclusion**, you sum up and give your final view.

You already have the building blocks. You can give your opinion with *à mon avis* and *je pense que*, from <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-opinions" text="the lesson on expressing opinions" />, and you can explain causes and consequences with *car*, *grâce à* and *c'est pourquoi*, from <LessonLink id="b1-cause-and-consequence" text="the lesson on cause and consequence" />. This lesson shows how to put them in order.

## Putting your ideas in order

<VocabTable group="order" />

You know *d'abord*, *ensuite* and *enfin* for telling a story. They work for arguments too. *Premièrement* and *deuxièmement* are more formal, and they make each argument stand out clearly. Don't mix the two series: choose *d'abord… ensuite… enfin*, or *premièrement… deuxièmement… enfin*.

<Example fr="Premièrement, les voitures polluent. Deuxièmement, elles font du bruit." en="Firstly, cars pollute. Secondly, they're noisy." />
<Example fr="D'abord, c'est cher. Ensuite, ce n'est pas pratique. Enfin, c'est mauvais pour la planète." en="First, it's expensive. Then, it isn't practical. Finally, it's bad for the planet." />

## For and against

<VocabTable group="sides" />

*Pour* and *contre* work on their own or before a noun: *Je suis pour*, *Je suis contre l'interdiction*. To ask, say *Vous êtes pour ou contre ?*

<Example fr="Je suis pour les vélos en ville." en="I'm in favour of bikes in town." />
<Example fr="Le projet a des avantages, mais aussi des inconvénients." en="The project has advantages, but also drawbacks." />

*Les avantages et les inconvénients* is the standard way to say "the pros and cons". It's a good frame for the middle of an argument: one paragraph for each.

## Looking at both sides

<VocabTable group="weigh" />

A strong argument shows that you've thought about the other side. *Il est vrai que…, mais…* lets you accept a point and then answer it. It's the written cousin of *ce n'est pas faux, mais…*, from <LessonLink id="b1-agreeing-disagreeing" text="the lesson on agreeing and disagreeing" />.

<Example fr="Il est vrai que la voiture est pratique, mais elle pollue." en="It's true that the car is practical, but it pollutes." />
<Example fr="D'une part, les bus sont moins chers. D'autre part, ils sont souvent en retard." en="On the one hand, buses are cheaper. On the other, they're often late." />

*D'une part… d'autre part* is a little more formal than *d'un côté… de l'autre*, which you met in the same lesson. Both present two sides of the same question. Use *en résumé* to open your conclusion.

## Debate words

<VocabTable group="debate" />

<Example fr="Chaque élève défend une idée et la justifie." en="Each pupil defends an idea and justifies it." />
<Example fr="La ville veut interdire les voitures dans le centre." en="The city wants to ban cars from the centre." />
<Example fr="Il y a trop de circulation le matin." en="There's too much traffic in the morning." />

*Interdire* is conjugated like *dire*, except for *vous interdisez*. *La circulation* is the usual word for road traffic.

## En français

In a class debate, the teacher asks two pupils, Léa and Hugo, about banning cars from the town centre.

- **Le professeur :** *Notre débat aujourd'hui : faut-il interdire les voitures dans le centre-ville ? Qui est pour ?* (Our debate today: should cars be banned from the town centre? Who's in favour?)
- **Léa :** *Moi, je suis pour, monsieur. D'abord, les voitures polluent. Ensuite, il y a trop de circulation.* (I'm in favour, sir. First, cars pollute. Then, there's too much traffic.)
- **Le professeur :** *Très bien. Et qui est contre ? Justifiez votre avis.* (Very good. And who's against? Justify your opinion.)
- **Hugo :** *Moi, je suis contre. Il est vrai que la pollution est un problème, mais beaucoup de gens ont besoin de leur voiture.* (I'm against. It's true that pollution is a problem, but lots of people need their car.)
- **Léa :** *Pourquoi ? Il y a des bus et des vélos !* (Why? There are buses and bikes!)
- **Hugo :** *Oui, mais tu oublies les personnes âgées. Pour elles, une interdiction, c'est difficile.* (Yes, but you're forgetting elderly people. For them, a ban is difficult.)
- **Le professeur :** *Ce n'est pas faux. Alors, quels sont les avantages et les inconvénients ?* (That's a fair point. So what are the pros and cons?)
- **Léa :** *D'une part, la ville serait plus calme. D'autre part, les gens marcheraient plus.* (On the one hand, the town would be quieter. On the other, people would walk more.)
- **Hugo :** *Et l'inconvénient, c'est que les magasins vont perdre des clients !* (And the drawback is that the shops are going to lose customers!)
- **Le professeur :** *En résumé, chacun défend une idée. Très bon débat !* (In summary, each of you is defending an idea. A very good debate!)

## A blog article

Here's a short blog post that follows the same plan, with one paragraph for each part.

*Pour ou contre la voiture en ville ?*

*Dans ma ville, on parle beaucoup d'un projet : interdire les voitures dans le centre. Voici mon avis.*

*D'abord, les avantages. Premièrement, les voitures polluent, et la pollution est mauvaise pour les enfants. Deuxièmement, sans voitures, le centre serait plus calme et plus beau : on pourrait marcher et faire du vélo.*

*Ensuite, les inconvénients. Il est vrai que beaucoup de gens ont besoin de leur voiture, comme les personnes âgées et les familles. Et certains magasins ont peur de perdre des clients.*

*Enfin, ma conclusion. D'une part, une interdiction totale, c'est difficile. D'autre part, il y a de plus en plus de circulation, c'est pourquoi il faut changer quelque chose. En résumé, je suis pour, mais avec plus de bus et des parkings à l'entrée de la ville. Et vous, vous êtes pour ou contre ?*

(For or against cars in town? In my town, people are talking a lot about a project: banning cars from the centre. Here's my opinion. First, the advantages. Firstly, cars pollute, and pollution is bad for children. Secondly, without cars, the centre would be quieter and nicer: we could walk and cycle. Next, the drawbacks. It's true that lots of people need their car, such as elderly people and families. And some shops are afraid of losing customers. Finally, my conclusion. On the one hand, a total ban is difficult. On the other, there's more and more traffic, which is why something has to change. In summary, I'm in favour, but with more buses and car parks at the edge of town. And you, are you for or against?)

<Callout type="warning" title="Il est vrai que + indicative">
  You've learned that many *il est… que* phrases take the subjunctive, such as *il est important que tu viennes*. *Il est vrai que* is different: it states a fact, so it takes the indicative. It's **il est vrai que la voiture est pratique**, not *il est vrai que la voiture soit pratique*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the other side first">
  Before you write your own argument, spend two minutes writing the best argument against your view, in two or three French sentences. Then start your real text with *Il est vrai que…, mais…*, using one of those sentences. Your argument will sound fairer, and you'll already have your middle paragraph.
</Callout>
