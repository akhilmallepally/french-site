By the end of this lesson you'll know how French came to Québec, the main words and expressions that differ from France, and how to understand a Québécois friend.

## A short history

<VocabTable group="history" />

**La Nouvelle-France**: in the 16th and 17th centuries, France claimed a large part of North America. In 1608, the explorer Samuel de Champlain founded Québec City, on the St Lawrence River. French settlers farmed the land, and their descendants are today's Québécois.

**The British conquest**: after a long war, New France passed to Britain in 1763. The French-speaking population stayed, kept its language and its Catholic traditions, and became a French-speaking minority inside an English-speaking country.

**La Charte de la langue française**, often called *la loi 101* (Bill 101), dates from 1977. It made French the official language of Québec, at work, in business and in most schools. Today, Québec is the main French-speaking area of North America.

**La Fête nationale du Québec** is on 24 June, with concerts, flags and big celebrations across the province.

You saw Québec's place in the French-speaking world in <LessonLink id="a2-culture-francophone-world" text="the lesson on the francophone world" />, with *le souper*, *la fin de semaine* and *magasiner*.

## Everyday words

<VocabTable group="words" />

Many everyday words are different in Québec. Some are old French words that France stopped using, like *un char*, once a cart. Others are English words that took on a French life, like *un chum*. And some are French inventions to avoid English, like *un courriel*, which France also recommends but rarely uses.

<Example fr="Mon chum a acheté un nouveau char." en="My boyfriend bought a new car." />
<Example fr="Je t'envoie un courriel ce soir." en="I'll send you an email tonight." />
<Example fr="On mange une poutine pour le dîner ?" en="Shall we have poutine for lunch?" />

Meals are shifted, as in Belgium and Switzerland: *le déjeuner* is breakfast, *le dîner* is lunch and *le souper* is the evening meal. *La poutine* is Québec's most famous dish: chips covered in cheese curds and hot gravy.

## Expressions

<VocabTable group="expressions" />

When you say *Merci*, a Québécois will often answer *Bienvenue !*, as English speakers say "You're welcome". In France, *bienvenue* only means "welcome", to a new place.

<Example fr="Il fait frette ! Mets ta tuque." en="It's freezing! Put your woolly hat on." />
<Example fr="J'ai de la misère à comprendre l'accent." en="I have trouble understanding the accent." />
<Example fr="Tu aimes le ski ? Pantoute !" en="Do you like skiing? Not at all!" />

*C'est le fun* means "it's fun", and you'll hear it everywhere: *C'est le fun de faire du ski.*

## Tu and vous

In Québec, *tu* is used more freely than in France. Shop assistants, waiters and even colleagues you've just met will often say *tu* to you, and it's friendly, not rude. In formal situations, at the bank or with older people, *vous* is still common. If someone says *tu* to you, you can usually answer with *tu*.

## Accent and understanding

The Québec accent sounds very different from Paris French, and at first you may find it hard to follow. Vowels are often longer, and *t* and *d* before *i* and *u* sound like "ts" and "dz": *tu* sounds a little like "tsu". Written French, on the other hand, is almost the same on both sides of the Atlantic, so newspapers and websites from Québec are easy to read.

## En français

Léo, from Lyon, is visiting his friend Émilie in Montréal.

- *Salut Léo ! Bienvenue à Montréal ! Il fait frette, non ?* (Hi Léo! Welcome to Montréal! It's freezing, isn't it?)
- *Frette ? Très froid ? Oui, et j'ai oublié mon chapeau.* (Frette? Very cold? Yes, and I forgot my hat.)
- *Ton chapeau ? Tu veux dire ta tuque ! Viens, mon chum nous attend dans son char.* (Your hat? You mean your tuque! Come on, my boyfriend's waiting for us in his car.)
- *Ton chum, c'est ton copain ? Et un char, c'est une voiture ?* (Your chum, is that your boyfriend? And a char is a car?)
- *Exactement ! Et pour le dîner, on va manger une poutine.* (Exactly! And for lunch, we're going to eat a poutine.)
- *Le dîner ? Il est seulement midi !* (Dinner? It's only midday!)
- *Ici, le dîner, c'est à midi. Le soir, c'est le souper.* (Here, dîner is at midday. In the evening, it's souper.)
- *D'accord. J'ai de la misère à tout comprendre, mais c'est le fun !* (OK. I'm having trouble understanding everything, but it's fun!)
- *Tu vois ? Tu parles déjà québécois !* (You see? You're already speaking Québécois!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Bienvenue or de rien?">
  In Québec, the usual answer to *merci* is **Bienvenue !** In France, it's **De rien** or *Je vous en prie*, and *bienvenue* only means "welcome", to someone arriving: *Bienvenue à Paris !* If you answer *merci* with *bienvenue* in Paris, people will understand, but they'll guess you've been to Montréal.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read Québec, then listen">
  Choose one short news article from a Québec website and read it first: the written French will be easy. Then find a radio or video report on the same story and listen. Because you already know what it's about, you can focus on the accent, and it will start to sound familiar much faster.
</Callout>
