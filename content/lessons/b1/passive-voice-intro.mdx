By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand and use the passive in the present and the passé composé: *Le pont a été construit en 1900*.

## What the passive does

In an ordinary, active sentence, the subject does the action: *Les secours sauvent l'enfant* (The emergency services rescue the child). In a passive sentence, the subject is the person or thing the action is done to: *L'enfant est sauvé par les secours* (The child is rescued by the emergency services).

You use the passive when the result matters more than who did it. That's why it's very common in the news, which you met in <LessonLink id="b1-media-and-news" text="the lesson on media and news" />.

## How to form it

The passive is *être* + a past participle. The tense is carried by *être*.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present | *On fabrique ces voitures en France.* | *Ces voitures sont fabriquées en France.* |
| Passé composé | *Un incendie a détruit la forêt.* | *La forêt a été détruite par un incendie.* |

In the passé composé, *être* itself is in the passé composé, so you get *a été* + the participle. Remember that *être* takes *avoir* in its own passé composé: *j'ai été*, *il a été*, *ils ont été*.

<Example fr="Ces voitures sont fabriquées en France." en="These cars are made in France." />
<Example fr="Le pont a été construit en 1900." en="The bridge was built in 1900." />
<Example fr="Le voleur a été arrêté hier soir." en="The thief was arrested last night." />

## Agreement

The participle agrees with the subject, as with the verbs that take *être* in <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the lesson on the passé composé with être" />. Add *-e* for a feminine subject, *-s* for a plural one, and *-es* for a feminine plural.

<Example fr="La maison a été construite en 1950." en="The house was built in 1950." />
<Example fr="Trois personnes ont été sauvées par les secours." en="Three people were rescued by the emergency services." />
<Example fr="La présidente a été élue en mai." en="The president was elected in May." />

The agreement is only on the participle. *Été* never changes.

## Who did it: par

To say who or what did the action, add *par*. It's the subject of the active sentence.

<Example fr="La forêt a été détruite par un incendie." en="The forest was destroyed by a fire." />
<Example fr="L'homme a été arrêté par la police." en="The man was arrested by the police." />

Very often, there's no *par* at all, because the agent is obvious or unknown: *Le voleur a été arrêté.* Everyone knows it was the police.

## Three news items

Here are three short news items, first in the passive, as in a newspaper, and then with *on*, as you'd tell them to a friend.

*Incendie dans les Alpes : une forêt a été détruite dimanche. Deux hommes ont été arrêtés par la police.*

*Tempête en Bretagne : un pont a été détruit, et dix personnes ont été sauvées par les secours.*

*La nouvelle présidente de la région a été élue hier.*

(Fire in the Alps: a forest was destroyed on Sunday. Two men were arrested by the police. Storm in Brittany: a bridge was destroyed, and ten people were rescued by the emergency services. The region's new president was elected yesterday.)

With *on*:

*On a arrêté deux hommes. On a sauvé dix personnes. On a élu une nouvelle présidente hier.*

(They arrested two men. They rescued ten people. They elected a new president yesterday.)

## When French prefers on

French uses the passive less than English, especially in conversation. When there's no *par*, French often prefers *on* with an active verb: *On a arrêté le voleur* rather than *Le voleur a été arrêté*. Both are correct. The passive sounds more like the news, and *on* sounds more like everyday speech. You'll see more ways to avoid the passive at B2.

## En français

Two colleagues are talking about the news on Monday morning.

- *Tu as vu les infos ? Il y a eu une grosse tempête en Bretagne.* (Have you seen the news? There was a big storm in Brittany.)
- *Oui, c'est grave. Un pont a été détruit, non ?* (Yes, it's serious. A bridge was destroyed, wasn't it?)
- *Oui, le vieux pont du port. Il a été construit en 1900.* (Yes, the old bridge in the harbour. It was built in 1900.)
- *Et les gens ? Il y a des blessés ?* (And the people? Are there any injured?)
- *Non, heureusement. Dix personnes ont été sauvées par les secours.* (No, fortunately. Ten people were rescued by the emergency services.)
- *Tant mieux. Et il y a eu des inondations ?* (Good. And were there floods?)
- *Oui, dans plusieurs villages. Beaucoup de maisons sont sous l'eau.* (Yes, in several villages. Lots of houses are under water.)
- *J'espère qu'on va vite construire un nouveau pont.* (I hope they'll build a new bridge quickly.)

<Callout type="warning" title="A été, then the participle">
  In the passé composé of the passive, you need both participles: *été* and the main verb. It's **la maison a été construite**, not *la maison a construite* or *la maison est été construite*. And only the second participle agrees: **elles ont été sauvées**, never *elles ont étées sauvées*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Hunt for passives in headlines">
  Read five French news headlines today and look for *a été*, *ont été*, *est* or *sont* followed by a participle. For each one, say who did the action, and turn it into an active sentence or an *on* sentence. You'll soon recognize the passive at a glance, and the news will be easier to follow.
</Callout>
