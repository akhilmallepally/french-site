## How an opinion piece works

An opinion piece, *un texte d'opinion*, has three parts. Keep them in this order and your reader will follow you easily.

1. **Your position.** One sentence that answers the question, with an opinion marker: *À mon avis, …*
2. **Two reasons, each with an example.** Start the second reason with *de plus*, and introduce each example with *par exemple*. A sentence with *ce qui… c'est* or *dont* makes a reason sound precise.
3. **A conclusion.** Start with *en conclusion* and repeat your position in different words.

Write in the present, and give each sentence one idea. Keep it short: eight to ten sentences are enough. A convincing piece, *un texte convaincant*, gives real examples rather than long explanations.

## A model opinion piece

*Beaucoup d'amis ou deux ou trois amis proches ?*

*À mon avis, deux ou trois amis proches, c'est mieux que beaucoup d'amis. D'abord, ce qui compte en amitié, c'est la confiance, et on ne peut pas faire confiance à vingt personnes. De plus, il faut du temps pour une vraie amitié. Par exemple, ma meilleure amie, c'est la personne dont j'ai besoin quand ça ne va pas. Nous nous entendons très bien depuis quinze ans. En conclusion, je préfère avoir peu d'amis, mais de vrais amis. Sarah*

(Lots of friends, or two or three close friends? In my opinion, two or three close friends are better than lots of friends. First, what matters in friendship is trust, and you can't trust twenty people. What's more, a real friendship takes time. For example, my best friend is the person I need when things aren't going well. We've got on very well for fifteen years. In conclusion, I'd rather have few friends, but real friends. Sarah)

## A reader reacts

Hugo, a reader, *un lecteur*, decides to react. He uses *vous*, because he doesn't know Sarah.

*Un lecteur réagit*

*Je ne suis pas d'accord avec vous, Sarah. Ce n'est pas faux : la confiance, c'est important. Mais ce qui me plaît, c'est d'avoir des amis au travail, au foot et dans mon quartier. Je m'entends bien avec beaucoup de personnes, et je trouve ça génial. De plus, on apprend beaucoup avec tous ses amis. En conclusion, la confiance compte, c'est vrai, mais je ne veux pas choisir entre mes amis ! Hugo*

(A reader reacts. I don't agree with you, Sarah. That's a fair point: trust is important. But what I like is having friends at work, at football and in my neighbourhood. I get on well with lots of people, and I think that's brilliant. What's more, you learn a lot from all your friends. In conclusion, trust matters, it's true, but I don't want to choose between my friends! Hugo)

## How to reply when you disagree

Hugo's reply follows four steps, and you can use them for any reply:

1. Say who you're answering, and that you disagree: *Je ne suis pas d'accord avec vous.*
2. Accept what's true in their view: *Ce n'est pas faux*, *c'est vrai*.
3. Give your own reasons, with *mais* and *de plus*.
4. End with *en conclusion* and a clear sentence.

## Your turn

Choose one question, and write an opinion piece of eight to ten sentences, on paper or in a notes app. Then swap sides, react as a reader, and write a reply that disagrees.

## Check your work

- Does your first sentence give your position, with an opinion marker?
- Did you put *que* after *penser*, *croire* and *trouver*?
- Is there one sentence with *ce qui* or *ce que*, and one with *dont*?
- In your reply, did you concede a point before you disagreed?
- Does each reason start a new sentence, with *de plus* or *par exemple*?
- Did you keep *vous* all the way through the reply?

<Callout type="warning" title="Answer the idea, not the person">
  In writing, *tu as tort* or *vous avez tort* sounds much harsher than in conversation, because there's no smile or tone of voice to soften it. Disagree with the idea instead: **Je ne suis pas d'accord avec vous** or **Ce n'est pas faux, mais…**. Your reply will be more convincing, and the writer is more likely to read to the end.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the conclusion first">
  Before you start, write your last sentence: the one that begins *En conclusion*. Once you know where you're going, choosing two reasons and two examples is much easier, and every sentence you write has a job to do. You can always polish the conclusion at the end.
</Callout>
