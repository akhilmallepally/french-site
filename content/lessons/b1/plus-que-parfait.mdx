By the end of this lesson you'll be able to go back in time inside a story, and say what had already happened.

## The past before the past

When you tell a story in the past, you sometimes need to go further back, to something that had already happened. English uses "had": "When I arrived, the train had left." French uses the **plus-que-parfait**.

<Example fr="Quand je suis arrivé à la gare, le train était parti." en="When I arrived at the station, the train had left." />
<Example fr="Elle était fatiguée parce qu'elle n'avait pas dormi." en="She was tired because she hadn't slept." />

In <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-vs-imparfait" text="the lesson on the passé composé and the imparfait" />, you used two past tenses: one for events, one for the scene. The plus-que-parfait sits behind both of them, in a past that comes earlier still.

## How it's formed

Take the imparfait of *avoir* or *être*, and add the past participle. The choice between *avoir* and *être* is exactly the same as in the passé composé.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

| Passé composé | Plus-que-parfait |
|---|---|
| *j'ai fini* (I finished) | *j'avais fini* (I had finished) |
| *nous sommes partis* (we left) | *nous étions partis* (we had left) |
| *elle s'est levée* (she got up) | *elle s'était levée* (she had got up) |

The agreement rules don't change either. With *être*, the participle agrees with the subject, as in <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the lesson on the passé composé with être" />: *elles étaient arrivées*. With *avoir*, it agrees with a direct object that comes before the verb, as in <LessonLink id="a2-pronouns-in-passe-compose" text="the lesson on pronouns in the passé composé" />: *les clés que j'avais oubliées*.

## When to use it

Use the plus-que-parfait when you step back from the main events of a story to something that came before.

<Example fr="J'ai vu que quelqu'un avait fermé la porte à clé." en="I saw that someone had locked the door." />
<Example fr="Il ne savait pas où il avait mis ses clés." en="He didn't know where he had put his keys." />
<Example fr="Nous avions déjà mangé quand ils sont arrivés." en="We had already eaten when they arrived." />

*Déjà* and *pas encore*, from <LessonLink id="a2-time-expressions-past" text="the lesson on time expressions in the past" />, go between the auxiliary and the participle: *j'avais déjà vu ce film*, *elle n'était pas encore partie*.

English often uses the simple past where French needs the plus-que-parfait. "I realized I forgot my keys" is fine in English, but French has to show the order: *Je me suis rendu compte que j'avais oublié mes clés.*

## A story in three tenses

Here's how the three past tenses work together. The imparfait sets the scene, the passé composé moves the story forward, and the plus-que-parfait looks back.

<Example fr="Il était huit heures et il pleuvait." en="It was eight o'clock and it was raining. (scene)" />
<Example fr="Je suis sorti, et je me suis aperçu que j'avais oublié mon parapluie." en="I went out, and I noticed I had forgotten my umbrella. (events, then a step back)" />
<Example fr="Je suis rentré, mais mon chat avait disparu !" en="I went back in, but my cat had disappeared! (event, then a step back)" />

## Words for things that went wrong

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="Je n'avais pas entendu mon réveil, alors je suis arrivé trop tard." en="I hadn't heard my alarm, so I arrived too late." />
<Example fr="La prochaine fois, je partirai plus tôt." en="Next time, I'll leave earlier." />
<Example fr="Je m'étais levé auparavant, mais j'étais encore fatigué." en="I had got up earlier, but I was still tired." />

*Se rendre compte* is a reflexive verb, so it takes *être*, but its participle doesn't agree, because the real object is *compte*: *elle s'est rendu compte*. *S'apercevoir* agrees normally: *elle s'est aperçue*.

## En français

A friend explains why he missed the trip to Lyon.

- *Tu n'es pas venu à Lyon samedi ? On t'a attendu !* (You didn't come to Lyon on Saturday? We waited for you!)
- *Non, j'ai raté le train. Quand je suis arrivé à la gare, il était déjà parti.* (No, I missed the train. When I got to the station, it had already left.)
- *Mais tu étais en retard ?* (But were you late?)
- *Oui. Je n'avais pas entendu mon réveil.* (Yes. I hadn't heard my alarm.)
- *Tu t'étais couché tard ?* (Had you gone to bed late?)
- *Oui, j'avais regardé trois films.* (Yes, I had watched three films.)
- *Et tu n'as pas pris un autre train ?* (And didn't you take another train?)
- *Si, mais quand je suis monté, je me suis rendu compte que j'avais oublié mon billet !* (Yes, but when I got on, I realized I had forgotten my ticket!)
- *Oh non ! Et le contrôleur ?* (Oh no! And the ticket inspector?)
- *Il a été sympa. Il m'a laissé voyager.* (He was nice. He let me travel.)

<Callout type="warning" title="One word changes the story">
  Compare **Quand je suis arrivé, le train est parti** and **Quand je suis arrivé, le train était parti**. In the first, the train leaves as you arrive: you watch it go. In the second, it had already gone before you got there. When the order matters, check which one you mean.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Draw a line">
  If you're not sure which tense you need, draw a short line on paper and mark "now" at the right-hand end. Put the main events of your story on the line, then mark anything that happened before them further to the left. Everything in that earlier zone takes the plus-que-parfait.
</Callout>
