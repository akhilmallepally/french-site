By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say what you want other people to do, and how you feel about what they do.

## Wanting someone else to do something

English says "I want you to come". French can't put the other person straight after *vouloir*. It uses *que*, the person, and the subjunctive: "I want that you come".

<VocabTable group="wishes" />

<Example fr="Je veux que tu viennes." en="I want you to come." />
<Example fr="Maman veut que je rentre à Noël." en="Mum wants me to come home for Christmas." />
<Example fr="J'aimerais que vous restiez dîner." en="I'd like you to stay for dinner." />
<Example fr="Mes parents préfèrent que nous prenions le train." en="My parents prefer that we take the train." />

*J'aimerais que* and *je voudrais que* are a little softer than *je veux que*. *Souhaiter que* is more formal, and it's common in wishes for other people: *Je souhaite que tout se passe bien* (I hope everything goes well).

## Being happy, sad or surprised

When you react to what someone else does, the reaction comes first, then *que* and the subjunctive. You know many of these adjectives from <LessonLink id="a2-emotions-and-feelings" text="the lesson on emotions and feelings" />.

<VocabTable group="feelings" />

<Example fr="Je suis content que tu sois là." en="I'm happy you're here." />
<Example fr="Elle est triste que ses enfants partent." en="She's sad that her children are leaving." />
<Example fr="Nous sommes surpris qu'il ne vienne pas." en="We're surprised he isn't coming." />
<Example fr="Ça me fait plaisir que tu m'appelles." en="It makes me happy that you're calling me." />

*Déçu*, *fier* and *fâché* work the same way: *Je suis fier que tu réussisses* (I'm proud that you're succeeding).

## Regret and fear

<VocabTable group="regret" />

<Example fr="C'est dommage qu'il pleuve." en="It's a shame it's raining." />
<Example fr="Je regrette que vous ne puissiez pas venir." en="I'm sorry you can't come." />
<Example fr="J'ai peur que le train soit en retard." en="I'm afraid the train will be late." />
<Example fr="Je suis désolé que tu sois malade." en="I'm sorry you're ill." />

*Je suis désolé que* is the everyday way to say you're sorry about someone else's situation. *Regretter que* means the same, but it sounds more formal, and it's common in letters and emails: *Nous regrettons que vous ne puissiez pas venir.*

After *avoir peur que* and *craindre que*, written French often adds a small *ne* that doesn't make the sentence negative: *J'ai peur qu'il ne soit en retard.* It's formal, and you only need to recognize it. Don't read it as "not".

## Hope is different

Not every feeling takes the subjunctive. *Espérer que*, from <LessonLink id="b1-dreams-and-ambitions" text="the lesson on dreams and ambitions" />, takes the indicative, often the futur.

| Subjunctive | Indicative |
|---|---|
| *Je souhaite que tu viennes.* | *J'espère que tu viendras.* |
| *Je veux qu'il réussisse.* | *J'espère qu'il réussira.* |

## The family

<VocabTable group="family" />

*Maman* and *papa* are what children and adults call their own parents. With other people, you can say *ma mère* and *mon père*.

## En français

Two siblings are talking about Christmas.

- *Maman veut que je rentre à Noël.* (Mum wants me to come home for Christmas.)
- *Moi aussi. Elle aimerait que nous soyons tous là le 24.* (Me too. She'd like us all to be there on the 24th.)
- *Je suis content qu'on se voie, mais c'est dommage que ce soit si court.* (I'm happy we'll see each other, but it's a shame it's so short.)
- *Oui. Et papa préfère que nous prenions le train.* (Yes. And Dad would rather we took the train.)
- *Pourquoi ? J'aimerais mieux prendre la voiture.* (Why? I'd rather take the car.)
- *Il a peur qu'il y ait de la neige sur la route.* (He's afraid there'll be snow on the road.)
- *Ah, d'accord. Et ta copine, elle vient ?* (Ah, OK. And your girlfriend, is she coming?)
- *Non. Maman est triste qu'elle ne puisse pas venir.* (No. Mum's sad she can't come.)
- *Dommage. Mais je suis ravi que tu sois là, toi !* (Shame. But I'm delighted that you'll be there!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Talk about now, not the past">
  These sentences react to something happening now or later: *Je suis content que tu sois là* (I'm happy you're here). To react to something already finished, "I'm happy you came", French needs a past form of the subjunctive that you'll learn in <LessonLink id="b2-subjonctif-passe" text="the lesson on the past subjunctive" />. For now, keep your reactions in the present: **Je suis content que tu sois là**, not *je suis content que tu es venu*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="React to the news">
  Read three short news headlines, in French or in English, and react to each one in French: *Je suis content que…*, *C'est dommage que…*, *J'ai peur que…*. Real news gives you real verbs, and having an opinion about it makes the sentence easier to remember than a textbook example.
</Callout>
