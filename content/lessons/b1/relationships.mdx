By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about friends, partners and arguments, and say how a relationship has changed.

## Friends

In <LessonLink id="a2-emotions-and-feelings" text="the lesson on emotions and feelings" /> you said how you feel. These words describe the people you feel it about.

<VocabTable group="friends" />

<Example fr="Léa est une amie proche depuis l'école." en="Léa has been a close friend since school." />
<Example fr="Je m'entends très bien avec mon frère." en="I get on very well with my brother." />
<Example fr="Je te fais confiance." en="I trust you." />
<Example fr="Au lycée, je me suis fait des amis très vite." en="At secondary school, I made friends very quickly." />

*Faire confiance* takes *à* before the person, so with a pronoun it uses *lui* or *leur*: *Je lui fais confiance* (I trust him or her), as in <LessonLink id="a2-indirect-object-pronouns" text="the lesson on indirect object pronouns" />.

## Love

<VocabTable group="love" />

<Example fr="Ils sont en couple depuis deux ans." en="They've been together for two years." />
<Example fr="Elle est tombée amoureuse de Paul pendant les vacances." en="She fell in love with Paul during the holidays." />
<Example fr="Mes parents se sont mariés en 1995." en="My parents got married in 1995." />
<Example fr="Mon frère est célibataire, et ça lui va très bien !" en="My brother is single, and that suits him fine!" />

*Tomber amoureux* uses *être* in the passé composé, like *tomber*: *il est tombé amoureux*, *elle est tombée amoureuse*. You know *le copain* and *la copine* from <LessonLink id="a1-family" text="the lesson on family" />. They often mean "boyfriend" and "girlfriend": *Ma copine s'appelle Inès.*

*Le mariage* means both the marriage and the wedding day: *Le mariage est en juin* (The wedding is in June). *Se marier* is the verb, and it always keeps its *se*.

## Arguments and endings

<VocabTable group="conflict" />

<Example fr="Marc et Julie se sont disputés hier, mais ils se sont réconciliés ce matin." en="Marc and Julie argued yesterday, but they made up this morning." />
<Example fr="Après cinq ans, ils ont rompu." en="After five years, they broke up." />
<Example fr="Il est jaloux, et je n'aime pas la jalousie." en="He's jealous, and I don't like jealousy." />

*Une dispute* is an argument between people who know each other, often about something small. *Un conflit* is bigger and lasts longer, for example between neighbours or colleagues. *Rompre* is irregular in the *il* form: *il rompt*, with a silent *t*. Its participle is *rompu*, and it uses *avoir*: *ils ont rompu*.

## Doing things to each other

Many of these verbs are reciprocal: two people do something to each other. You met this use of *se* in <LessonLink id="a2-reflexive-passe-compose" text="the lesson on reflexive verbs in the passé composé" />. The verb takes *être* in the passé composé, and the participle agrees with the subject.

<Example fr="Nous nous entendons bien." en="We get on well." />
<Example fr="Ils se disputent souvent." en="They argue often." />
<Example fr="Elles se sont réconciliées." en="They made up. (two women)" />
<Example fr="Nous nous sommes mariés en mai." en="We got married in May." />

*Se séparer* works the same way: *Ils se sont séparés l'année dernière* (They split up last year).

## Words that change for a man or a woman

Several of these words have a masculine and a feminine form, so check who you're talking about.

- *Il est tombé amoureux* and *elle est tombée amoureuse*: both the participle and the adjective agree.
- *Un ami proche* and *une amie proche*: *proche* doesn't change, but *ami* does in writing.
- *Un célibataire* and *une célibataire*: only the article changes.

*L'amitié* and *l'amour* hide their gender behind *l'*. *L'amitié* is feminine (*une belle amitié*), and *l'amour* is masculine (*un grand amour*).

## En français

Two friends are talking about Julie and Marc.

- *Tu as des nouvelles de Julie ?* (Have you heard from Julie?)
- *Oui. Elle s'est disputée avec Marc samedi.* (Yes. She argued with Marc on Saturday.)
- *Encore ? Pourquoi ?* (Again? Why?)
- *Il est jaloux de son nouveau collègue.* (He's jealous of her new colleague.)
- *Ils vont se séparer ?* (Are they going to split up?)
- *Non ! Ils se sont réconciliés hier soir.* (No! They made up last night.)
- *Ils sont en couple depuis combien de temps ?* (How long have they been together?)
- *Depuis trois ans. Et ils vont se marier l'été prochain !* (For three years. And they're getting married next summer!)
- *Super ! Elle lui fait confiance ?* (Great! Does she trust him?)
- *Oui. Et ils s'entendent très bien, tu sais.* (Yes. And they get on very well, you know.)

## A letter to an advice column

*Bonjour, je m'appelle Clara et j'ai 25 ans. Ma meilleure amie s'appelle Inès. Nous sommes amies depuis l'école, et je lui fais confiance. Mais le mois dernier, nous nous sommes disputées : elle est tombée amoureuse de mon frère ! Je suis jalouse de leur couple, et maintenant, nous ne nous parlons plus. Je voudrais me réconcilier avec elle. Qu'est-ce que je dois faire ? Clara*

(Hello, my name is Clara and I'm 25. My best friend is called Inès. We've been friends since school, and I trust her. But last month, we had an argument: she fell in love with my brother! I'm jealous of them as a couple, and now we don't speak to each other any more. I'd like to make up with her. What should I do? Clara)

<Callout type="warning" title="Married to, in love with">
  The little words don't match English. You get married **avec** someone: **Il s'est marié avec Anne**, not *à Anne*. You fall in love **de** someone: **Elle est tombée amoureuse de lui**, not *avec lui*. And *marier* without *se* means to marry other people, as a mayor or a priest does, so keep the *se*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn the opposites together">
  Many of these words come in pairs: *se disputer* and *se réconcilier*, *se marier* and *divorcer*, *être en couple* and *être célibataire*. Learn each pair as one unit, on the same line of your notes. When you need one, the other comes with it, and you can tell a whole story in two verbs.
</Callout>
