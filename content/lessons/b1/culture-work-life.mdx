By the end of this lesson you'll know how working hours, holidays and lunch breaks usually work in France, and how to handle *tu* and *vous* at work.

## Working hours

<VocabTable group="time" />

**La semaine de 35 heures** became the legal standard in 2000. Many people actually work more, especially *cadres*, but extra hours are either paid as *heures supplémentaires* or given back as **RTT** days (*réduction du temps de travail*), which employees can take as extra time off.

**Les congés payés** date from 1936, when workers got two weeks of paid holiday for the first time. Today, employees have five weeks a year. Many families still take a large part of them in July or August, and some small businesses close for the whole of August.

When a public holiday falls on a Thursday or a Tuesday, many people **font le pont**: they take the Friday or the Monday off too, and have a four-day weekend.

<Example fr="Le 1er mai est un jeudi, alors je fais le pont." en="The 1st of May is a Thursday, so I'm taking the Friday off too." />
<Example fr="J'ai fait des heures supplémentaires cette semaine." en="I did some overtime this week." />

## The lunch break

<VocabTable group="lunch" />

**La pause déjeuner** is important in France. It often lasts an hour, sometimes more, and many people eat a proper meal with colleagues rather than a sandwich at their desk. Large companies have a staff canteen. In smaller ones, employees often receive **tickets-restaurant**, meal vouchers that pay for part of their lunch in a restaurant or a supermarket.

## Strikes and unions

<VocabTable group="strikes" />

France has a reputation for strikes, especially in public transport and schools. **Une grève** is often organized by **les syndicats**, the trade unions, and it can last a day or several weeks. Strikes are announced in advance, so people can find another way to get to work.

<Example fr="Demain, les trains font grève. Je vais travailler de chez moi." en="Tomorrow, the trains are on strike. I'll work from home." />

## Tu or vous at work?

<VocabTable group="people" />

You know *tutoyer* and *vouvoyer* from <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the lesson on subject pronouns" />. At work, the rule depends on the company. In start-ups and young teams, everyone often says *tu*, including to the boss. In traditional companies, banks and administrations, people often keep *vous* for years, especially with the *patron* and others higher in **la hiérarchie**. The safe choice is to start with *vous* and wait to be invited: *On peut se tutoyer, si tu veux.*

## Work today

<VocabTable group="today" />

**Le télétravail** grew enormously in France from 2020, and many office workers now work from home one or two days a week. **Le droit à la déconnexion**, which dates from 2017, means that companies of a certain size must agree rules so that employees don't have to answer emails in the evening or at the weekend.

<Example fr="Je fais du télétravail le lundi et le vendredi." en="I work from home on Mondays and Fridays." />

## En français

Tom has just arrived from England, and he's asking his colleague Camille some questions.

- *Camille, je peux te poser une question ? Ici, on se tutoie ?* (Camille, can I ask you a question? Do we say tu here?)
- *Entre collègues, oui. Mais avec la patronne, on dit vous.* (Between colleagues, yes. But with the boss, we say vous.)
- *D'accord. Et pour les vacances, j'ai combien de semaines ?* (OK. And for holidays, how many weeks do I get?)
- *Cinq semaines de congés payés, plus les RTT.* (Five weeks of paid holiday, plus the RTT days.)
- *Cinq semaines ? En Angleterre, j'en avais quatre !* (Five weeks? In England, I had four!)
- *Et en mai, il y a beaucoup de jours fériés, alors on fait souvent le pont.* (And in May, there are lots of public holidays, so we often take long weekends.)
- *Génial. Et à midi, vous mangez ensemble ?* (Brilliant. And at lunchtime, do you all eat together?)
- *Souvent, oui. On a des tickets-restaurant. Tu viens avec nous aujourd'hui ?* (Often, yes. We get meal vouchers. Are you coming with us today?)
- *Avec plaisir !* (With pleasure!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Plural words for time off">
  Several words for time off are plural in French, even for one period: **les congés**, **les vacances**, **les RTT**. *Je prends mes congés en août*, not *mon congé*. *Un jour de congé* (a day off) is the main exception. And it's **faire grève**, with no article: *Les trains font grève.*
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask how things work here">
  On your first day in a French workplace, ask one colleague directly: *Ici, on se tutoie ou on se vouvoie ?* It's a normal question, and it shows you care about getting it right. The answer tells you more about the company than the website does.
</Callout>
