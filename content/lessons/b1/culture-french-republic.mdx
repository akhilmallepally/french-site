By the end of this lesson you'll know the main symbols and values of the French Republic, the history behind the 14 juillet, and how French elections work.

## The motto

<VocabTable group="motto" />

**Liberté, égalité, fraternité** is *la devise de la République*. You'll see it on the front of town halls, schools and other public buildings, and on some coins. The three words come from the time of the French Revolution: freedom for each person, equality before the law, and brotherhood between citizens.

France has been a republic, with some breaks, since the Revolution. The current one, *la Cinquième République* (the Fifth Republic), dates from 1958.

## Symbols

<VocabTable group="symbols" />

**Marianne** is a woman who represents the Republic. Every town hall has *un buste de Marianne*, and her face is on French stamps and on some euro coins. Over the years, famous actresses have been chosen as models for her.

**Le drapeau**, the flag, is *bleu, blanc, rouge*, in vertical bands. People often call it *le drapeau tricolore*, or simply *le tricolore*.

**La Marseillaise** is *l'hymne national*. It was written in 1792, during a war, and it got its name from soldiers from Marseille who sang it on their way to Paris. It's sung at official ceremonies and before international football and rugby matches.

<Example fr="Le drapeau français est bleu, blanc, rouge." en="The French flag is blue, white and red." />
<Example fr="Il y a un buste de Marianne à la mairie." en="There's a bust of Marianne in the town hall." />

## Laïcité

<VocabTable group="state" />

**La laïcité** is one of the key ideas of the Republic. A law of 1905 separates the Churches and the State: the State has no official religion, and everyone is free to believe or not to believe. In practice, it means that public schools give no religious teaching, and that the State treats all religions the same way.

*Laïcité* is an important and sometimes sensitive subject in France, and it's often in the news. If it comes up, listen first and ask questions.

<Example fr="En France, l'État et les religions sont séparés depuis 1905." en="In France, the State and religion have been separate since 1905." />

## The history of the 14 juillet

<VocabTable group="history" />

You know from <LessonLink id="a2-culture-holidays-and-festivals" text="the lesson on holidays and festivals" /> that **le 14 juillet** is *la fête nationale*, with a parade and fireworks. It recalls two events. The first is **la prise de la Bastille**, on 14 July 1789, when the people of Paris stormed an old royal prison, at the start of *la Révolution française*. The second is the *Fête de la Fédération*, a great celebration of national unity held on the same date in 1790.

<Example fr="La Bastille a été prise le 14 juillet 1789." en="The Bastille was stormed on 14 July 1789." />

## Elections

<VocabTable group="elections" />

**Le président** (or *la présidente*) *de la République* is elected for five years. This has been the rule since 2002; before, it was seven. Most French elections have **deux tours** (two rounds). If no candidate gets more than half the votes in the first round, the two leading candidates go through to a second round two weeks later.

**Le droit de vote**: every French citizen can vote from the age of 18. This has been the voting age since 1974. Elections take place on a Sunday, often in a school or in the town hall.

**Le maire** runs the town. Citizens elect the town council, *le conseil municipal*, and the council then chooses the mayor. That's why the mayor is *élu par le conseil municipal*.

<Example fr="Le président est élu pour cinq ans." en="The president is elected for five years." />
<Example fr="Les électeurs votent le dimanche." en="Voters vote on Sundays." />

## En français

Emma, an exchange student, is visiting the town hall with her French friend Lucas.

- *Lucas, qui est cette femme ? Il y a un buste d'elle à l'entrée.* (Lucas, who's that woman? There's a bust of her at the entrance.)
- *C'est Marianne. Elle représente la République.* (That's Marianne. She represents the Republic.)
- *Ah, d'accord. Et ces trois mots, là ?* (Oh, OK. And those three words there?)
- *C'est la devise : Liberté, égalité, fraternité. On la voit sur toutes les mairies.* (It's the motto: Liberty, equality, fraternity. You see it on all town halls.)
- *Et pourquoi il y a des drapeaux devant la mairie aujourd'hui ?* (And why are there flags in front of the town hall today?)
- *Parce que dimanche, il y a une élection. Les électeurs votent ici.* (Because on Sunday, there's an election. The voters vote here.)
- *Tu peux voter, toi ?* (Can you vote?)
- *Oui ! J'ai 19 ans, donc je vote pour la deuxième fois.* (Yes! I'm 19, so I'm voting for the second time.)
- *C'est génial. Je peux venir avec toi ?* (That's great. Can I come with you?)

<Callout type="warning" title="Le maire, la mairie, la mer">
  These three words sound alike, but they're very different. **Le maire** is the mayor, a person. **La mairie** is the town hall, a building. **La mer** is the sea. Don't say *je vais au maire* when you mean the town hall: it's **je vais à la mairie**. *Maire* is masculine for a man, and you'll hear *la maire* for a woman.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the front of the building">
  Next time you walk past a French town hall or school, in real life or on a street map online, look up at the front. Find the motto, the flag, and sometimes the date the building was built. Say in French what you see: *Il y a la devise et le drapeau.* Public buildings are a free lesson in French civic life.
</Callout>
