By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say "only" with *ne… que*, and "neither… nor" with *ne… ni… ni*: *Je n'ai que dix euros*, *Je n'ai ni frère ni sœur*.

## Ne… que: only

You know two-part negatives like *ne… plus* and *ne… rien* from <LessonLink id="a2-negative-expressions" text="the lesson on other negative expressions" />. *Ne… que* looks like them, but it means "only". *Ne* goes before the verb, as usual. *Que* goes right before the word or words that are restricted.

<VocabTable group="only" />

<Example fr="Je n'ai que dix euros." en="I've only got ten euros." />
<Example fr="Elle ne travaille que le matin." en="She only works in the mornings." />
<Example fr="Nous n'avons qu'un billet." en="We've only got one ticket." />

*Que* becomes *qu'* before a vowel. Because *que* moves to the restricted word, it can come quite late in the sentence: *Je ne paie par carte que dans les grands magasins* (I only pay by card in big shops).

## Not a real negative

*Ne… que* doesn't say that something is missing: you have something, just not much. So the article doesn't change to *de* as it does after *pas*. Compare:

| Negative | Restriction |
|---|---|
| *Je n'ai pas de pièces.* (I haven't got any coins.) | *Je n'ai que des pièces.* (I've only got coins.) |
| *Il ne boit pas d'eau.* (He doesn't drink water.) | *Il ne boit que de l'eau.* (He only drinks water.) |

## Seulement and uniquement

You know *seulement* from <LessonLink id="b1-money-and-banking" text="the lesson on money and banking" />. It means the same as *ne… que*, and so does *uniquement*, which is a little more formal and very common on signs.

<Example fr="J'ai seulement dix euros." en="I've only got ten euros." />
<Example fr="Paiement uniquement par carte." en="Card payment only." />

*Ne… que* is the most natural in speech and writing. *Seulement* is handy when the restricted word is the verb itself: *Je regarde seulement* (I'm just looking).

## Only… left

Add *plus* to say that only a little is left: *ne… plus que*. With *rester*, the impersonal form *il ne reste que* means "there's only… left".

<Example fr="Je n'ai plus que cinq euros." en="I've only got five euros left." />
<Example fr="Il ne reste que deux billets pour le concert." en="There are only two tickets left for the concert." />
<Example fr="J'ai à peine assez d'argent pour le loyer." en="I've barely got enough money for the rent." />

*À peine* (barely) isn't a negative at all, so it doesn't need *ne*. It usually goes after the verb.

## Ni… ni: neither… nor

<VocabTable group="neither" />

*Ne… ni… ni* says no to two things at once. *Ne* goes before the verb, and each *ni* goes before one of the two things. There's no *pas*.

With *un*, *une*, *des* and the partitive (*du*, *de la*), the article disappears after *ni*. With *le*, *la* and *les*, for example after *aimer*, it stays.

<Example fr="Je n'ai ni frère ni sœur." en="I have neither a brother nor a sister." />
<Example fr="Je ne bois ni café ni thé." en="I drink neither coffee nor tea." />
<Example fr="Je n'aime ni les chats ni les chiens." en="I like neither cats nor dogs." />

When the two things are the subject, each *ni* goes first and *ne* still comes before the verb: *Ni Paul ni Marie ne viennent ce soir.* As a short answer, *ni l'un ni l'autre* means "neither of them".

<Example fr="Tu préfères le studio ou l'appartement ? Ni l'un ni l'autre." en="Do you prefer the studio or the flat? Neither." />

## Money words

<VocabTable group="money" />

*Une pièce* and *un billet* have new meanings here: you've seen them as "a room" and "a ticket". The context almost always makes it clear. *La monnaie* is small change, and *Vous avez de la monnaie ?* means "Have you got change?"

<Example fr="Je n'ai pas de monnaie, je n'ai qu'un billet de 50 euros." en="I haven't got any change, I've only got a 50-euro note." />
<Example fr="Il est très dépensier : il est toujours fauché à la fin du mois." en="He's a big spender: he's always broke at the end of the month." />

## En français

Two flatmates, Julie and Malik, check their money at the end of the month.

- *Malik, tu peux me prêter dix euros ? Je suis fauchée.* (Malik, can you lend me ten euros? I'm broke.)
- *Désolé, je n'ai que cinq euros sur moi.* (Sorry, I've only got five euros on me.)
- *Et sur ton compte ?* (And in your account?)
- *Il ne reste que vingt euros, et le loyer, c'est dans trois jours !* (There's only twenty euros left, and the rent is in three days!)
- *Moi, je n'ai ni argent ni carte. J'ai perdu ma carte hier.* (I've got neither money nor a card. I lost my card yesterday.)
- *Tu es trop dépensière, Julie.* (You spend too much, Julie.)
- *Ce n'est pas faux. Bon, il reste des pâtes ou du riz ?* (That's a fair point. Well, is there any pasta or rice left?)
- *Ni l'un ni l'autre ! On n'a que des œufs.* (Neither! We've only got eggs.)
- *Alors ce soir, on mange des œufs !* (Then tonight, we're eating eggs!)

<Callout type="warning" title="No pas with que or ni">
  *Ne… que* and *ne… ni… ni* replace *pas*. Don't add it. It's **je n'ai que dix euros**, not *je n'ai pas que dix euros*, which means "I haven't only got ten euros". And it's **je n'ai ni frère ni sœur**, never *je n'ai pas ni frère ni sœur*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen for the que">
  In everyday speech, people often drop *ne*: you'll hear *J'ai que dix euros* and *Il reste que deux billets*. When you hear *que* right after a verb, before an amount or a noun, think "only". Spotting it will save you from thinking someone has *nothing* when they really have *a little*.
</Callout>
