By the end of this lesson you'll be able to show how sure you are, choosing the indicative for certainty and the subjunctive for doubt.

## Sure: the indicative

When you're sure, or fairly sure, French uses the ordinary indicative after *que*. You've done this since <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-opinions" text="the lesson on expressing opinions" />: *je pense que*, *je crois que*, *je trouve que*.

<VocabTable group="certain" />

<Example fr="Je suis sûr qu'elle viendra." en="I'm sure she'll come." />
<Example fr="Je suis certain que c'est vrai." en="I'm certain it's true." />
<Example fr="Il est évident que tu es fatigué." en="It's obvious you're tired." />
<Example fr="Il est clair qu'il a raison." en="It's clear he's right." />

## Not sure: the subjunctive

When you doubt something, or say you don't think it's true, French switches to the subjunctive.

<VocabTable group="doubt" />

<Example fr="Je doute que ce soit vrai." en="I doubt it's true." />
<Example fr="Je ne pense pas qu'il ait raison." en="I don't think he's right." />
<Example fr="Je ne crois pas que nous ayons le temps." en="I don't think we have time." />
<Example fr="Je ne suis pas sûr qu'elle vienne." en="I'm not sure she'll come." />

In <LessonLink id="b1-agreeing-disagreeing" text="the lesson on agreeing and disagreeing" />, you used *je ne suis pas sûr* on its own. Now you can finish the sentence. Notice that the subjunctive also covers the future: *qu'elle vienne* can mean "she's coming" or "she'll come".

*Douter* is the odd one out: it takes the subjunctive even in the affirmative, because doubt is its meaning. *Douter de* is followed by a noun: *Je doute de son histoire* (I doubt his story).

## The same verb, two moods

The verbs of opinion change mood when they become negative. Compare:

| Indicative (you think it's true) | Subjunctive (you don't) |
|---|---|
| *Je pense qu'il vient.* | *Je ne pense pas qu'il vienne.* |
| *Je crois que c'est possible.* | *Je ne crois pas que ce soit possible.* |
| *Je suis sûr qu'elle sait.* | *Je ne suis pas sûr qu'elle sache.* |
| *Je suis certain qu'ils ont raison.* | *Je doute qu'ils aient raison.* |

With many *-er* verbs, the two forms sound the same, so you can't hear the difference: *je pense qu'il arrive* / *je ne pense pas qu'il arrive*. With *être*, *avoir*, *aller*, *faire* and the other irregular verbs, the difference is clear.

## Polite doubt

The subjunctive after a negative opinion is also a polite way to disagree, softer than *tu as tort*, from <LessonLink id="b1-agreeing-disagreeing" text="the lesson on agreeing and disagreeing" />.

<Example fr="Je ne suis pas sûr que ce soit une bonne idée." en="I'm not sure it's a good idea." />
<Example fr="Je ne pense pas que ce soit le bon moment." en="I don't think it's the right time." />

Both sentences say "no" without saying it directly, which makes them useful at work and with people you don't know well.

## Without que

*Sûrement* and *certainement* say how sure you are without *que*, so they don't change the mood. *Sûrement* is weaker than its English look-alike: it often means "probably".

<VocabTable group="adverbs" />

<Example fr="Il viendra sûrement." en="He'll most likely come." />
<Example fr="C'est certainement le meilleur restaurant de la ville." en="It's certainly the best restaurant in town." />
<Example fr="Tu as une preuve ?" en="Have you got any proof?" />

## En français

Two friends have heard a rumour about their favourite café.

- *Tu sais que le café de la rue Victor-Hugo va fermer ?* (Do you know the café on Rue Victor-Hugo is going to close?)
- *Ce n'est pas vrai ! Je doute que ce soit vrai. Il y a toujours beaucoup de gens.* (No way! I doubt that's true. There are always lots of people.)
- *Je ne suis pas sûre, mais Karim l'a lu dans le journal.* (I'm not sure, but Karim read it in the paper.)
- *Dans le journal ? Il a une preuve ?* (In the paper? Has he got any proof?)
- *Non. Mais il est certain que Marc veut partir. Tu sais, l'homme du café.* (No. But he's certain that Marc wants to leave. You know, the café man.)
- *Je ne crois pas qu'il parte. Il adore son café !* (I don't think he'll leave. He loves his café!)
- *C'est vrai. Il le vend sûrement à quelqu'un d'autre.* (That's true. He's probably selling it to someone else.)
- *Alors il est clair que le café ne ferme pas !* (Then it's clear that the café isn't closing!)
- *Bon, on va lui demander demain.* (OK, we'll ask him tomorrow.)

<Callout type="warning" title="The negative changes the mood">
  *Je pense qu'il a raison* takes the indicative, but **je ne pense pas qu'il ait raison** takes the subjunctive. It's easy to keep *a* in the negative because you're copying the positive sentence. Each time you write *ne… pas* in front of *penser*, *croire* or *être sûr*, change the verb after *que*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The verb tells you even without ne">
  In fast speech, French speakers often drop the *ne*: *Je pense pas qu'il vienne.* When you're listening, the subjunctive (*vienne*, *soit*, *ait*) still tells you the speaker is doubting. Listen for these forms in films and conversations: they're a clue to the meaning even when you miss the start of the sentence.
</Callout>
