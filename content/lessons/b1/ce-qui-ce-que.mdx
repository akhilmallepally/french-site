By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say "what" in the middle of a sentence, as in "what I like" or "everything you say", and put the most important part of an opinion at the end.

## From qui and que to ce qui and ce que

In <LessonLink id="a2-relative-qui-que-ou" text="the lesson on qui, que and où" />, *qui* and *que* came after a noun: *le livre que je lis* (the book I'm reading). Sometimes there's no noun, because you mean "the thing that", or simply "what". French then puts *ce* in front.

<VocabTable group="core" />

The choice works exactly as it did with nouns. Look at what comes next.

- **Ce qui** is the subject, so a verb follows. An object pronoun such as *me* or *te* can come in between.
- **Ce que** is the object, so a subject and a verb follow. Before a vowel, *que* becomes *qu'*.

<Example fr="Je ne comprends pas ce que tu dis." en="I don't understand what you're saying." />
<Example fr="Ce qui m'intéresse, c'est l'histoire." en="What interests me is history." />
<Example fr="Ce qu'il veut, c'est dormir." en="What he wants is to sleep." />
<Example fr="Prends ce que tu veux." en="Take what you want." />

*Ce* is masculine and singular, so the verb after *ce qui* is in the *il* form: *ce qui compte*, *ce qui me plaît*. In the passé composé, the participle after *ce que* doesn't agree: *ce que j'ai vu*, *ce que nous avons fait*.

## Putting the important part last

The most useful pattern splits a sentence in two. The first half says what kind of thing you mean, and *c'est* then brings the important part to the end, where the listener notices it most.

<VocabTable group="frames" />

<Example fr="Ce qui me plaît, c'est la musique." en="What I like is the music." />
<Example fr="Ce que je préfère, c'est le dimanche." en="What I prefer is Sunday." />
<Example fr="Ce qui compte, c'est la vérité." en="What matters is the truth." />

Compare *La musique me plaît* (I like the music) with *Ce qui me plaît, c'est la musique* (What I like is the music). The second one tells the listener that the music is what counts for you, perhaps more than anything else.

## Verbs that react

These verbs describe how something affects you. The thing is the subject and you are the object, so they go naturally with *ce qui*.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

<Example fr="Ce qui m'énerve, c'est la pluie." en="What annoys me is the rain." />
<Example fr="Ce qui me surprend, c'est le prix." en="What surprises me is the price." />
<Example fr="Ce qui me gêne, c'est la télé le soir." en="What bothers me is the TV in the evening." />

*Plaire* works the opposite way round from "like", just like *manquer* in *tu me manques*: *Ce film me plaît* means "I like this film". In the *il* form, it takes a circumflex: *il plaît*. *Surprendre* is built on *prendre*, so it follows the same pattern: *ça me surprend*, *ils me surprennent*.

## Tout ce qui, tout ce que

Put *tout* in front to mean "everything that" or "all that".

<Example fr="Tout ce que tu dis est vrai." en="Everything you say is true." />
<Example fr="Tout ce qui est sur la table est pour toi." en="Everything on the table is for you." />
<Example fr="C'est tout ce que je sais." en="That's all I know." />

## En français

Two friends are leaving a concert.

- *Alors, qu'est-ce que tu en penses ?* (So, what do you think of it?)
- *Ce qui m'a plu, c'est la guitare. C'était génial.* (What I liked was the guitar. It was brilliant.)
- *Tu trouves ? Moi, ce que j'ai préféré, c'est la dernière chanson.* (Do you think so? What I liked best was the last song.)
- *Oui, elle est super. Mais ce qui m'a énervé, c'est l'homme derrière nous.* (Yes, it's great. But what annoyed me was the man behind us.)
- *L'homme derrière nous ? Pourquoi ?* (The man behind us? Why?)
- *Il parlait pendant toutes les chansons !* (He was talking during all the songs!)
- *C'est vrai. Et ce qui m'a surpris, c'est le prix de l'eau.* (That's true. And what surprised me was the price of the water.)
- *Huit euros pour un verre d'eau ! Ce qui compte, c'est la musique, bien sûr…* (Eight euros for a glass of water! What matters is the music, of course…)

## What I love about Lyon

Here's a short paragraph from a blog.

*Ce que j'aime à Lyon, c'est la vieille ville et ses petites rues. Ce qui me plaît aussi, c'est le marché du dimanche : on trouve tout ce qu'on veut. Ce qui m'énerve, ce sont les voitures le matin. Mais ce qui compte pour moi, c'est mon quartier. Les gens sont sympas, et tout ce qui est important est à côté de chez moi.*

(What I love about Lyon is the old town and its little streets. What I also like is the Sunday market: you find everything you want. What annoys me is the cars in the morning. But what matters to me is my neighbourhood. The people are nice, and everything important is near my place.)

<Callout type="warning" title="No qu'est-ce que in the middle">
  *Qu'est-ce que* starts a question: *Qu'est-ce que tu fais ?* In the middle of a sentence, "what" is **ce que**: **J'aime ce que tu fais**, not *j'aime qu'est-ce que tu fais*. In the same way, *qu'est-ce qui* becomes **ce qui**: **Je regarde ce qui se passe** (I'm watching what's happening).
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Save the best for last">
  When you want people to remember one point, build up to it. Start with *Ce qui compte, c'est…* or *Ce qui me plaît, c'est…*, pause for a moment, then say the key word. In speech, the first half also gives you a second or two to find the right word.
</Callout>
