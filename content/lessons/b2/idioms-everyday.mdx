By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand and use 21 very common French idioms, and to know which ones are fine at work and which are only for friends.

## How idioms work

<VocabTable group="other" />

An idiom, *une expression imagée*, means more than the sum of its words: *il pleut des cordes* is literally "it's raining ropes", but it simply means "it's pouring". You can't translate idioms word for word in either direction, and many English idioms have a French partner that uses a completely different picture. You already know a few phrases of this kind, such as *tomber amoureux*, from <LessonLink id="b1-relationships" text="the lesson on relationships" />.

Two things matter when you use an idiom. First, the verb changes as usual, but the rest stays fixed: *j'ai la flemme*, *il avait la flemme*. Second, each idiom has a register. In the vocab tables, *neutral* means you can use it with almost anyone, and *informal* means friends and family only.

<Example fr="Je suis dans la lune ce matin, je n'ai pas assez dormi." en="I'm daydreaming this morning, I didn't sleep enough." />
<Example fr="J'ai la flemme de faire la cuisine ce soir." en="I can't be bothered to cook tonight." />
<Example fr="Elle a eu un coup de foudre pour cette maison." en="She fell in love with this house at first sight." />

*Avoir un coup de foudre* can be about a person, a place or an object. *Avoir la flemme* is followed by *de* + infinitive.

## The body

<VocabTable group="body" />

Many French idioms use parts of the body. Notice that *coûter les yeux de la tête* and *coûter un rein* both mean "to be very expensive", but *coûter un rein* (literally "to cost a kidney") is more informal and more recent.

<Example fr="Ce voyage m'a coûté les yeux de la tête." en="This trip cost me an arm and a leg." />
<Example fr="Arrête de me casser les pieds avec cette histoire !" en="Stop going on at me about that story!" />
<Example fr="Il a mis les pieds dans le plat en parlant de la fête surprise." en="He put his foot in it by mentioning the surprise party." />
<Example fr="Elle connaît tout le monde : elle a le bras long." en="She knows everyone: she has influence." />

<Example fr="Il ne fait jamais rien : il a un poil dans la main !" en="He never does anything: he's bone idle!" />
<Example fr="Je me suis creusé la tête, mais je n'ai pas trouvé la réponse." en="I racked my brains, but I didn't find the answer." />
<Example fr="Elle a fini ses devoirs en un clin d'œil." en="She finished her homework in the blink of an eye." />

*Casser les pieds à quelqu'un* is informal, and close to rude when said directly: use it to describe someone, not to their face. *Faire la tête* (to sulk) is very common with children and partners: *Pourquoi tu fais la tête ?*

## Food

<VocabTable group="food" />

<Example fr="Je ne suis pas dans mon assiette aujourd'hui." en="I'm feeling a bit off today." />
<Example fr="Arrête de raconter des salades, je sais que c'est faux." en="Stop telling stories, I know it's not true." />

*Ne pas être dans son assiette* has nothing to do with plates: it means you feel slightly unwell or not yourself. The possessive changes with the person: *je ne suis pas dans mon assiette*, *tu n'es pas dans ton assiette ?*

## Animals

<VocabTable group="animals" />

<Example fr="Je ne trouve pas la réponse : je donne ma langue au chat." en="I can't find the answer: I give up." />
<Example fr="Il faut appeler un chat un chat : ce projet ne marche pas." en="Let's call a spade a spade: this project isn't working." />

*Donner sa langue au chat* is what you say when you give up on a riddle or a guess. *Avoir le cafard* (literally "to have the cockroach") means to feel down, and has nothing to do with insects in modern French.

## Using idioms well

Idioms make your French sound natural, but only in the right place. Use one or two in a conversation, not five in a row, or you'll sound as if you're reciting a list. Keep the informal ones, such as *coûter un rein* or *avoir la flemme*, for friends and family; in a job interview or a formal email, choose a plain sentence instead (*c'est très cher*, *je n'ai pas le temps*). Some idioms also sound old-fashioned to young speakers, so listen to how people around you talk before you adopt a new one. Finally, check the small words: it's *poser un lapin à quelqu'un* and *casser les pieds à quelqu'un*, with *à*, and *avoir la flemme de* + infinitive.

## En français

Zoé finds her flatmate Malik on the sofa on a Sunday evening.

- *Ça ne va pas ? Tu n'es pas dans ton assiette ?* (What's wrong? Are you feeling off?)
- *Non, j'ai le cafard. Quelle semaine !* (No, I'm feeling down. What a week!)
- *Qu'est-ce qui s'est passé ?* (What happened?)
- *D'abord, Hugo m'a posé un lapin samedi. J'ai attendu une heure au café.* (First, Hugo stood me up on Saturday. I waited an hour at the café.)
- *Ce n'est pas sympa. Il t'a appelé après ?* (That's not nice. Did he call you afterwards?)
- *Oui, mais il m'a raconté des salades : il dit qu'il a oublié son portable.* (Yes, but he told me some story: he says he forgot his phone.)
- *Et au travail ?* (And at work?)
- *J'ai du pain sur la planche, et ma patronne me casse les pieds.* (I've got a lot on my plate, and my boss is getting on my nerves.)
- *Tu devrais lui parler et appeler un chat un chat.* (You should talk to her and call a spade a spade.)
- *Je sais, mais j'ai la flemme de lui parler cette semaine.* (I know, but I can't be bothered to talk to her this week.)
- *Bon, ce soir, on sort ? Il ne pleut plus des cordes.* (Right, shall we go out tonight? It's not pouring any more.)
- *D'accord, bonne idée ! Allons-y.* (OK, good idea! Let's go.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Il pleut des cordes, not des chats">
  English idioms rarely translate word for word. *Il pleut des chats et des chiens* means nothing in French: say **il pleut des cordes**. In the same way, an expensive phone doesn't cost "an arm and a leg" but **les yeux de la tête**. When you're not sure of the French idiom, use a plain sentence instead: *il pleut beaucoup*, *ça coûte très cher*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Picture the idiom">
  For each idiom, imagine the literal picture for a second: a rabbit placed on a chair instead of your date, a rope of rain, a hair in your hand that stops you working. The strange image is easy to remember, and it brings the meaning back with it. Then use the idiom once that day, in a message to a friend.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Une expression imagée ne se traduit pas mot à mot. En français, il pleut des cordes, pas des chats ! Beaucoup d'expressions parlent de la tête, des yeux, des pieds ou de la main. D'autres parlent de ce qu'on mange, comme le pain, les pommes et les salades, ou des animaux, comme le chat et le lapin. Le verbe change, mais le reste de l'expression ne change pas. Beaucoup d'expressions sont du registre familier : on les dit avec des amis, pas dans une lettre formelle.
