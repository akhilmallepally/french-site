By the end of this lesson you'll be able to explain how healthcare works and is paid for in France, and follow a public debate about health.

## What you already know

You know *la carte Vitale*, *une ordonnance*, *le médecin traitant* and *un rendez-vous* from <LessonLink id="a2-at-the-doctor" text="the lesson at the doctor's" />, and symptoms from <LessonLink id="a2-body-and-health" text="the lesson on the body and health" />. You know *être remboursé* from <LessonLink id="b1-making-a-complaint" text="the lesson on making a complaint" />, and *se faire* + infinitive from <LessonLink id="b2-faire-causatif" text="the lesson on faire + infinitive" />. This lesson looks at the system behind the doctor's visit.

## Who pays

<VocabTable group="system" />

*La santé* is health, and *la santé publique* is the health of the whole population. In France, the cost of a visit to the doctor is shared. **La Sécurité sociale**, the state health insurance, pays part of it. Most people also have **une mutuelle**, a top-up insurance, often through their employer, which pays all or part of the rest. What remains for the patient is **le reste à charge**.

<Example fr="La Sécurité sociale rembourse une partie de la consultation." en="State health insurance reimburses part of the consultation." />
<Example fr="Avec une bonne mutuelle, le reste à charge est faible." en="With good top-up insurance, the out-of-pocket cost is low." />

*La prise en charge* means coverage (*la prise en charge des frais*), and also the care given to a patient (*la prise en charge des malades*).

## Doctors and hospitals

<VocabTable group="doctors" />

Each adult chooses *un médecin traitant*, usually *un généraliste* (a GP). Going through this doctor first (*passer par le médecin traitant*), before seeing *un spécialiste*, is called the coordinated care route, and it gives better reimbursement. For an emergency, people go to *les urgences* of a hospital.

**Une clinique** is a false friend: it's usually a private hospital, not a GP's surgery. You go *chez le médecin* or *chez le généraliste*. Doctors and *le personnel soignant* (nurses and carers) *soignent* (treat) patients. When you're ill and can't work, the doctor gives you *un arrêt maladie* (a sick note), which you send to your employer.

<Example fr="Mon généraliste m'a envoyé chez un spécialiste." en="My GP referred me to a specialist." />

<Example fr="Le personnel soignant travaille souvent la nuit." en="Healthcare staff often work at night." />

## Prevention

<VocabTable group="prevention" />

Public health relies on *la prévention*: *le dépistage* (screening), which finds *une maladie* early, and *les campagnes de vaccination*. *Se faire vacciner* (to get vaccinated) uses *se faire* + infinitive. *Une maladie chronique* is a long-term illness that needs regular care.

<Example fr="Le dépistage permet de trouver une maladie plus tôt." en="Screening makes it possible to find an illness earlier." />

## Public debates

<VocabTable group="debates" />

Health is a regular subject of public debate in France. Two topics come up often. *Les déserts médicaux* are areas, often rural, where there are too few doctors. *Les délais d'attente* (waiting times) to see some specialists can be long. People also discuss the workload of *le personnel soignant* and the cost of the system. When you talk about these subjects, the nuancing phrases from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing opinions" /> help you stay balanced.

<Example fr="Dans certaines régions, il faut attendre des mois pour voir un spécialiste." en="In some regions, you have to wait months to see a specialist." />

## One visit, step by step

You can now follow one visit through the whole system. Most of the words come from <LessonLink id="a2-at-the-doctor" text="the lesson at the doctor's" />; this lesson adds what happens to the money.

- First, you book: *je prends rendez-vous chez mon médecin traitant* (I make an appointment with my GP).
- At the end of the visit, you show your card: *je donne ma carte Vitale* (I hand over my carte Vitale).
- If you need medicine, the doctor writes a prescription: *il me donne une ordonnance* (he gives me a prescription).
- Then the reimbursement comes in two parts: *la Sécurité sociale me rembourse une partie, et ma mutuelle paie le reste* (state health insurance reimburses part of it, and my top-up insurance pays the rest).

If you skip the médecin traitant and go straight to a specialist, the first part is smaller. That's why the dialogue below starts with the choice of a GP.

## Talking about health carefully

Health is personal. In French, as in English, people don't usually ask colleagues about their illnesses; *j'étais en arrêt maladie* is enough. In debates, keep to facts and avoid general claims about groups of patients or doctors. And for any question about your own health, the right person to ask is a doctor.

## En français

Sarah has just arrived in France for work. She asks her colleague, Monsieur Martin, how healthcare works.

- *Je viens d'arriver en France. Comment est-ce que je choisis un médecin ?* (I've just arrived in France. How do I choose a doctor?)
- *Il faut déclarer un médecin traitant, en général un généraliste près de chez vous.* (You have to register a médecin traitant, usually a GP near where you live.)
- *Pourquoi c'est important ?* (Why is that important?)
- *Si vous passez par lui, la Sécurité sociale vous rembourse mieux.* (If you go through him, state health insurance reimburses you better.)
- *Et si j'ai besoin d'un spécialiste ?* (And if I need a specialist?)
- *Votre médecin traitant vous enverra chez un spécialiste. Mais pour certains, les délais d'attente sont longs.* (Your GP will refer you to a specialist. But for some, the waiting times are long.)
- *La Sécurité sociale paie tout ?* (Does state health insurance pay for everything?)
- *Non, une partie seulement. Pour le reste, beaucoup de gens ont une mutuelle, souvent par leur entreprise.* (No, only part of it. For the rest, many people have top-up insurance, often through their company.)
- *D'accord. Et si je suis malade, je dois prévenir le bureau ?* (OK. And if I'm ill, do I have to let the office know?)
- *Oui, le médecin vous donnera un arrêt maladie, et vous l'enverrez à votre employeur.* (Yes, the doctor will give you a sick note, and you'll send it to your employer.)
- *Merci, c'est plus clair. Je vais chercher un généraliste dès demain.* (Thank you, that's clearer. I'll look for a GP tomorrow.)

<Callout type="warning" title="La clinique isn't the GP">
  English speakers say "I'm going to the clinic" for a GP's surgery. In France, *une clinique* is usually a private hospital. For your GP, say **je vais chez le médecin** or **je vais chez mon généraliste**.
</Callout>

</Callout>

## En résumé

En France, chaque adulte choisit un médecin traitant, en général un généraliste. Quand on passe par lui avant de voir un spécialiste, on est mieux remboursé. Une consultation est payée en partie par la Sécurité sociale. La mutuelle paie souvent le reste, et ce qui reste au patient s'appelle le reste à charge. Pour un problème grave, on va aux urgences. Attention : on ne va pas voir son généraliste dans une clinique : une clinique est en général un hôpital privé. La santé publique repose aussi sur la prévention, avec le dépistage et les campagnes de vaccination. Parmi les débats, on parle souvent des déserts médicaux, où il y a trop peu de médecins, et des délais d'attente.
