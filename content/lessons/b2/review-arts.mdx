## A few new words

These five words help you place a work in its time.

<VocabTable />

*Le contexte historique* is the period a work comes from: what was happening when it was made. *Une œuvre engagée* defends a political or social idea; you know *engagé* from <LessonLink id="b2-culture-french-press" text="the lesson on the French press" />. A work that *a marqué son temps* changed how people of its period saw things. *Son héritage* is what it leaves to later artists. And a work that still speaks to us today is *intemporelle*.

<Example fr="Pour comprendre ce roman, il faut connaître son contexte historique." en="To understand this novel, you need to know its historical context." />
<Example fr="Cette chanson engagée a marqué son temps." en="This committed song left its mark on its time." />
<Example fr="Son héritage est encore visible chez les jeunes peintres." en="Her legacy can still be seen in young painters' work." />
<Example fr="Ce poème parle d'amour : il est intemporel." en="This poem is about love: it's timeless." />

## Context before judgement

A critical description now has four steps: the context, the description, the interpretation and the judgement. Put the context first, in one or two sentences, with a date and an event. Then describe, interpret and judge, as in the lesson on describing a work of art.

- To give the date, use the passive in the passé composé: *ce tableau a été peint en 1946*. In a museum guide or an art book, you may read the passé simple instead: *il fut peint en 1946*.
- To link the work to its period, use *le contexte historique* and a connector: *par conséquent*, *en effet*.
- To judge its importance, use *marquer son temps*, *un héritage* or *intemporel*.

Keep the context short. A reader wants to understand the work, not read a history lesson: one event that explains the work is enough.

## A model description

This is an original critical description of an invented painting. The painter, Jean Varenne, and the painting are invented.

*« Après la pluie », Jean Varenne, 1946*

*Jean Varenne peint ce tableau en 1946, peu après la Seconde Guerre mondiale. Le contexte historique est important : la France sort de l'Occupation, et beaucoup de familles n'ont plus rien.*

*Au premier plan, une jeune femme et un enfant marchent dans une rue vide. Tous les deux regardent l'arrière-plan, où une lumière vive arrive entre deux bâtiments anciens. Les couleurs sont sombres, mais cette seule touche de lumière change tout. Il s'en dégage une impression de paix.*

*Certes, ce n'est pas une œuvre engagée : Varenne ne défend pas d'idée politique. Pourtant, ce tableau marque son temps : pour beaucoup de Français, la lumière symbolise la Libération et la fin de la guerre.*

*Aujourd'hui encore, la toile semble intemporelle. Son héritage est clair : après lui, beaucoup de jeunes peintres choisissent des couleurs sombres et une seule touche de lumière. Tout compte fait, c'est une simple scène de rue, mais c'est un vrai chef-d'œuvre.*

("After the Rain", Jean Varenne, 1946. Jean Varenne paints this picture in 1946, shortly after the Second World War. The historical context is important: France is coming out of the Occupation, and many families have nothing left. In the foreground, a young woman and a child are walking down an empty street. Both of them are looking at the background, where a bright light comes between two old buildings. The colours are dark, but this single touch of light changes everything. An impression of peace emerges from it. Admittedly, it isn't a committed work: Varenne doesn't defend any political idea. And yet this painting leaves its mark on its time: for many French people, the light symbolizes the Liberation and the end of the war. Even today, the canvas seems timeless. Its legacy is clear: after him, many young painters choose dark colours and a single touch of light. All things considered, it's just a street scene, but it's a real masterpiece.)

## How the text uses the grammar

- The context comes first, with a date and the history from the lesson on French history: *la Seconde Guerre mondiale*, *l'Occupation*, *la Libération*.
- The whole text uses the present, as critics and history books often do: *peint*, *sort*, *marque*, *symbolise*.
- The description follows the space of the picture: *au premier plan*, *l'arrière-plan*.
- *Tout* appears as a pronoun (*change tout*), in *tous les deux* (both), and in the set phrase *tout compte fait*.
- Adjective position changes the meaning: *bâtiments anciens* (old), *une seule touche* (only one), *une simple scène* (just a scene), *un vrai chef-d'œuvre* (a real one).
- The judgement is nuanced with *certes* and *pourtant*, then closes with the new words *marqué son temps*, *intemporelle* and *héritage*.

## Your turn

Choose a work you know: a painting, a song, a novel or a film. Write a short critical description of five parts, on paper or in a notes app.

1. **Context**, in one or two sentences, with a date and *le contexte historique*.
2. **Description**, with *au premier plan*, *à l'arrière-plan* or *au centre*, or the subject of the work.
3. **Interpretation**, with *évoquer*, *symboliser* or *il s'en dégage*.
4. **Judgement**, with *certes… mais* and a precise adjective, such as *frappant*, *saisissant* or *sobre*.
5. **Importance**, with *marquer son temps*, *un héritage* or *intemporel*.

## Check your work

- Did you put the context first, and keep it short?
- Are the adjectives in the right place for the meaning you want (*un ancien ami*, *un bâtiment ancien*)?
- Does *tout* agree when it's a determiner (*toute la ville*), and is *tous* used for a mixed group?
- Did you use the passé composé, not the passé simple, in what you wrote?
- Does each interpretation point to a detail of the work?

<Callout type="warning" title="Une œuvre engagée, not engageante">
  For a work that defends an idea, learners sometimes say *une œuvre engageante*, which means "an appealing work". The word for a committed work is **engagée**: **une œuvre engagée**, **un artiste engagé**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Find one date before you write">
  Before you write about a work, look up just one fact: the year it was made, and one event of that year. Write that sentence first. With the context fixed, the description and the judgement become easier, because you know what the work was answering.
</Callout>
