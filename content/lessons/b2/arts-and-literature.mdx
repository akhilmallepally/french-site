By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about painting, theatre, poetry and literary movements, and recognize the passé simple when you read a story.

## What you already know

You know *un roman*, *un auteur / une autrice*, *un personnage* and *une scène* from <LessonLink id="b1-cinema-and-books" text="the lesson on cinema and books" />, and how to give your opinion of a book from <LessonLink id="b1-reviewing-a-film-or-book" text="the lesson on reviewing a film or book" />. This lesson adds painting, theatre and poetry, and a past tense you'll meet in books.

## Painting

<VocabTable group="painting" />

*Un tableau* is a painting. You know the word from <LessonLink id="b2-presenting-data" text="the lesson on presenting data" /> as a table of figures; in a museum, it usually means a picture. *Une toile* is the canvas, and by extension the painting itself. *Une œuvre* is any work of art: a painting, a book, a piece of music. The best ones are *des chefs-d'œuvre* (masterpieces), with the plural *s* on *chef*.

<Example fr="Le peintre a peint ce tableau en une semaine." en="The painter painted this picture in a week." />
<Example fr="Cette exposition présente les toiles d'une jeune peintre." en="This exhibition shows the canvases of a young painter." />
<Example fr="Pour moi, cette œuvre est un chef-d'œuvre." en="For me, this work is a masterpiece." />

*Peindre* is irregular: *il peint*, *ils peignent*, and *il a peint* in the passé composé.

## Theatre

<VocabTable group="theatre" />

*Une pièce de théâtre* is a play. You know *une pièce* as a room and a coin. *La mise en scène* is the way a play is staged, and *le metteur en scène* is the person who stages it.

**Un comédien** is a false friend: it's an actor, especially on stage. Someone who tells jokes is *un humoriste*.

<Example fr="Les comédiens jouent cette pièce depuis trois mois." en="The actors have been performing this play for three months." />
<Example fr="La metteuse en scène a choisi une mise en scène très simple." en="The director chose a very simple staging." />

## Poetry

<VocabTable group="poetry" />

A poem is made of lines, *des vers*, grouped in *des strophes*. **Un vers** is a false friend too: it's one line, not a whole verse. The lines often end with *une rime*. Poems are published together in *un recueil*.

<Example fr="Chaque strophe de ce poème a quatre vers." en="Each verse of this poem has four lines." />
<Example fr="Les deux derniers vers ont la même rime." en="The last two lines have the same rhyme." />

## Stories

<VocabTable group="stories" />

Besides novels, you'll read *des contes* (tales, often for children) and *des nouvelles* (short stories). You know *une nouvelle* as a piece of news; in a bookshop, it's a short story. The person who tells the story is *le narrateur*, who isn't always the author. *Un thème* is what a work is about.

<Example fr="Le narrateur de cette nouvelle est un enfant." en="The narrator of this short story is a child." />
<Example fr="L'amour est le thème principal de ce conte." en="Love is the main theme of this tale." />

## Movements

<VocabTable group="movements" />

*Un mouvement artistique* is a group of artists who share ideas at the same time. Three are worth knowing by name.

- In the early 19th century, *le romantisme* put feelings and nature at the centre, in poetry, novels and painting.
- In the late 19th century, *l'impressionnisme* was above all a painting movement: painters worked outside and tried to show light and movement.
- In the 1920s, *le surréalisme* looked for the world of dreams, in poetry and painting.

<Example fr="Ce mouvement artistique a changé la peinture." en="This artistic movement changed painting." />
<Example fr="Le surréalisme s'intéresse aux rêves." en="Surrealism is interested in dreams." />

## Recognizing the passé simple

<VocabTable group="ps" />

In novels, tales and history books, you'll meet a past tense that people don't use in speech: *le passé simple*. It means the same as the passé composé (a finished action), but it belongs to written, literary French. At B2 you only need to recognize it, mostly in the third person.

The endings tell you which verb you're reading:

| Verb type | *il / elle* | *ils / elles* | Passé composé |
|---|---|---|---|
| *-er* verbs (*parler*) | *il parla* | *ils parlèrent* | *il a parlé* |
| most *-ir* and *-re* verbs (*partir*, *attendre*) | *il partit*, *il attendit* | *ils partirent*, *ils attendirent* | *il est parti*, *il a attendu* |
| many other verbs (*vouloir*, *connaître*) | *il voulut*, *il connut* | *ils voulurent*, *ils connurent* | *il a voulu*, *il a connu* |

A few very common verbs are irregular, and you'll see them on almost every page:

- For *être*, the forms are *il fut* and *ils furent*.
- For *avoir*, they are *il eut* and *ils eurent*.
- For *faire*, they are *il fit* and *ils firent*.
- For *venir*, they are *il vint* and *ils vinrent*.
- For *prendre*, they are *il prit* and *ils prirent*.

Here is the opening of an invented tale. The imparfait sets the scene, and the passé simple tells what happened.

<Example fr="Il était une fois un roi qui vivait dans un grand château." en="Once upon a time, there was a king who lived in a big castle." />
<Example fr="Un jour, le roi partit seul dans la forêt." en="One day, the king went off alone into the forest." />
<Example fr="Il rencontra une vieille femme et lui parla pendant une heure." en="He met an old woman and spoke to her for an hour." />
<Example fr="Quand il revint au château, la reine et leurs enfants l'attendaient devant la porte." en="When he came back to the castle, the queen and their children were waiting for him at the door." />

*Revint* is the passé simple of *revenir*, which is built on *venir* (*il vint*). Two things help when you read. First, the passé simple and the imparfait work together exactly like the passé composé and the imparfait. Second, some forms look like the present: *il finit* can be present or passé simple, and only the context tells you which.

## En français

Clara and Malik leave an exhibition and plan their weekend.

- *Alors, cette exposition, tu as aimé ?* (So, did you like the exhibition?)
- *Beaucoup. Les toiles de ce peintre sont magnifiques.* (A lot. This painter's canvases are magnificent.)
- *Pour moi, le grand tableau près de la sortie est un vrai chef-d'œuvre.* (For me, the big painting near the exit is a real masterpiece.)
- *C'est vrai. Tu veux aller au théâtre samedi ?* (That's true. Do you want to go to the theatre on Saturday?)
- *Oui ! Il y a une nouvelle pièce au théâtre du centre-ville.* (Yes! There's a new play at the theatre in the town centre.)
- *Qui est le metteur en scène ?* (Who's the director?)
- *Je ne sais pas, mais la mise en scène est très moderne, d'après les journaux.* (I don't know, but the staging is very modern, according to the papers.)
- *Et les comédiens, ils sont bons ?* (And the actors, are they good?)
- *Excellents. Mais ce n'est pas une pièce drôle : le thème, c'est l'amour impossible.* (Excellent. But it isn't a funny play: the theme is impossible love.)
- *Pas de problème. Je réserve deux billets ce soir ?* (No problem. Shall I book two tickets tonight?)
- *Oui, merci ! Et samedi, je t'apporte mon recueil de poèmes préféré.* (Yes, thanks! And on Saturday, I'll bring you my favourite collection of poems.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Un comédien is an actor">
  *Un comédien* looks like "a comedian", but it means an actor, often on stage: *ce comédien joue dans une pièce de Molière*. For someone who tells jokes, say **un humoriste**: **c'est un humoriste très drôle**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the verb ending first">
  When a sentence in a novel looks strange, find the verb and read its ending before anything else. An *-a* added straight to the verb stem (*parl-a*), or the endings *-èrent*, *-irent* and *-urent*, tell you it's the passé simple. On its own, *-a* proves nothing: *il a* and *il va* are present, and *il parlera* is future. Replace it in your head with the passé composé (*il parla* = *il a parlé*), and the sentence becomes ordinary French.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Pour parler de peinture, on dit un tableau, une toile, une œuvre et un chef-d'œuvre. Un peintre peint des tableaux, et un musée présente une exposition. Au théâtre, on voit une pièce de théâtre. Le metteur en scène choisit la mise en scène, et les comédiens jouent. Attention : un comédien n'est pas un humoriste. Un poème a des strophes, une strophe a des vers, et les vers ont souvent une rime. Dans un recueil, il y a des poèmes ou des nouvelles. Le romantisme, l'impressionnisme et le surréalisme sont des mouvements artistiques. Enfin, le passé simple est un temps des livres, pas de la conversation. Il est facile à voir dans un texte : il parla, ils parlèrent, il partit, il fut.
