By the end of this lesson you'll know where and how French is used in West and Central Africa and the Maghreb, and you'll recognize some local words, writers and traditions.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="a2-culture-francophone-world" text="the lesson on the French-speaking world" />, you met *le Sénégal*, *la Côte d'Ivoire*, *le Cameroun* and the Maghreb, and you learned that many Senegalese people also speak Wolof. In <LessonLink id="b1-culture-quebec" text="the lesson on Quebec" />, you saw that French has its own words and accents outside France. This lesson looks at how French lives beside other languages in Africa, without repeating the list of countries.

## French beside other languages

<VocabTable group="languages" />

In many African countries, French is *une langue officielle*: the language of the State, the law, the administration and, often, school. But it's rarely the only language. Beside it, there are *des langues nationales*, the languages of the country's peoples, which families speak at home.

**Le Sénégal.** French is the official language, and Wolof is the most widely spoken national language. For many people, French is *une langue seconde*: learned at school and used at work, after the language of the family.

**La Côte d'Ivoire.** French is the official language, and many local languages are spoken. In Abidjan, French is also the everyday language of the street.

**Le Cameroun.** The country is officially *bilingue*, in French and English, with many local languages too.

Speaking two, three or four languages is normal in these countries. This *plurilinguisme* is part of daily life: people change language with the situation, sometimes inside one conversation.

<Example fr="Au Cameroun, le français et l'anglais sont des langues officielles." en="In Cameroon, French and English are official languages." />
<Example fr="Beaucoup de Sénégalais parlent wolof en famille et français au travail." en="Many Senegalese people speak Wolof at home and French at work." />

The place of French in Africa comes from the colonial period, which you saw in <LessonLink id="b2-culture-french-history" text="the lesson on French history" />. After independence, many countries kept French as one of their official languages.

## The Maghreb

In Algeria, Morocco and Tunisia, the official languages are Arabic and, in Algeria and Morocco, Tamazight (Berber). French isn't an official language there, but it remains widely used in education, business, science and the media. Many people move between Arabic, French and sometimes Tamazight every day.

<Example fr="Au Maroc, le français n'est pas une langue officielle, mais on le parle souvent au travail." en="In Morocco, French isn't an official language, but it's often spoken at work." />

## When languages meet

<VocabTable group="contact" />

*Le contact des langues* changes French. Local languages give French *des emprunts* (loanwords), and French gives words to local languages. Sometimes a whole new way of speaking appears from *un mélange* of languages.

**Le nouchi** was born in the streets of Abidjan. It mixes French with Ivorian languages and English, and it's now used by many young people and in music.

**Le camfranglais**, in Cameroon, mixes French, English and local languages. It's above all a language of young people in the cities.

<Example fr="Le nouchi est un mélange de français, de langues ivoiriennes et d'anglais." en="Nouchi is a mix of French, Ivorian languages and English." />

## Labelled local words

<VocabTable group="local" />

You'll meet *des mots locaux* that people in France may not know. Each one belongs to a country or a region, so use it with its label, as you did with Quebec words.

- In Senegal, a petrol station is *une essencerie*, from *essence* (petrol), where France says *une station-service*.
- In Côte d'Ivoire, *s'enjailler* (from the English "enjoy") means to have fun; it's informal.
- In much of West and Central Africa, *un taxi-brousse* is a shared taxi that travels between towns.

<Example fr="On s'arrête à l'essencerie avant de partir ?" en="Shall we stop at the petrol station before we leave? (Senegal, spoken)" />
<Example fr="Nous avons pris un taxi-brousse pour aller au village." en="We took a bush taxi to get to the village." />

In an exam or a formal letter, use standard French. You'll see how to choose your register in <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />.

## Literature and music

<VocabTable group="culture" />

**La négritude** is a literary movement born in Paris in the 1930s, among black students from Africa and the Caribbean writing in French. They wanted to show the value of black cultures and histories. One of its best-known poets, **Léopold Sédar Senghor**, was Senegalese; he later became the first president of independent Senegal, and the first African member of the Académie française.

<Example fr="La négritude est un mouvement littéraire des années 1930." en="Négritude is a literary movement of the 1930s." />

**Les griots** are West African storytellers and musicians. They keep the history of families and villages, and tell it in words and songs. Today, many African artists sing in French, in local languages, or in both, and their music is popular across the French-speaking world.

<Example fr="Le griot chante l'histoire de la famille pendant la fête." en="The griot sings the family's history during the celebration." />

## En français

Awa, a student from Abidjan, explains an Ivorian expression to her classmate Lucas.

- *Awa, hier tu m'as écrit « je vais m'enjailler ». Ça veut dire quoi ?* (Awa, yesterday you wrote "je vais m'enjailler" to me. What does it mean?)
- *C'est du nouchi, le français de la rue à Abidjan. S'enjailler, c'est s'amuser.* (It's nouchi, the street French of Abidjan. S'enjailler means to have fun.)
- *Ça vient de l'anglais, non ?* (It comes from English, doesn't it?)
- *Oui, de « enjoy ». Le nouchi a beaucoup d'emprunts à l'anglais et aux langues ivoiriennes.* (Yes, from "enjoy". Nouchi has lots of loanwords from English and from Ivorian languages.)
- *Et tout le monde parle nouchi en Côte d'Ivoire ?* (And does everyone speak nouchi in Côte d'Ivoire?)
- *Surtout les jeunes. Mais à l'école et au travail, nous parlons le français standard.* (Mostly young people. But at school and at work, we speak standard French.)
- *Alors tu es bilingue ?* (So you're bilingual?)
- *Plus que ça ! Je parle aussi le dioula avec ma famille.* (More than that! I also speak Dioula with my family.)
- *Moi, je ne parle que le français, et un peu d'anglais.* (I only speak French, and a bit of English.)
- *Alors viens à Abidjan : mes amis vont t'apprendre quelques mots locaux !* (Then come to Abidjan: my friends will teach you a few local words!)
- *D'accord, mais d'abord, apprends-moi à m'enjailler !* (OK, but first, teach me how to have fun!)

## A magazine piece

This is an original magazine piece about French in Africa.

*Le français d'Afrique, une langue vivante*

*Aujourd'hui, la francophonie est de plus en plus africaine. Du Sénégal au Cameroun, le français est une langue officielle, mais il vit à côté de beaucoup de langues nationales. Pour beaucoup de gens, c'est une langue seconde : on la parle à l'école et au travail, et on garde sa langue de famille à la maison.*

*Ce plurilinguisme change le français. Dans les rues d'Abidjan, les jeunes parlent le nouchi, un mélange de français, de langues ivoiriennes et d'anglais. Au Cameroun, le camfranglais joue le même rôle. Ces façons de parler donnent au français des mots nouveaux, comme « s'enjailler » ou « une essencerie ».*

*Dans les années 1930, à Paris, des étudiants africains et antillais lancèrent le mouvement de la négritude. Léopold Sédar Senghor, poète sénégalais, en fut l'une des grandes voix.*

*Aujourd'hui, le français d'Afrique a sa littérature, et la tradition orale reste forte : les griots racontent toujours l'histoire des familles. Grâce à ce contact des langues, le français continue de s'enrichir.*

Note: *lancèrent* (launched) and *fut* (was) are in the passé simple, the forms of *lancer* and *être*, from <LessonLink id="b2-arts-and-literature" text="the lesson on arts and literature" />.

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(The French of Africa, a living language. Today, the French-speaking world is more and more African. From Senegal to Cameroon, French is an official language, but it lives beside many national languages. For many people, it's a second language: they speak it at school and at work, and keep their family language at home. This multilingualism is changing French. In the streets of Abidjan, young people speak nouchi, a mix of French, Ivorian languages and English. In Cameroon, camfranglais plays the same role. These ways of speaking give French new words, such as "s'enjailler" or "une essencerie". In the 1930s, in Paris, African and Caribbean students launched the négritude movement. Léopold Sédar Senghor, a Senegalese poet, was one of its great voices. Today, the French of Africa has its literature, and the oral tradition is still strong: griots still tell the history of families. Thanks to this contact between languages, French keeps growing richer.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Langue officielle, not langue nationale">
  Learners often call French *la langue nationale* of Senegal or Côte d'Ivoire. French is the official language there; the national languages are the country's own languages. Write **le français est la langue officielle du Sénégal**, and **le wolof est une langue nationale**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen to French from another country">
  Once a week, listen to a radio programme, a podcast or songs from a French-speaking African country. Note one local word and its country, and check its standard French equivalent. Your ear will get used to other accents, and you'll understand more of the French-speaking world.
</Callout>
