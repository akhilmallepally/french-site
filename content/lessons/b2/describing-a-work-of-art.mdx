By the end of this lesson you'll be able to describe a painting, say what it suggests to you, and give a balanced judgement.

## What you already know

You gave your opinion of a film or a book in <LessonLink id="b1-reviewing-a-film-or-book" text="the lesson on reviewing a film or book" />, and you know *émouvant* from <LessonLink id="b1-cinema-and-books" text="the lesson on cinema and books" /> and *représenter* from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-french-republic" text="the lesson on the French Republic" />. You learned *un tableau*, *une toile* and *un chef-d'œuvre* in <LessonLink id="b2-arts-and-literature" text="the lesson on arts and literature" />, and adjectives that change meaning in <LessonLink id="b2-adjective-position-meaning" text="the lesson on adjectives that change meaning" />. This lesson gives you a method for talking about a work of art.

## Three steps

A good description of a work of art moves in three steps, and each step has its own language.

- First, describe what anyone can see: *au premier plan, une femme lit* (in the foreground, a woman is reading).
- Then, interpret what it suggests: *la lumière évoque le matin* (the light suggests the morning).
- Last, judge: *c'est une œuvre frappante* (it's a striking work).

Keeping the steps apart makes your opinion more convincing, because the reader can see what it's based on.

## Where things are

<VocabTable group="space" />

Start with the space of the picture. *Au premier plan* is what is closest to the viewer, *à l'arrière-plan* what is furthest away, and *au centre* the middle. For the sides, say *à gauche du tableau* or *à droite du tableau*, from the viewer's point of view.

<Example fr="Au premier plan, un enfant joue avec un chien." en="In the foreground, a child is playing with a dog." />
<Example fr="À l'arrière-plan, on voit une ville sous la pluie." en="In the background, you can see a town in the rain." />
<Example fr="À gauche du tableau, une porte est ouverte." en="On the left of the painting, a door is open." />

## Colour, light and the gaze

<VocabTable group="look" />

Then describe the look of the work. *Les couleurs vives* are bright, *les couleurs sombres* dark. *La lumière* can come from one side, or be soft or hard. *Une touche* is a single brushstroke: the impressionists painted in small *touches* of pure colour. *Le regard* is the look in a person's eyes, and *le spectateur* is the person looking at the work.

<Example fr="Le peintre choisit des couleurs sombres et peu de lumière." en="The painter uses dark colours and little light." />
<Example fr="Le regard de la jeune femme suit le spectateur." en="The young woman's gaze follows the viewer." />
<Example fr="On voit chaque touche de couleur sur la toile." en="You can see each brushstroke of colour on the canvas." />

## Saying what it suggests

<VocabTable group="meaning" />

Interpretation verbs let you go beyond what is visible. *Évoquer* means "to bring to mind", *suggérer* "to suggest" and *symboliser* "to stand for". You know *représenter* (to show, to depict), which describes rather than interprets. *Il s'en dégage* (there emerges from it) introduces the general feeling of a work; it's the idiomatic *en* you met in <LessonLink id="b2-idiomatic-y-en" text="the lesson on idiomatic y and en" />.

<Example fr="Cette lumière rouge évoque la fin d'une journée." en="This red light brings to mind the end of a day." />
<Example fr="La fenêtre ouverte symbolise la liberté." en="The open window stands for freedom." />
<Example fr="Il s'en dégage une impression de calme." en="An impression of calm emerges from it." />

Present an interpretation as yours, not as a fact: *selon moi*, *on peut penser que*, *cela suggère peut-être*. Several interpretations of one work can be valid, and *une interprétation* is stronger when it points to a detail of the picture.

<Example fr="Mon interprétation est différente : pour moi, cette femme attend quelqu'un." en="My interpretation is different: for me, this woman is waiting for someone." />

## Judging

<VocabTable group="judge" />

For the judgement, choose precise adjectives rather than *beau* or *bien*. *Frappant* (striking) is for a detail that catches the eye; *saisissant* (gripping) is stronger, for a work that holds you. *Sobre* is a compliment in art criticism: understated, with no unnecessary effects. You know *émouvant* (moving).

<Example fr="Le contraste entre les deux personnages est frappant." en="The contrast between the two figures is striking." />
<Example fr="C'est un portrait sobre, mais saisissant." en="It's an understated portrait, but a gripping one." />

To nuance a judgement, use the tools from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing opinions" /> and *certes… mais*: *certes, la composition est simple, mais la lumière est saisissante*. To put your main point first, use the mise en relief from <LessonLink id="b2-mise-en-relief" text="the lesson on mise en relief" />: *ce qui me plaît, c'est la lumière*.

Adjective position matters here too: *une curieuse lumière* is an odd light, with *curieuse* before the noun.

## En français

Clara and Malik stand in front of a large painting and don't agree.

- *Qu'est-ce que tu en penses ?* (What do you think of it?)
- *Franchement, je ne comprends pas. C'est juste une femme assise devant une fenêtre.* (Honestly, I don't get it. It's just a woman sitting in front of a window.)
- *Regarde le regard de la femme : elle attend quelque chose.* (Look at the woman's gaze: she's waiting for something.)
- *Peut-être. Mais les couleurs sont très sombres.* (Maybe. But the colours are very dark.)
- *Justement. Il s'en dégage une impression de solitude.* (Exactly. There's an impression of loneliness coming from it.)
- *Et la fenêtre ouverte, à gauche du tableau ?* (And the open window, on the left of the painting?)
- *Pour moi, elle symbolise la liberté, ou une vie qu'elle n'a pas.* (For me, it stands for freedom, or a life she doesn't have.)
- *C'est une interprétation. Moi, je vois juste une fenêtre !* (That's one interpretation. I just see a window!)
- *D'accord, mais avoue que la lumière est frappante.* (OK, but admit that the light is striking.)
- *Ça, oui. Le tableau est sobre, mais il est saisissant.* (That, yes. The painting is understated, but it's gripping.)
- *Tu vois, tu commences à l'aimer !* (You see, you're starting to like it!)

## An exhibition review

This is an original review of an invented painting by an invented painter, written in the style of a culture magazine.

*« L'Attente », une toile saisissante*

*La galerie du centre-ville présente cette semaine les toiles de Claire Morel. Au centre de l'exposition, on découvre « L'Attente », une grande toile aux couleurs sombres.*

*Au premier plan, une femme est assise devant une table vide. À gauche du tableau, une fenêtre ouverte laisse entrer une lumière froide. À l'arrière-plan, on voit une ville sous la pluie.*

*Le regard de la femme est frappant : elle ne regarde pas le spectateur, mais la fenêtre. La lumière évoque le matin, après une nuit sans sommeil. Selon nous, la fenêtre symbolise un départ, ou peut-être une personne qui ne reviendra pas. Il s'en dégage une impression de silence et d'attente.*

*Certes, la composition est très simple, et certains visiteurs la trouveront trop sobre. Mais c'est justement cette simplicité qu'on aime. Ce qu'on garde en mémoire, c'est ce regard.*

*Une œuvre à voir, pour quiconque aime la peinture qui raconte une histoire.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

("The Wait", a gripping canvas. The town-centre gallery is showing Claire Morel's canvases this week. At the centre of the exhibition is "The Wait", a large canvas in dark colours. In the foreground, a woman is sitting at an empty table. On the left of the painting, an open window lets in a cold light. In the background, you can see a town in the rain. The woman's gaze is striking: she isn't looking at the viewer, but at the window. The light brings to mind the morning, after a sleepless night. In our view, the window stands for a departure, or perhaps a person who won't come back. An impression of silence and waiting emerges from it. Admittedly, the composition is very simple, and some visitors will find it too understated. But it's precisely this simplicity that you love. What you remember is that gaze. A work to see, for anyone who loves painting that tells a story.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Il s'en dégage, not il en dégage">
  *Dégager* alone means "to bring out" or "to clear". For the feeling that comes from a work, the verb is pronominal: *il en dégage une impression de calme* is wrong. Write **il s'en dégage une impression de calme**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Describe for one minute before you judge">
  In front of a painting, force yourself to describe it in French for one full minute before you give any opinion: the foreground, the background, the colours, the light. Only then say what it evokes and what you think. Your judgement will be sharper, and you'll practise the most useful words.
</Callout>
