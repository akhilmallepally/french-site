By the end of this lesson you'll be able to choose a reporting verb that shows the speaker's attitude, and to shift time words when you report what was said at another time.

## What you already know

You know *dire que*, *répondre que*, *expliquer que* and *préciser* from <LessonLink id="b1-reported-speech-present" text="the lesson on reported speech" />, and *reconnaître que* from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing your opinion" />. *Dire* is neutral: it tells the reader what was said, but not how. Journalists rarely stop there. The verb they choose tells you whether the speaker was calm, firm, defensive or doubtful, and whether the journalist believes them.

## Neutral verbs

<VocabTable group="neutral" />

These verbs report without judging. *Annoncer* is for news, often about the future; *déclarer* is for an official statement. *Estimer que* gives an opinion, and *rappeler que* reminds the reader of a fact they may know already. You also know *ajouter* (to add) from <LessonLink id="b1-food-and-cooking" text="the lesson on food and cooking" />, where it was about recipes; in reporting, *il a ajouté que* introduces a second point.

<Example fr="Le maire a annoncé que la piscine ouvrirait en juin." en="The mayor announced that the pool would open in June." />
<Example fr="La ministre a rappelé que les députés avaient déjà voté la loi." en="The minister pointed out that the MPs had already passed the law." />

<Example fr="Le maire estime que le projet est trop cher." en="The mayor considers that the project is too expensive." />

*Citer* (to quote) is for exact words, which go between French quotation marks: *« … »*. *Une citation* is the quotation itself.

<Example fr="Le journaliste a commencé son article par une citation." en="The journalist began his article with a quotation." />

## Strong verbs

<VocabTable group="strong" />

*Affirmer* and *souligner* show that the speaker was firm, or wanted to make a point clear. *Insister sur* + noun means to stress something; you know *insister pour que* + subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b2-reported-questions-orders" text="the last lesson" />. *Dénoncer* and *critiquer* report an attack on someone's actions, and *mettre en garde contre* reports a warning.

<Example fr="Il a affirmé qu'il n'avait rien vu." en="He stated firmly that he hadn't seen anything." />
<Example fr="L'association dénonce les conditions de travail." en="The charity condemns the working conditions." />

<Example fr="Elle a insisté sur l'importance de la sécurité." en="She stressed the importance of safety." />
<Example fr="La police met en garde contre les faux mails." en="The police are warning against fake emails." />

## Doubt and denial

<VocabTable group="doubt" />

**Prétendre** is a false friend: it doesn't mean "to pretend". It means "to claim", and it suggests that the reporter doubts the claim. *Il prétend qu'il était malade* means "he claims he was ill", and the reader is invited to doubt it. *S'interroger sur* (to wonder about) raises a question without accusing.

<Example fr="Les journalistes s'interrogent sur l'avenir du projet." en="Journalists are wondering about the future of the project." />
<Example fr="Il fait semblant de dormir." en="He's pretending to be asleep." />

**Nier** (to deny) and **démentir** (to deny officially) reject a statement. *Démentir* is the word for a government or a company that says a piece of news is false. After *nier que*, the verb usually goes in the subjunctive, as after *douter que*.

<Example fr="Le gouvernement a démenti cette information." en="The government denied this report." />
<Example fr="Il nie que le projet soit trop cher." en="He denies that the project is too expensive." />

## Admitting

<VocabTable group="admit" />

*Admettre* (to admit) concedes a point, often unwillingly. *Avouer* (to confess) is stronger: it's what you do when you've done something wrong. Both take the indicative, with the usual shifts after a past verb: *il a avoué qu'il avait pris l'argent* (he confessed he had taken the money).

<Example fr="Elle a admis que le projet avait pris du retard." en="She admitted that the project had been delayed." />

## Reporting verbs in headlines

French headlines usually put the reporting verb in the present, even for yesterday's news: *Le ministre dément*, *La maire annonce une nouvelle piscine*. The present makes the news feel immediate. In the article itself, the journalist then switches to the passé composé and shifts the tenses after it. You also know *selon* from <LessonLink id="b1-conditionnel-present" text="the lesson on the conditionnel présent" />, which reports a source without any verb at all.

## Shifting time words

<VocabTable group="time" />

When you report something said on another day, time words that pointed to that day have to change, because *demain* now means a different day. You know *ce jour-là* from <LessonLink id="a2-telling-a-story" text="the lesson on telling a story" />.

<Example fr="Elle a dit qu'elle était occupée à ce moment-là." en="She said she was busy at that moment." />

| Said on the day | Reported later |
|---|---|
| *aujourd'hui* | *ce jour-là* |
| *hier* | *la veille* |
| *demain* | *le lendemain* |
| *maintenant* | *à ce moment-là* |
| *la semaine dernière* | *la semaine précédente* |
| *la semaine prochaine* | *la semaine suivante* |

<Example fr="« Je pars demain. » → Il a dit qu'il partait le lendemain." en="'I'm leaving tomorrow.' → He said he was leaving the next day." />

If you report on the same day, keep the original words: *ce matin, il m'a dit qu'il partait demain* is correct, because *demain* is still tomorrow.

## En français

Emma and Hugo, two journalism students, compare two papers' reports of the same speech.

- *Tu as lu les articles sur le discours de la ministre ?* (Have you read the articles on the minister's speech?)
- *Oui, et ils ne racontent pas la même chose ! Le premier dit qu'elle a annoncé une réforme.* (Yes, and they don't say the same thing! The first one says she announced a reform.)
- *Et le deuxième prétend qu'elle a avoué une erreur.* (And the second claims she confessed to a mistake.)
- *C'est faux. Elle a seulement admis que le projet avait pris du retard.* (That's wrong. She only admitted that the project had been delayed.)
- *Elle a aussi souligné qu'elle n'avait encore rien décidé.* (She also stressed that she hadn't decided anything yet.)
- *Oui, et elle a démenti les informations sur sa démission.* (Yes, and she denied the reports about her resignation.)
- *Le deuxième journal cite seulement une phrase. Ce n'est pas sérieux.* (The second paper only quotes one sentence. That's not serious journalism.)
- *Le premier, lui, rappelle qu'elle avait promis cette réforme l'année précédente.* (The first one points out that she had promised this reform the year before.)
- *C'est vrai. Et il ajoute qu'elle présentera le projet la semaine prochaine.* (True. And it adds that she'll present the project next week.)
- *Bref, pour savoir ce qu'elle a vraiment dit, il faut lire sa déclaration complète.* (In short, to know what she really said, you have to read her full statement.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Prétendre doesn't mean pretend">
  *Il prétend être malade* doesn't mean "he's pretending to be ill". It means **he claims he's ill**, and you're not sure he is. For "to pretend", French uses *faire semblant de*: **il fait semblant d'être malade**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Compare two headlines">
  When a big story breaks, find it in two French newspapers and copy the main reporting verb from each article. *Annonce* in one and *prétend* in the other tells you more about the papers than about the story. Collecting verb pairs this way builds the vocabulary and trains you to read the press critically.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Dire, annoncer, déclarer et rappeler que ne montrent pas l'avis du journaliste. Avec affirmer ou souligner, la personne parle avec force. Avec prétendre, le journaliste doute de ce qu'elle dit. Avec nier et démentir, on dit qu'une information est fausse, et après nier que, on met souvent le subjonctif. Quand on rapporte des paroles plus tard, demain devient le lendemain, et hier devient la veille.
