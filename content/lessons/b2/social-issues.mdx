By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about poverty, housing, integration and inequality, and about the people and groups who try to change things.

## Before you start

You already know *le chômage* from <LessonLink id="b1-careers-and-job-hunting" text="the lesson on careers and job hunting" />, *l'égalité* and *un citoyen* from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-french-republic" text="the lesson on the French Republic" />, and *le loyer* from <LessonLink id="b1-housing-and-renting" text="the lesson on housing and renting" />. This lesson builds the wider vocabulary of social debates. It deliberately uses no figures: numbers on poverty or immigration change every year and depend on how they're measured, so check a current source before you quote one. In debates, these words often come with the phrases from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing your opinion" />, such as *dans une certaine mesure*: a social issue is rarely simple, and a nuanced view sounds more convincing.

## Poverty and inequality

<VocabTable group="poverty" />

*Les inégalités* is usually plural in French, where English often says "inequality". *La pauvreté* is poverty in general; *la précarité* is the insecurity of people who could fall into it, for example with short contracts and low pay. *Défavorisé* (disadvantaged) is the neutral word for people or areas with fewer opportunities.

<Example fr="Les inégalités entre les villes et les campagnes restent fortes." en="Inequality between cities and the countryside remains high." />
<Example fr="Elle a un emploi précaire : son contrat finit dans deux mois." en="She has an insecure job: her contract ends in two months." />
<Example fr="Personne ne doit être exclu à cause de son origine." en="Nobody should be excluded because of where they come from." />

*Pauvre* also means "poor" in the sense of "unlucky" when it goes before the noun. You'll see this pattern with other adjectives later in B2, in <LessonLink id="b2-adjective-position-meaning" text="the lesson on adjectives that change meaning" />.

## Housing

<VocabTable group="housing" />

*La crise du logement* describes a situation where homes are too expensive or too rare for the people who need them. *Un logement social* is rented at a lower price to households with modest incomes. *Un sans-abri* (a homeless person) is the neutral, respectful word; in everyday speech you'll also hear *un SDF*, short for *sans domicile fixe*.

<Example fr="Avec la crise du logement, même les gens qui travaillent ont du mal à trouver un logement." en="With the housing crisis, even people who work struggle to find a home." />
<Example fr="Elle attend un logement social depuis deux ans." en="She's been waiting for social housing for two years." />

## People and integration

<VocabTable group="people" />

*Un immigré* is someone who has moved to France from another country. *S'intégrer* (to integrate, to fit in) is reflexive: *il s'est bien intégré*. These are sensitive subjects in France, as everywhere, so it's worth using neutral words and avoiding generalizations: say *certains*, *beaucoup*, or *dans certains cas*, rather than talking about a whole group.

<Example fr="Apprendre la langue aide beaucoup à s'intégrer." en="Learning the language helps a lot with integration." />
<Example fr="Les familles ont moins de pouvoir d'achat cette année." en="Families have less purchasing power this year." />

*Un chômeur* (an unemployed person) comes from *le chômage*, which you already know. *Le pouvoir d'achat* (purchasing power) is what your income actually lets you buy, and it's a constant topic in French political debate.

## Who helps

<VocabTable group="help" />

France has many *associations*, groups run partly or entirely by *bénévoles* (volunteers). The word *association* covers charities, but also sports clubs and neighbourhood groups. *Le bénévolat* is the activity itself. *Une aide sociale* is a welfare benefit paid by the State or the local authorities.

<Example fr="Mon frère fait du bénévolat dans une association pour les sans-abri." en="My brother does voluntary work with a charity for homeless people." />

## Taking action

<VocabTable group="action" />

*Une manifestation* is a demonstration in the street, and *manifester* means to take part in one. *Lutter contre* (to fight against) and its noun *la lutte contre* are the standard phrases for campaigns. *Réduire* (to reduce) is conjugated like *conduire*. *Un enjeu* is what's at stake in a debate, and *une mesure* is a policy decision.

<Example fr="Beaucoup de personnes ont manifesté samedi à Paris." en="Many people demonstrated in Paris on Saturday." />
<Example fr="L'État a annoncé de nouvelles mesures pour réduire la pauvreté." en="The State announced new measures to reduce poverty." />
<Example fr="Le logement est un enjeu très important pour les jeunes." en="Housing is a very important issue for young people." />

## En français

Léo and Chloé, two students, are preparing a presentation for their class.

- *Pour la présentation de vendredi, on parle des sans-abri ?* (For Friday's presentation, shall we talk about homeless people?)
- *Bonne idée. C'est un enjeu important, et de plus en plus de gens vivent dans la précarité.* (Good idea. It's an important issue, and more and more people are living in insecurity.)
- *D'accord, mais on ne donne pas de statistiques sans une source fiable.* (OK, but we don't give any statistics without a reliable source.)
- *Tu as raison. On peut aussi parler de la crise du logement.* (You're right. We can talk about the housing crisis too.)
- *Oui. Avec les loyers, même des gens qui travaillent ont du mal à trouver un logement.* (Yes. With rents the way they are, even people who work struggle to find a home.)
- *Et les solutions ? Les logements sociaux, les aides sociales…* (And the solutions? Social housing, welfare benefits…)
- *Et les associations ! Ma tante est bénévole dans une association à Lyon.* (And charities! My aunt volunteers for a charity in Lyon.)
- *Super. Tu pourrais lui poser quelques questions ?* (Great. Could you ask her a few questions?)
- *Oui, je l'appelle ce soir. Elle connaît bien le bénévolat.* (Yes, I'll call her tonight. She knows a lot about voluntary work.)
- *Parfait. Moi, je cherche des articles sur la lutte contre la pauvreté.* (Perfect. I'll look for articles on the fight against poverty.)
- *Et à la fin, on pose une question à la classe : comment réduire les inégalités ?* (And at the end, we'll ask the class a question: how can we reduce inequality?)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une manifestation is a protest">
  In French, **une manifestation** is a demonstration in the street: *Il y a une manifestation contre la réforme.* It doesn't mean "a sign of something", the usual English sense of "manifestation". For that meaning, use **un signe**: *C'est un signe de la crise.*
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Draw a word map">
  Pick one issue, such as housing, and write it in the middle of a page. Around it, write the problems in one colour (*la crise du logement*, *les loyers*), the people in another (*les sans-abri*, *les bénévoles*), and the solutions in a third (*les logements sociaux*, *une mesure*). Seeing the words grouped by role helps you use them together when you speak.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Les inégalités, la pauvreté et la crise du logement sont des enjeux importants. Beaucoup de personnes vivent dans la précarité, et certaines sont sans-abri. Pour les aider, il y a des aides sociales, des logements sociaux et des associations. Les bénévoles donnent leur temps pour lutter contre la pauvreté. Quand des citoyens ne sont pas d'accord avec une mesure, ils peuvent manifester dans la rue. Le but est de réduire les inégalités et d'aider chacun à s'intégrer.
