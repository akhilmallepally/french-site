By the end of this lesson you'll be able to contrast two facts and concede a point with the main French connectors of opposition and concession.

## What you already know

You already concede a point with *il est vrai que…, mais…*, from <LessonLink id="b1-presenting-an-argument" text="the lesson on presenting an argument" />, and you compare two sides with *d'un côté… de l'autre*, from <LessonLink id="b1-agreeing-disagreeing" text="the lesson on agreeing and disagreeing" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-subjunctive-conjunctions" text="the last lesson" />, you added *bien que* + subjunctive. This lesson gives you the other common ways of saying "but", "despite" and "whereas".

## Despite: malgré, not bien que

<VocabTable group="despite" />

*Malgré* (despite) is a preposition: it's followed by a noun or a pronoun. *Bien que* is a conjunction: it's followed by a verb in the subjunctive. The two say the same thing in different shapes.

<Example fr="Malgré la pluie, le match a lieu." en="Despite the rain, the match is going ahead." />
<Example fr="Bien qu'il pleuve, le match a lieu." en="Although it's raining, the match is going ahead." />

## Even if: même si

<VocabTable group="evenif" />

*Même si* (even if, even though) also concedes a point, but it takes the indicative, like *si*. It's the usual way to concede a whole clause without the subjunctive. The meaning is close to *bien que*, but the mood is different: *même s'il pleut*, *bien qu'il pleuve*.

<Example fr="Même s'il pleut, le match a lieu." en="Even if it's raining, the match is going ahead." />
<Example fr="Je sors ce soir, même si je suis fatigué." en="I'm going out tonight, even though I'm tired." />

## Starting a sentence: pourtant, cependant, néanmoins

These three adverbs usually start a new sentence or a new clause, often after a full stop or a semicolon, and they're followed by a comma. They all mean roughly "however", but they differ in register. *Pourtant* (yet, and yet) is common in speech and writing, and often expresses surprise. *Cependant* (however) is neutral and frequent in writing. *Néanmoins* (nevertheless) is formal.

<Example fr="Il a beaucoup travaillé. Pourtant, il a raté l'examen." en="He worked a lot. And yet he failed the exam." />
<Example fr="Le projet est intéressant. Cependant, il coûte trop cher." en="The project is interesting. However, it costs too much." />
<Example fr="Le projet avance bien ; néanmoins, il reste beaucoup de travail." en="The project is going well; nevertheless, there's still a lot of work." />

## Comparing two facts: alors que, tandis que

<VocabTable group="contrast" />

*Alors que* and *tandis que* (whereas) put two facts side by side to show a contrast. Unlike *bien que*, they take the indicative. There's no deeper reason to look for: it's simply the rule for each conjunction, so learn *bien que* with the subjunctive and *alors que* and *tandis que* with the indicative.

<Example fr="Mon frère adore la ville, alors que je préfère la campagne." en="My brother loves the city, whereas I prefer the countryside." />
<Example fr="Les loyers sont de plus en plus chers, tandis que les salaires restent les mêmes." en="Rents are more and more expensive, while salaries stay the same." />

*Alors que* can also mean "while" in time (*il est arrivé alors que je partais*), but the contrast meaning is the more common one. *En revanche* (on the other hand) is an adverb that does the same job between two sentences: *Paris est cher. En revanche, les salaires y sont plus hauts.*

## Still and in vain: quand même, avoir beau

*Quand même* (all the same, still) is the spoken way to say that something happens despite an obstacle. It usually comes after the verb.

<Example fr="Il était malade, mais il est venu quand même." en="He was ill, but he came all the same." />

*Avoir beau* + infinitive says that an effort doesn't work. Only *avoir* changes; *beau* stays as it is.

<Example fr="J'ai beau chercher, je ne trouve pas mes clés." en="However hard I look, I can't find my keys." />
<Example fr="Elle a eu beau parler, il n'a pas changé d'avis." en="She talked in vain; he didn't change his mind." />

*Certes* (admittedly) accepts a point before a *mais*, a little more formally than *c'est vrai*: *Certes, c'est cher, mais c'est utile.*

## Choosing the right connector

If what you concede is a noun, use *malgré*. If it's a full clause, use *bien que* + subjunctive, or *même si* + indicative. If you compare two facts in one sentence, use *alors que* or *tandis que*. If you start a new sentence, use *pourtant* or *cependant*, or *néanmoins* in formal writing, and add *en revanche* when you simply contrast two sides. In conversation, *quand même* at the end of the sentence is often all you need: *C'était cher, mais j'y suis allé quand même.*

## Talking about differences

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="L'écart entre les salaires reste important." en="The gap between salaries remains large." />
<Example fr="Il y a une contradiction dans ce texte." en="There's a contradiction in this text." />
<Example fr="Beaucoup de gens s'opposent à ce projet." en="Many people are opposed to this project." />

*Contradictoire* (contradictory) describes two ideas that can't both be true; *un paradoxe* (a paradox) is a situation that seems contradictory but is real.

## En français

Lucas visits Manon, who has moved from Paris to a small town.

- *Tu ne regrettes pas Paris ?* (Don't you miss Paris?)
- *Parfois. Malgré le bruit, j'aimais l'ambiance.* (Sometimes. Despite the noise, I liked the atmosphere.)
- *Ici, en revanche, c'est calme, et les loyers sont bas.* (Here, on the other hand, it's quiet, and rents are low.)
- *Certes, mais il n'y a pas beaucoup de cinémas.* (Admittedly, but there aren't many cinemas.)
- *C'est vrai. Pourtant, tu sors plus qu'avant, non ?* (That's true. And yet you go out more than before, don't you?)
- *Oui. J'ai beau trouver la ville trop calme, je sors avec mes amis tous les week-ends.* (Yes. I may find the town too quiet, but I go out with my friends every weekend.)
- *Alors qu'à Paris, tu travaillais tard tous les soirs !* (Whereas in Paris, you worked late every evening!)
- *Exactement. Ici, mon niveau de vie est meilleur, même avec un salaire plus bas.* (Exactly. Here, my standard of living is better, even with a lower salary.)
- *Il y a un écart entre les salaires, non ?* (There's a gap between salaries, isn't there?)
- *Oui, mais tout coûte moins cher. C'est un peu un paradoxe.* (Yes, but everything costs less. It's a bit of a paradox.)
- *Bref, tu restes ici ?* (Anyway, are you staying here?)
- *Oui, je reste. Mais je viendrai quand même souvent à Paris !* (Yes, I'm staying. But I'll still come to Paris often!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Malgré + noun, bien que + verb">
  Don't put *que* after *malgré*. *Malgré qu'il pleut* is wrong, and even *malgré qu'il pleuve* is criticized. Before a verb, use **bien qu'il pleuve**; before a noun, use **malgré la pluie**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="One fact, two connectors">
  Take one contrast from your own life, such as your town and the next one, and write it twice: once with *alors que* in one sentence, and once with *en revanche* across two sentences. Then add a third version with *malgré* and a noun. Saying the same idea three ways trains you to pick the connector that fits the sentence you've started.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Malgré est suivi d'un nom : malgré la pluie. Bien que est suivi d'un verbe au subjonctif. Pourtant, cependant et néanmoins commencent souvent une phrase, et néanmoins est plus soutenu. Même si est suivi de l'indicatif : même s'il pleut. Alors que et tandis que comparent deux faits avec l'indicatif. En revanche montre une différence entre deux phrases. Avoir beau est suivi d'un infinitif : j'ai beau chercher veut dire que je cherche beaucoup, mais je ne trouve pas. Quand même se met après le verbe : il est malade, mais il vient quand même.
