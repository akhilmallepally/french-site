By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use *tout* in its three roles, pronounce *tous* correctly, and use the most common phrases built on it.

## What you already know

You know *tout le monde*, *chaque*, *chacun* and *n'importe qui* from <LessonLink id="b1-indefinite-pronouns" text="the lesson on indefinite pronouns" />, and *tout ce que* from <LessonLink id="b1-ce-qui-ce-que" text="the lesson on ce qui and ce que" />. You also know the set phrases *tout de suite*, *tout à coup*, *tout à fait* and *pas du tout*, and *partout* (everywhere) from <LessonLink id="b2-argumentative-essay" text="the lesson on the argumentative essay" />. This lesson puts *tout* into one system.

## Three roles

*Tout* can be three different words, and each one behaves in its own way.

- As a determiner, before a noun, it agrees with the noun: *toute la journée* (the whole day).
- As a pronoun, it replaces a noun: *tout est prêt* (everything is ready), *ils sont tous là* (they're all here).
- As an adverb, before an adjective, it means "completely": *il est tout seul* (he's all alone).

## Tout as a determiner

<VocabTable group="det" />

Before a noun with *le*, *la*, *les*, a possessive or a demonstrative, *tout* has four forms. In the singular, it means "the whole"; in the plural, "all the" or "every".

<Example fr="Nous avons visité tout le musée en une matinée." en="We visited the whole museum in one morning." />
<Example fr="Toute la ville est venue au vernissage." en="The whole town came to the opening." />
<Example fr="Elle a lu tous ses romans et toutes ses nouvelles." en="She has read all her novels and all her short stories." />

With a time word, *tous les* means "every": you know *tous les jours* from A1, and you can say *toutes les semaines* or *tous les deux ans*. In this role, the *s* of *tous* is silent: *tous les jours* sounds like "too lay zhoor".

<Example fr="Le musée change d'exposition tous les trois mois." en="The museum changes its exhibition every three months." />

## Tout as a pronoun

<VocabTable group="pron" />

Alone, *tout* means "everything". In a compound tense, it goes between the auxiliary and the participle.

<Example fr="Tout est prêt pour l'exposition." en="Everything is ready for the exhibition." />
<Example fr="Le guide a tout expliqué." en="The guide explained everything." />

*Tous* and *toutes* mean "all of them". They usually go after a simple verb, and between the auxiliary and the participle in a compound tense. As a pronoun, *tous* is pronounced with an *s* sound at the end, which is how you hear the difference.

<Example fr="Les tableaux ? Je les connais tous." en="The paintings? I know all of them." />
<Example fr="Les visiteuses sont toutes arrivées." en="The visitors have all arrived." />

*Tous les deux* and *toutes les deux* mean "both". Use the masculine as soon as there's one man in the group, as with any plural.

<Example fr="Mon frère et ma sœur sont tous les deux peintres." en="My brother and my sister are both painters." />

## Tout as an adverb

<VocabTable group="adv" />

Before an adjective, *tout* means "completely, very". As an adverb, it doesn't change, with one exception: it agrees before a feminine adjective that starts with a consonant (or an aspirate *h*, as in *toute honteuse*, very ashamed).

<Example fr="Il est tout content de son nouveau tableau." en="He's really happy with his new painting." />
<Example fr="Elle est toute contente de son exposition." en="She's really happy with her exhibition." />
<Example fr="Elle est tout heureuse." en="She's very happy." />

The masculine plural shows why the pronunciation matters. *Ils sont tout contents*, with a silent ending, means "they're really happy" (the adverb). *Ils sont tous contents*, with an *s* sound, means "they're all happy" (the pronoun).

<Example fr="Après la visite, les enfants étaient tout contents." en="After the tour, the children were really happy." />
<Example fr="Après la visite, les enfants étaient tous contents." en="After the tour, the children were all happy." />

## Set phrases

<VocabTable group="phrases" />

These phrases are frequent in conversation and in writing. Learn them as units.

- To say "in any case, whatever happens", use *en tout cas*: *en tout cas, je viendrai* (in any case, I'll come).
- To dismiss what was just said, use *de toute façon*: *de toute façon, c'est trop tard* (anyway, it's too late).
- To keep a point despite something, use *tout de même*: *il pleuvait, mais nous sommes sortis tout de même* (it was raining, but we went out all the same).
- To give a final view after thinking, use *tout compte fait*: *tout compte fait, je préfère rester* (all things considered, I'd rather stay).
- To show determination, use *à tout prix*: *il veut gagner à tout prix* (he wants to win at all costs).
- To describe a complete change, use *du tout au tout*: *son style a changé du tout au tout* (her style has changed completely).

<Example fr="Mon frère regarde son téléphone tout le temps." en="My brother looks at his phone all the time." />

## Quiconque and quelconque

<VocabTable group="indef" />

*Quiconque* means "anyone who" or "whoever". It's formal, and it takes a singular verb. *Quelconque* goes after a noun and means "any at all": *pour une raison quelconque* (for some reason or other). Said of a work or a person, it means "ordinary, nothing special". You know *n'importe lequel* (any one) from <LessonLink id="b2-relative-lequel" text="the lesson on lequel" />.

<Example fr="Quiconque a un billet peut entrer dans toutes les salles." en="Anyone who has a ticket can go into all the rooms." />
<Example fr="Il n'est pas venu, pour une raison quelconque." en="He didn't come, for some reason or other." />
<Example fr="Ce film est quelconque : je l'ai déjà oublié." en="This film is nothing special: I've already forgotten it." />

## The whole list

- The determiner agrees with its noun, giving *tout le*, *toute la*, *tous les* and *toutes les*, with a silent *s*.
- The pronoun is *tout* for everything, and *tous* (with an *s* sound) or *toutes* for all of them.
- The adverb doesn't change, except before a feminine adjective starting with a consonant: *toute contente*, *toutes seules*.
- The set phrases are *en tout cas*, *de toute façon*, *tout de même*, *tout compte fait*, *à tout prix*, *du tout au tout* and *tout le temps*.
- *Quiconque* takes a singular verb, and *quelconque* goes after the noun.

## En français

A museum guide answers questions from a group of visitors.

<VocabTable group="museum" />

- *Excusez-moi, madame, nous pouvons voir toutes les salles ?* (Excuse me, can we see all the rooms?)
- *Oui, toutes les salles sont ouvertes aujourd'hui. La visite prend toute la matinée.* (Yes, all the rooms are open today. The tour takes the whole morning.)
- *Et les tableaux de cette salle, ils sont tous du même peintre ?* (And the paintings in this room, are they all by the same painter?)
- *Oui, ils sont tous de lui. Il a peint toute sa vie dans la même ville.* (Yes, they're all his. He painted all his life in the same town.)
- *Nous pouvons prendre des photos ?* (Can we take photos?)
- *Non, je suis désolée. Quiconque prend une photo doit quitter la salle.* (No, I'm sorry. Anyone who takes a photo has to leave the room.)
- *D'accord. Et ce petit tableau, il est célèbre ?* (All right. And this small painting, is it famous?)
- *Au début, les journaux le trouvaient quelconque. Mais tout compte fait, c'est son chef-d'œuvre.* (At first, the papers found it ordinary. But all things considered, it's his masterpiece.)
- *Mon frère et moi, nous voulons tous les deux savoir : il y a un café dans le musée ?* (My brother and I both want to know: is there a café in the museum?)
- *Oui, près de la sortie. En tout cas, prenez votre temps : avec votre billet, vous pouvez rester toute la journée.* (Yes, near the exit. In any case, take your time: with your ticket, you can stay all day.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Ils sont tous venus: say the s">
  Learners often keep the silent *s* of *tous les jours* when *tous* is a pronoun. In **ils sont tous venus** and **je les connais tous**, the *s* is pronounced. Without it, listeners may hear *tout*, the adverb.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask what tout is doing">
  Before you write *tout*, ask one question: is it before a noun, replacing a noun, or before an adjective? The answer gives you the rule at once: agree with the noun, agree with what it replaces, or stay unchanged (except before a feminine consonant).
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le mot tout a trois rôles. Devant un nom, il s'accorde avec le nom : tout le musée, toute la ville, tous les jours, toutes les salles. Dans ce cas, on ne prononce pas le s de tous. Comme pronom, tout veut dire toutes les choses : tout est prêt. Tous et toutes remplacent un nom au pluriel : ils sont tous venus. Ici, on prononce le s de tous. Devant un adjectif, tout veut dire très, et il ne change pas : il est tout content, ils sont tout contents. Il s'accorde seulement devant un adjectif féminin qui commence par une consonne : elle est toute contente. Enfin, quiconque veut dire n'importe quelle personne qui, et il est suivi d'un verbe au singulier.
