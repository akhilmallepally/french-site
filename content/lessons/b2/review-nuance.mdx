## A few new words

These six words are useful in any opinion piece. A *tribune* is the French name for a signed opinion text in a newspaper or on a website.

<VocabTable />

*Un débat de société* is broader than *un débat* (a debate), which you know from <LessonLink id="b1-presenting-an-argument" text="the lesson on presenting an argument" />: it's a question that a whole society argues about, such as housing or work. *Une piste* first means a track or a trail. In an argument, it's an idea you suggest exploring, without claiming that it solves everything. *Concilier* usually takes two nouns joined by *et*: you reconcile one thing and another. *Un juste milieu* is close to *un compromis* (a compromise), which you know from <LessonLink id="b1-subjunctive-vs-infinitive" text="the lesson on the subjunctive versus the infinitive" />, but it stresses balance rather than giving something up. *À long terme* often goes at the start of a sentence, followed by a comma.

## A model opinion piece

A resident of a large city wrote this *tribune* for the local news website.

*Des logements sociaux en centre-ville : une piste à suivre ?*

*Le logement est devenu un vrai débat de société. Dans beaucoup de villes, vivre en centre-ville coûte les yeux de la tête, si bien que les familles défavorisées partent loin de leur travail. Il me semble que nous pouvons changer la situation.*

*A priori, la solution est simple : proposer plus de logements sociaux au centre. En effet, cette mesure vise à réduire les inégalités entre les quartiers. En outre, les gens habitent près de leur travail, d'où moins de transports.*

*Certes, ce n'est pas si simple. Bien que les loyers soient plus bas pour les familles, les logements sociaux coûtent cher à la ville. Certains habitants ont aussi peur que leur quartier change. Or, une ville où seules les personnes riches peuvent vivre perd une partie de sa vie.*

*Il faut donc trouver un juste milieu. Une piste : demander à chaque nouveau projet quelques logements sociaux, afin que chaque quartier reste ouvert à tous. Il s'agit de concilier le prix des logements et la vie des quartiers.*

*En somme, cette mesure ne va pas tout changer, mais à long terme, elle peut rendre nos villes plus justes.*

(Social housing in the city centre: an idea worth exploring? Housing has become a real public debate. In many cities, living in the centre costs an arm and a leg, so disadvantaged families move far from their work. It seems to me that we can change the situation. On the face of it, the solution is simple: offer more social housing in the centre. Indeed, this measure aims to reduce inequality between neighbourhoods. Furthermore, people live near their work, hence less commuting. Admittedly, it isn't that simple. Although rents are lower for families, social housing is expensive for the city. Some residents are also afraid that their neighbourhood will change. Yet a city where only rich people can live loses part of its life. So we have to find a happy medium. One idea: ask every new project for a few social homes, so that every neighbourhood stays open to everyone. It's about reconciling the price of housing and the life of neighbourhoods. All in all, this measure isn't going to change everything, but in the long term, it can make our cities fairer.)

## How the text uses the grammar

- **A nuanced start.** *Il me semble que* and *a priori* present the opinion as a view, not a fact. *Il me semble que* takes the indicative: *nous pouvons*.
- **An idiom in a formal text.** *Coûter les yeux de la tête* is familiar, but one well-placed idiom makes a *tribune* lively. More than one would make it sound like a chat.
- **Connectors.** *Si bien que* gives a result in the same sentence. *En effet* explains, *en outre* adds and *d'où* is followed by a noun. *Or* turns the reasoning back to the writer's side.
- **The concession paragraph.** *Certes* admits that it isn't that simple, and *bien que* + subjunctive (*soient*) gives the cost. The writer accepts the other side's point, then answers it with *or*.
- **Purpose.** *Vise à* + infinitive states the aim of the measure. *Afin que* + subjunctive (*reste*) states the aim of the proposal, because the subject changes.
- **The ending.** *En somme* introduces the conclusion, and *à long terme* keeps the promise realistic.

The order of the text matters as much as the grammar. The writer states the problem, gives a first answer, concedes the weak point, then proposes a compromise. Readers trust a *tribune* that shows the other side before it concludes.

## Planning before you write

A good *tribune* is planned before it's written. Spend five minutes on these steps, in English or in French, before you write a single sentence of the text.

- Write your opinion in one sentence. If you can't, you're not ready to write the text yet.
- List two arguments for your opinion and the strongest argument against it. That last one becomes your concession paragraph.
- Decide which connector links each step: one to add, one to explain, one to concede and one to conclude. Four or five connectors are enough for a whole text.
- Choose a proposal that's realistic. A *piste* that sounds possible convinces more readers than a perfect plan nobody believes in.
- Decide on your register. A *tribune* is formal, so use *nous* rather than *on*, and keep familiar words for a single idiom at most.

## Your turn

Choose a social issue in your town or your country: housing, transport, work or poverty. Write a short *tribune* of three or four paragraphs, on paper or in a notes app.

1. **A first paragraph** that presents the issue as *un débat de société*, with one nuancing phrase such as *il me semble que* or *dans une certaine mesure*.
2. **A concession paragraph** that starts with *certes* and uses *bien que* + subjunctive or *malgré* + noun.
3. **A proposal paragraph** that gives *une piste* with *pour que* or *afin que* + subjunctive.
4. **A short conclusion** with *en somme* and *à long terme*.

Try to include one idiom from this unit, but only one. Give your *tribune* a title that's a question: it invites the reader to think with you.

## Check your work

- After *bien que*, *pour que* and *afin que*, is the verb in the subjunctive?
- After *il me semble que*, is the verb in the indicative?
- Did you use *malgré* only before a noun, and *bien que* only before a clause?
- With one subject, did you use *pour* or *afin de* + infinitive instead of *pour que*?
- Does every connector start a sentence and take a comma, except *si bien que* and *bien que*, which join two clauses?

<Callout type="warning" title="Malgré que is not standard">
  Learners who know *malgré* and *bien que* often mix them into *malgré que*. You'll hear it, but it's not accepted in careful writing, so keep it out of a *tribune*. Write **bien que les loyers soient chers** or **malgré le prix des loyers**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read your text as your opponent">
  When your draft is ready, read it again as someone who disagrees with you. Mark the sentence they'd attack first. If your concession paragraph doesn't answer that sentence, rewrite it so that it does. A *tribune* that answers its strongest objection is far more convincing.
</Callout>
