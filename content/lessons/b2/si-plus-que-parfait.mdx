By the end of this lesson you'll be able to talk about the unreal past: what would have happened if things had gone differently.

## What you already know

You know two *si* patterns. *Si* + present with the futur describes a real possibility, from <LessonLink id="b1-si-present-futur" text="the lesson on si with the present" />, and *si* + imparfait with the conditionnel describes an unreal present, from <LessonLink id="b1-si-imparfait-conditionnel" text="the lesson on si with the imparfait" />. You also know the plus-que-parfait (*j'avais fini*) from <LessonLink id="b1-plus-que-parfait" text="the lesson on the plus-que-parfait" />, and the conditionnel passé from the last lesson.

## The third pattern

<VocabTable group="si" />

To imagine a different past, put the *si* clause in the **plus-que-parfait** and the result in the **conditionnel passé**. Both events are in the past, and neither really happened.

<Example fr="Si j'avais su, je serais venu." en="If I'd known, I'd have come." />
<Example fr="Si nous avions pris la voiture, nous serions arrivés à l'heure." en="If we'd taken the car, we'd have arrived on time." />

The facts are always the opposite of the sentence. *Si j'avais su* means that I didn't know. *Je serais venu* means that I didn't come. To build the sentence from a real story, turn each fact around: *il n'a pas étudié, il a raté l'examen* becomes *s'il avait étudié, il n'aurait pas raté l'examen*.

In speech, *j'avais* and *j'aurais* sound very close: listen for the *r*, which only the conditionnel has. When you write, check the first verb after *si*: it should be *avais*, *avait*, *étais* or *était*, never a form with *-rai-*.

The *si* clause can come first or second. When it comes first, put a comma after it; when it comes second, there's no comma.

<Example fr="Tu aurais aimé ce film si tu l'avais vu." en="You'd have liked that film if you'd seen it." />

## Never the conditionnel after si

The rule from B1 still holds in the past: **no conditionnel after *si***. The conditionnel goes in the result, never in the condition. *Si j'aurais su* is a very common mistake, even among French speakers, but it's wrong in standard French.

**Si seulement** (if only) + plus-que-parfait expresses a regret about the past, often as an exclamation with no result clause: *Si seulement j'avais su !* (If only I'd known!) You'll use it more in <LessonLink id="b2-regret-and-reproach" text="the lesson on regret and reproach" />.

## Luck and chance

<VocabTable group="chance" />

Unreal past sentences often come with words about luck. *Par malchance* (by bad luck) and *par hasard* (by chance) explain why things happened as they did. *Un hasard* is a coincidence, and *un concours de circonstances* is a chain of small events that leads to a big one. **Heureusement que** + indicative means "it's a good thing that".

<Example fr="Par hasard, j'ai rencontré une amie à la gare." en="By chance, I met a friend at the station." />
<Example fr="Heureusement que tu étais là !" en="It's a good thing you were there!" />
<Example fr="Ce voyage a changé le cours de ma vie." en="That trip changed the course of my life." />

You know *rater* (to miss) and *prévoir* (to plan, to foresee) from <LessonLink id="b1-si-present-futur" text="the lesson on si with the present" />. *Imprévisible* (unpredictable) describes what nobody could foresee.

## Near misses

<VocabTable group="close" />

Some events almost happened. **Faillir** + infinitive, in the passé composé, says that something nearly happened but didn't: *j'ai failli tomber* (I almost fell). *De justesse* means "only just", and *au dernier moment* means "at the last minute".

<Example fr="J'ai failli rater le train : je suis arrivé au dernier moment." en="I almost missed the train: I arrived at the last minute." />
<Example fr="Elle a eu son bus de justesse." en="She only just caught her bus." />

Near misses invite the unreal past: *si j'étais arrivé une minute plus tard, j'aurais raté le train*.

## Suspecting and doubting

<VocabTable group="doubt" />

**Se douter de** (to suspect) is a false friend inside French itself. *Douter de*, from <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-doubt-certainty" text="the lesson on doubt and certainty" />, means to doubt, which is almost the opposite. *Se douter que* takes the indicative, because you think it's true.

<Example fr="Je me doutais qu'il allait être en retard." en="I suspected he was going to be late." />

## The whole list

- Real possibility: *si* + present, futur (*si tu viens, je serai content*).
- Unreal present: *si* + imparfait, conditionnel présent (*si tu venais, je serais content*).
- Unreal past: *si* + plus-que-parfait, conditionnel passé (*si tu étais venu, j'aurais été content*).
- In all three: no conditionnel after *si*.

## En français

Léa tells her friend Karim about her holiday in Brittany.

- *Alors, ces vacances en Bretagne ?* (So, how was the holiday in Brittany?)
- *Super, mais pas du tout comme je pensais ! Par malchance, j'ai raté le train à Paris.* (Great, but not at all as I'd imagined! By bad luck, I missed the train in Paris.)
- *Ah non ! Tu étais en retard ?* (Oh no! Were you late?)
- *Le métro s'est arrêté. Je suis arrivée au dernier moment, et les portes se sont fermées.* (The metro stopped. I arrived at the last minute, and the doors closed.)
- *Si tu étais partie plus tôt, tu l'aurais eu.* (If you'd left earlier, you'd have caught it.)
- *Je sais. Mais par hasard, j'ai rencontré une amie à la gare, et elle allait aussi en Bretagne en voiture.* (I know. But by chance, I met a friend at the station, and she was also driving to Brittany.)
- *Quel hasard ! Heureusement que tu l'as vue.* (What a coincidence! It's a good thing you saw her.)
- *Oui. Sans elle, je n'aurais pas vu ce joli village au bord de la mer. Ce retard a changé le cours de mes vacances.* (Yes. Without her, I wouldn't have seen that pretty village by the sea. That delay changed the course of my holiday.)
- *C'est un vrai concours de circonstances ! Tu te doutais de quelque chose ?* (What a chain of events! Did you suspect anything?)
- *Pas du tout. C'était vraiment imprévisible !* (Not at all. It was really unpredictable!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Si j'avais su, not si j'aurais su">
  The conditionnel never goes after *si*, even in the past, and even though you'll hear it. *Si j'aurais su, je serais venu* is wrong. Say **si j'avais su, je serais venu**: the plus-que-parfait after *si*, the conditionnel passé in the other half.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Turn the facts around">
  Take something small that happened to you this week, in two facts: *j'ai oublié mon parapluie, j'ai été sous la pluie*. Then turn both facts around in one sentence: *si je n'avais pas oublié mon parapluie, je n'aurais pas été sous la pluie*. Doing this with real events makes the pattern automatic, because you always know which facts are true.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Pour parler d'un passé qui n'est pas arrivé, on met si avec le plus-que-parfait, puis le conditionnel passé : si j'avais su, je serais venu. En réalité, je n'ai pas su, et je ne suis pas venu. Après si, on ne met jamais le conditionnel. La phrase avec si peut aussi venir en deuxième : je serais venu si j'avais su. Avec si seulement et le plus-que-parfait, on exprime un regret : si seulement j'avais su !
