By the end of this lesson you'll be able to link two ideas with six conjunctions that always take the subjunctive, and to switch to an infinitive when one person does both actions.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b1-subjunctive-vs-infinitive" text="the lesson on the subjunctive or the infinitive" />, you learned that French uses *que* + subjunctive when there are two subjects (*je veux que tu partes*) and an infinitive when there's one (*je veux partir*). The same rule applies to the conjunctions in this lesson. You also know *avant de* + infinitive from <LessonLink id="b1-time-connectors" text="the lesson on time connectors" />.

## Six conjunctions, one mood

<VocabTable group="conj" />

| Conjunction | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *bien que* | although | *bien qu'il soit tard* |
| *avant que* | before | *avant que tu partes* |
| *à condition que* | provided that | *à condition que tu viennes* |
| *jusqu'à ce que* | until | *jusqu'à ce qu'elle arrive* |
| *sans que* | without | *sans que personne le sache* |
| *à moins que* | unless | *à moins qu'il pleuve* |

All six are always followed by the subjunctive. Learn them as a group, and you'll never have to decide case by case. Notice that *que* becomes *qu'* before a vowel: *bien qu'il*, *jusqu'à ce qu'elle*. The *à* of *à condition que* and *à moins que* keeps its accent.

## Bien que: although

*Bien que* introduces a fact that you accept but that doesn't change the main point. The fact may be true, but the subjunctive is still required.

<Example fr="Bien qu'elle soit fatiguée, elle continue à travailler." en="Although she's tired, she keeps working." />
<Example fr="Nous sortons, bien qu'il fasse froid." en="We're going out, although it's cold." />

## Avant que and jusqu'à ce que: time

*Avant que* means "before", and *jusqu'à ce que* means "until". Both look ahead to something that hasn't happened yet, which is why they take the subjunctive.

<Example fr="Je t'appelle avant que tu partes." en="I'll call you before you leave." />
<Example fr="Attends ici jusqu'à ce que je revienne." en="Wait here until I come back." />

*Attendre que* (to wait until) works in the same way: *j'attends que le bureau ouvre*.

## Conditions: à condition que, à moins que

*À condition que* (provided that) sets a condition. *À moins que* (unless) gives the one case where things change.

<Example fr="Je viendrai à condition que tu m'invites." en="I'll come provided that you invite me." />
<Example fr="Nous partirons à 8 heures, à moins qu'il y ait une grève." en="We'll leave at 8, unless there's a strike." />

## Sans que: without

*Sans que* means "without" when the second action has a different subject.

<Example fr="Il est entré sans que personne le voie." en="He came in without anyone seeing him." />

## The same subject: back to the infinitive

<VocabTable group="inf" />

When one person does both actions, four of these conjunctions become a preposition + infinitive. *Avant que* becomes *avant de*, *sans que* becomes *sans*, *à condition que* becomes *à condition de*, and *à moins que* becomes *à moins de*.

<Example fr="Je lis le contrat avant de signer." en="I read the contract before I sign." />
<Example fr="Elle est partie sans dire au revoir." en="She left without saying goodbye." />
<Example fr="Tu peux venir à condition d'arriver à l'heure." en="You can come provided that you arrive on time." />

*Bien que* and *jusqu'à ce que* have no infinitive partner; they keep *que* + subjunctive even with one subject.

## The expletive ne

After *avant que* and *à moins que*, careful or formal French often adds a small *ne*, called *le ne explétif*. It doesn't make the sentence negative: the meaning is exactly the same. You met it after *avoir peur que* in <LessonLink id="b1-subjonctif-wishes-emotions" text="the lesson on wishes and emotions" />.

<Example fr="Partez avant qu'il ne pleuve." en="Leave before it rains." />
<Example fr="Je viendrai, à moins qu'il n'y ait un problème." en="I'll come, unless there's a problem." />

You don't need to use it yourself, but recognize it so you don't read it as a negation.

## Applying for things

<VocabTable group="steps" />

These words come up when someone explains an official process, such as an application for housing or for papers. *Une démarche* is one step in a procedure; *les démarches* are all the paperwork together.

<Example fr="Il faut faire beaucoup de démarches avant d'obtenir un titre de séjour." en="You have to go through a lot of steps before you get a residence permit." />
<Example fr="Le centre d'accueil accueille les familles le matin." en="The drop-in centre receives families in the morning." />

## En français

Amina meets a volunteer at a drop-in centre about a housing application.

- *Bonjour, madame. Je voudrais faire une demande de logement social.* (Hello. I'd like to apply for social housing.)
- *Bonjour. Bien que les démarches soient longues, nous allons vous aider.* (Hello. Although the steps are long, we'll help you.)
- *Merci. Qu'est-ce que je dois préparer ?* (Thank you. What do I need to prepare?)
- *Un dossier complet. Nous ne pouvons pas l'envoyer à moins qu'il soit complet.* (A complete file. We can't send it unless it's complete.)
- *J'ai mon titre de séjour et mon contrat de travail.* (I have my residence permit and my employment contract.)
- *Parfait. Prenez une photo des documents avant que je les envoie.* (Perfect. Take a photo of the documents before I send them.)
- *Et après, combien de temps faut-il attendre ?* (And afterwards, how long do I have to wait?)
- *Ça dépend. Il faut attendre que la mairie vous réponde.* (It depends. You have to wait until the town hall replies.)
- *Je peux revenir vous voir, à condition de prendre rendez-vous ?* (Can I come back and see you, provided I make an appointment?)
- *Oui, ou venez le matin : nous vous accueillons sans rendez-vous.* (Yes, or come in the morning: we see people without an appointment.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Bien que + subjunctive, always">
  It's tempting to use the indicative after *bien que*, because the fact is true. But **bien qu'il soit fatigué**, never *bien qu'il est fatigué*. In conversation you may hear the indicative, but it's considered a mistake. If you want the indicative, use another word, such as *même si*, which you'll meet later in B2.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Swap the subject">
  Take a sentence with an infinitive, such as *Je pars avant de manger*, and change the second subject: *Je pars avant que tu manges*. Then go back the other way. Switching between the two forms ten times takes a minute, and it fixes the rule better than learning a list.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Bien que, avant que, à condition que, jusqu'à ce que, sans que et à moins que sont toujours suivis du subjonctif. Quand une seule personne fait les deux actions, on prend souvent l'infinitif : avant de partir, sans parler, à condition de venir, à moins de pouvoir. Bien que et jusqu'à ce que gardent que et le subjonctif. Après avant que et à moins que, on peut ajouter un petit ne. Ce ne ne change pas le sens de la phrase.
