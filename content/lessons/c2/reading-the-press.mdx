By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize the main French press genres by their style, name the parts of an article, and say where and how each genre lets an opinion through.

## What you already know

You know the French press and its institutions (*une agence de presse*, *une rédaction*, *une ligne éditoriale*, *un éditorialiste*) from <LessonLink id="b2-culture-french-press" text="the lesson on the French press" />, and *un éditorial*, *un reportage*, *une enquête* and *une interview* from <LessonLink id="b1-media-and-news" text="the lesson on the media" />. You know *un chapeau* from <LessonLink id="b2-summarizing-an-article" text="the lesson on summarizing an article" />, *une tribune* from <LessonLink id="b2-review-nuance" text="the review of nuance" />, and the signs of implicit judgment from <LessonLink id="c2-implicit-meaning" text="the lesson on implicit meaning" />. This lesson puts them together: each genre has its own style, and its own way of giving, or hiding, an opinion.

## Genres that report

<VocabTable group="facts" />

**La dépêche** (an agency wire) is written by a press agency and sent to newspapers, which publish it or use it. It starts with the place and the agency's name, gives the facts in the inverted pyramid, and avoids any opinion. Its sentences are short, its verbs factual (*a ouvert*, *a annoncé*).

**La brève** (a news brief) gives a single piece of news in a few lines, often with no signature.

**Le reportage** is written on the scene, often by *un envoyé spécial* or *une envoyée spéciale* (a special correspondent). It describes what the journalist sees and hears, so it uses the present, details and quotations from witnesses. Its opinion shows in what it chooses to describe.

**L'enquête** (an investigation) takes time: it checks sources, compares documents and explains a situation. **L'interview** gives the questions of the journalist and the answers of the person interviewed; its opinion shows in the choice of the person and of the questions. In a newspaper, these genres are often mixed on the same page: a dépêche gives the news, a reportage tells the scene, and an interview gives one person's voice.

<Example fr="La pyramide inversée place l'essentiel dans la première phrase : qui, quoi, où, quand." en="The inverted pyramid puts the essentials in the first sentence: who, what, where, when." />

*La pyramide inversée* (the inverted pyramid) is the order of most news articles: the most important facts first, then the details, then the background. A reader who stops after the first paragraph still knows the essentials.

## Genres that give an opinion

<VocabTable group="opinion" />

**L'éditorial** gives the position of the newspaper itself, usually signed by the editor or a senior journalist. You know *un éditorialiste* from B2.

**La chronique** (a column) is a regular piece by the same writer, *un chroniqueur* or *une chroniqueuse* (a columnist), on a personal tone. It mixes facts, impressions and judgment, and its readers come back for its voice.

**Le billet d'humeur** is a short, personal opinion piece, often ironic, written in the first person. It takes one fact and reacts to it.

**La tribune** is an opinion piece by *un contributeur extérieur* (an outside contributor): a researcher, an association, a citizen. The paper publishes it without taking it on as its own position.

<Example fr="Un contributeur extérieur signe la tribune, et le journal la publie." en="An outside contributor signs the tribune, and the paper publishes it." />

<Example fr="Dans un billet d'humeur, le journaliste écrit « je » et ne cache pas ce qu'il pense." en="In a short opinion piece, the journalist writes 'I' and doesn't hide what he thinks." />

## The parts of an article

<VocabTable group="parts" />

**La titraille** is the set of headings of an article: *le surtitre* (the kicker, above the title), the title itself and *le sous-titre* (the subtitle). **Le chapeau**, which you know, is the short paragraph in bold under the title that sums up the article. **L'accroche** of an article is its first lines, written to catch the reader: a striking fact, a scene, a quotation. Long articles are cut by *des intertitres* (crossheads).

<Example fr="L'accroche d'un article doit donner envie de lire la suite." en="The lead of an article must make you want to read on." />

<Example fr="Un intertitre coupe un long article en plusieurs parties." en="A crosshead cuts a long article into several parts." />

<Example fr="Le surtitre annonce le lieu, le titre donne l'information, et le chapeau la résume." en="The kicker announces the place, the title gives the news, and the standfirst sums it up." />

## Where opinion hides

Even in genres that report, opinion can come through, and the tools are those of the lesson on implicit meaning:

- **The choice of facts**: what the article mentions first, and what it leaves out.
- **The choice of words**: a connoted adjective, a judging verb (*prétendre*, *avouer*).
- **The quotations**: who is quoted, and who isn't.
- **The headings**: a title written as a question, or with scare quotes, already suggests a view.

Read the genre first: from a dépêche, you expect facts; from a billet, a view. Then check whether the text keeps to its genre.

## The model: one event, three genres

<VocabTable group="event" />

The three texts below are original. The event, the town, the agency (Info-Régions) and the newspaper (*L'Écho de Valbrune*) are invented.

*Valbrune (Info-Régions). Une bibliothèque installée sur une péniche a ouvert samedi sur le canal de Valbrune. Amarrée près de la gare, elle propose environ trois mille livres et sera ouverte du mardi au samedi. Le projet, voté par le conseil municipal il y a deux ans, a coûté moins que prévu, selon la mairie. Plusieurs centaines de visiteurs sont venus le premier jour. Une seconde péniche, consacrée aux enfants, pourrait ouvrir l'an prochain, a indiqué l'adjointe à la culture.*

*Chronique « Au fil de la ville », par Lucie Garnier. Comme chaque lundi, je vous parle de notre ville, et cette semaine d'un bateau qui ne part jamais. Je me suis souvent demandé ce qui fait entrer les gens dans une bibliothèque. Les livres, bien sûr, mais pas seulement : il faut un lieu qui donne envie d'entrer. Une péniche sur le canal, avec son bois neuf et ses fenêtres sur l'eau, est peut-être ce genre de lieu. On a beaucoup discuté de ce projet au conseil municipal, et certains le trouvaient trop coûteux. Je comprends leurs raisons. Mais je crois qu'une ville se juge aussi à ce qu'elle offre presque pour rien : un banc, un jardin, un livre prêté. Si cette péniche donne le goût de la lecture à quelques habitants, elle aura valu son prix. Je reviendrai sur ce sujet dans un mois, quand l'enthousiasme du premier jour sera passé.*

*Billet d'humeur. Une bibliothèque flottante, sur notre vieux canal ! On aura tout vu. Pendant que nos rues attendent toujours leurs nouveaux bancs, la ville offre aux lecteurs une péniche, des coussins et une vue sur le canal. Je ne suis pas contre les livres, bien sûr. On nous promet déjà une seconde péniche pour les enfants. Pourquoi pas une troisième pour les adultes qui attendent leur bus sous la pluie ? Mais la prochaine fois, on pourrait peut-être réparer la route avant de mettre les livres sur l'eau.*

(Valbrune (Info-Régions). A library set up on a barge opened on Saturday on the Valbrune canal. Moored near the station, it offers about three thousand books and will be open from Tuesday to Saturday. The project, voted by the town council two years ago, cost less than planned, according to the town hall. Several hundred visitors came on the first day. A second barge, for children, could open next year, the deputy mayor for culture said. / Column "Au fil de la ville", by Lucie Garnier. As every Monday, I'm talking to you about our town, and this week about a boat that never leaves. I've often wondered what makes people go into a library. Books, of course, but not only: you need a place that makes you want to go in. A barge on the canal, with its new wood and its windows on the water, may be that kind of place. There was a lot of discussion about this project at the town council, and some found it too expensive. I understand their reasons. But I believe that a town is also judged by what it offers for almost nothing: a bench, a garden, a lent book. If this barge gives a few residents a taste for reading, it will have been worth its price. I'll come back to this subject in a month, when the enthusiasm of the first day has passed. / Short opinion piece. A floating library, on our old canal! Now we've seen everything. While our streets are still waiting for their new benches, the town offers readers a barge, cushions and a view of the canal. I'm not against books, of course. We're already promised a second barge for children. Why not a third for the adults waiting for their bus in the rain? But next time, perhaps the road could be repaired before the books are put on the water.)

## How the three texts differ

- **The dépêche** opens with the place and the agency, follows the inverted pyramid, gives figures attributed to a source (*selon la mairie*, *a indiqué l'adjointe*), uses *pourrait ouvrir* (the conditional of *pouvoir*, a possibility) for what isn't decided yet, and has no adjective of judgment.
- **The chronique** has a column name and a regular slot (*« Au fil de la ville »*, *comme chaque lundi*), a reflective first-person voice (*je me suis souvent demandé*, *je crois*), and a judgment drawn from the writer's own view of what a town is for. It mentions the debate fairly (*je comprends leurs raisons*) and promises to come back to the subject.
- **The billet d'humeur** reacts with irony (*on aura tout vu*), opposes the barge to the unrepaired road, and ends with a mocking suggestion. It gives a view, and it says so.

The same facts appear in all three, but not with the same weight: the cost is a fact in the dépêche, a past debate in the chronique, and the whole point of the billet.

## En français

Inès and Hugo are journalism students. They're comparing the three texts for a class. They use *tu*.

- *Hugo, laquelle des trois est la dépêche ?* (Hugo, which of the three is the agency wire?)
- *La première : le lieu et l'agence au début, et seulement des faits.* (The first: the place and the agency at the start, and only facts.)
- *Elle dit quand même que le projet a coûté moins que prévu.* (It does say that the project cost less than planned.)
- *Oui, mais elle dit que l'information vient de la mairie. Ce n'est pas l'avis de l'agence.* (Yes, but it says the information comes from the town hall. It isn't the agency's view.)
- *La deuxième commence par « je ». C'est un billet ?* (The second starts with "je". Is it a billet?)
- *Non, c'est une chronique : elle a un nom, elle revient chaque lundi, et l'autrice réfléchit et donne son point de vue.* (No, it's a column: it has a name, it comes back every Monday, and the writer reflects and gives her view.)
- *Et la troisième, avec « On aura tout vu » ?* (And the third, with "On aura tout vu"?)
- *Ça, c'est un billet d'humeur : court, ironique, et très personnel.* (That's a short opinion piece: short, ironic, and very personal.)
- *Si on voulait la position du journal, il faudrait lire quoi ?* (If we wanted the paper's position, what would we have to read?)
- *L'éditorial. Et si un lecteur extérieur voulait répondre, il écrirait une tribune.* (The editorial. And if an outside reader wanted to respond, he'd write a tribune.)
- *Pour notre exposé, je présente la dépêche et la chronique ?* (For our presentation, shall I present the wire and the column?)
- *D'accord, et je prends le billet. C'est le plus amusant à analyser.* (OK, and I'll take the billet. It's the most fun to analyse.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une tribune isn't the paper's opinion">
  Learners often quote a tribune as if it were the newspaper's view: *le journal pense que…* A tribune is written by an outside contributor, and the paper only publishes it. Attribute it to its author: **dans une tribune publiée par le journal, une chercheuse estime que…** Keep *le journal estime* for the éditorial.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read the signature and the heading first">
  Before you read an article, look at three things: the heading of the section (*Opinions*, *Chronique*, *Actualité*), the signature (a journalist, an agency, an outside contributor) and the first line. Together, they tell you the genre, and so what to expect: facts, a voice, or a position.
</Callout>
