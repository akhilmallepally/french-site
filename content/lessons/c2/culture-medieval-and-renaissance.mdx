## The Middle Ages

<VocabTable group="middle" />

**La Chanson de Roland.** *La Chanson de Roland*, dated to around 1100, is *une chanson de geste* (an epic poem about heroic deeds). Its oldest surviving manuscript, kept at Oxford, dates from the 12th century. The poem is *anonyme* (anonymous): its author's name hasn't come down to us. It tells the story of Roland, *un chevalier* (a knight) of Charlemagne's army.

<Example fr="« Halt sunt li pui e li val tenebrus » (La Chanson de Roland, vers 814)" en="Our modern French translation: « Hauts sont les monts, et sombres les vallées ». Our English translation: &quot;High are the hills, and dark the valleys.&quot;" />

**Chrétien de Troyes.** Writing in the late 12th century, Chrétien de Troyes is a major author of *le roman courtois* (the courtly romance): long stories of knights, their adventures and their love.

**Le Roman de la Rose.** *Le Roman de la Rose*, from the 13th century, is often described as *un poème allégorique* (an allegorical poem). It has two authors: Guillaume de Lorris began it, and Jean de Meun continued it.

**François Villon.** François Villon was born in 1431. Nothing is known of him after 1463. His poems are read today for their mix of humour, regret and the thought of death.

## The Renaissance

<VocabTable group="renaissance" />

**L'humanisme.** In the 16th century, *la Renaissance* brought a movement called *l'humanisme* (humanism): scholars and writers went back to the texts of Antiquity, in Greek and Latin, and put their trust in education and knowledge.

**François Rabelais.** Rabelais published *Pantagruel* in 1532 and *Gargantua* in 1534, the comic stories of two *géants* (giants), full of learning, play with words and humour. He died in 1553.

**Du Bellay et la Pléiade.** In 1549, Joachim Du Bellay published the *Défense et illustration de la langue française*, which set out to *défendre la langue française* (defend the French language) as a language worthy of great poetry. He belonged to *la Pléiade*, a group of poets.

**Pierre de Ronsard.** Ronsard (1524–1585) is one of the best-known poets of the Pléiade. One of his *odes* (odes) is quoted in the model text below.

**Louise Labé.** Louise Labé's *Œuvres* were published in 1555. In 2006, a scholar argued that the work was *une création collective* (a collective creation) by a group of poets, which opened a debate on *la paternité de l'œuvre* (its authorship). Other scholars reject this argument. The debate is still open, and this lesson gives no verdict.

## Reading old spelling

<VocabTable group="reading" />

An extract in *l'orthographe d'origine* (the original spelling) looks strange, but a few habits help:

- **Read it aloud slowly**, with *une traduction en français moderne* (a translation into modern French) beside it.
- **Look for familiar endings**: Renaissance spelling writes *-oit* where modern French writes *-ait* (*avoit*, *avait*), and often adds an *s* that's no longer written (*desclose*, *ceste*).
- **Expect other words in Old French**: *pui* (hills) and *tenebrus* (dark) aren't modern words in old spelling, but words the language later changed or lost.

These habits are for reading only. Writing, in this course, is always in modern French.

<Example fr="Une citation garde l'orthographe d'origine du texte qu'on copie." en="A quotation keeps the original spelling of the text you copy." />

Here they are applied to the two lines of Ronsard quoted in the model text:

- **Mignonne** is a term of affection, addressed to the woman the poem speaks to.
- **Avoit** is *avait*: the *-oit* spelling of the imperfect.
- **Desclose** is *déclose* (opened): where modern spelling would write *é*, the edition writes *es*.
- **The sentence goes on**: what the rose had opened comes in the next line of the poem, which isn't quoted here. That's why our translation ends with three dots.

## The model: an article on the literature before the classics

The article below is original, written for the lesson. It quotes the first two lines of an ode by Ronsard in the spelling of a public edition, for recognition only, with our own modern French and English translations.

*Avant les classiques. On croit parfois que la littérature française commence au XVIIe siècle. Elle est pourtant bien plus ancienne. Ces siècles, qu'on appelle le Moyen Âge puis la Renaissance, ont laissé des œuvres que l'on lit encore. Vers 1100, La Chanson de Roland raconte la mort d'un chevalier de Charlemagne : c'est une chanson de geste, et son auteur est anonyme. À la fin du XIIe siècle, Chrétien de Troyes écrit des romans courtois, où les chevaliers vivent de grandes aventures et où l'amour tient une grande place.*

*Au XIIIe siècle, Le Roman de la Rose est un poème à deux auteurs : Guillaume de Lorris le commence, Jean de Meun le continue. Au XVe siècle, François Villon, né en 1431, mêle dans ses poèmes le rire et la pensée de la mort ; on ne sait rien de lui après 1463.*

*Au XVIe siècle, l'humanisme redécouvre les textes de l'Antiquité. Rabelais publie Pantagruel en 1532 et Gargantua en 1534, deux histoires de géants pleines de savoir et de jeux de mots. Il meurt en 1553. En 1549, Du Bellay publie la Défense et illustration de la langue française : il veut montrer que le français peut porter une grande poésie. Avec Ronsard, il appartient au groupe de la Pléiade.*

*Ronsard écrit une ode célèbre sur la rose qui se fane en un jour, image de la jeunesse qui passe. Elle commence ainsi : « Mignonne, allons voir si la rose / Qui ce matin avoit desclose ». En 1555 paraissent les Œuvres de Louise Labé ; en 2006, un chercheur a soutenu qu'elles étaient une création collective, et d'autres rejettent cette thèse.*

*Ces textes demandent un effort au lecteur d'aujourd'hui. Mais avec une traduction à côté, on y entend déjà la langue qui deviendra la nôtre.*

*Les deux vers de Ronsard, en français moderne (notre traduction) : « Mignonne, allons voir si la rose qui, ce matin, avait ouvert… »*

(Before the classics. People sometimes think French literature begins in the 17th century. Yet it is much older. These centuries, which we call the Middle Ages and then the Renaissance, have left works that are still read. Around 1100, La Chanson de Roland tells of the death of a knight of Charlemagne: it is a chanson de geste, and its author is anonymous. At the end of the 12th century, Chrétien de Troyes writes courtly romances, in which knights live great adventures and love has a large place. In the 13th century, Le Roman de la Rose is a poem with two authors: Guillaume de Lorris begins it, Jean de Meun continues it. In the 15th century, François Villon, born in 1431, mixes laughter and the thought of death in his poems; nothing is known of him after 1463. In the 16th century, humanism rediscovers the texts of Antiquity. Rabelais publishes Pantagruel in 1532 and Gargantua in 1534, two stories of giants full of learning and wordplay. He dies in 1553. In 1549, Du Bellay publishes the Défense et illustration de la langue française: he wants to show that French can carry great poetry. With Ronsard, he belongs to the group of the Pléiade. Ronsard writes a famous ode on the rose that fades in a day, an image of passing youth. It begins: "Mignonne, allons voir si la rose / Qui ce matin avoit desclose". In 1555, Louise Labé's Œuvres appear; in 2006, a scholar argued that they were a collective creation, and others reject this argument. These texts ask an effort of today's reader. But with a translation alongside, you can already hear in them the language that will become ours. / Ronsard's two lines, in modern French (our translation): "My sweet, let's go and see whether the rose that, this morning, had opened…")

## How the article works

- **The order**: from around 1100 to 1555, century by century, with one or two names per period.
- **One tense per paragraph**: the historical present tells each century (*raconte*, *écrit*, *publie*); the last paragraph speaks of today.
- **The debate about Louise Labé** is attributed (*un chercheur a soutenu*, *d'autres rejettent*), with no verdict.
- **The quotation**: two lines of Ronsard, under 15 words, in the spelling of the edition, with our own translation at the end. The article makes no claim about their metre.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't modernize a quotation">
  When they quote Ronsard, learners often correct the spelling as they copy: *qui ce matin avait déclose*. A quotation keeps the spelling of its edition: **« Qui ce matin avoit desclose »**. Put the modern version beside it, labelled as a translation, never in its place.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn one line per author">
  For each author of this lesson, learn one short line or one title, and the date that goes with it: *Halt sunt li pui* for Roland, *Pantagruel* (1532) for Rabelais, the *Défense* (1549) for Du Bellay, *Mignonne, allons voir si la rose* for Ronsard. A name with a line attached is far easier to remember than a list of names.
</Callout>
