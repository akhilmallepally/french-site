By the end of this lesson you'll be able to place the main movements and authors of French literature since 1900, talk about the literary season and its prizes, and recognize a constrained text such as a lipogram.

## What you already know

You know Proust, Sartre, Camus, Beauvoir, Duras and Annie Ernaux, and *l'existentialisme*, from <LessonLink id="c1-culture-literary-canon" text="the lesson on the literary canon" />. That lesson presents Camus as often linked with existentialism, though he rejected the label, and this lesson keeps that description. You know *le surréalisme* from <LessonLink id="b2-arts-and-literature" text="the lesson on arts and literature" />, and *remettre en question* (to question) from <LessonLink id="c1-philosophy-and-ideas" text="the lesson on philosophy and ideas" />. *Une lettre* is a letter of the alphabet here, as in <LessonLink id="a1-alphabet-and-accents" text="the lesson on the alphabet" />.

**No author of this lesson is quoted.** All of them died after 1900, or are alive, so their ideas are paraphrased. Living authors are named with their works only. Labels are given as "often linked with", because writers don't always accept the movement they're put in.

## Movements of the twentieth century

<VocabTable group="movements" />

**Marcel Proust.** *À la recherche du temps perdu*, Proust's long novel of memory, was published from 1913 to 1927.

**Le surréalisme.** In 1924, André Breton published the first *manifeste* (manifesto) of surrealism. The movement is often described as wanting to free the imagination from reason and habit, through dreams and chance.

**Le Nouveau Roman.** In the 1950s, *le Nouveau Roman* (the "new novel") is a name given to writers who *remettaient en question* (questioned) the traditional novel, with its plot and its characters. Alain Robbe-Grillet and Nathalie Sarraute are often linked with it.

**L'autofiction.** In 1977, Serge Doubrovsky *a forgé le mot* (coined the word) *autofiction*, for books that mix the author's own life and fiction. The term has since been used for many other writers.

<Example fr="Un mouvement littéraire réunit des écrivains autour de quelques idées communes." en="A literary movement brings writers together around a few shared ideas." />

<Example fr="Nathalie Sarraute est souvent associée au Nouveau Roman." en="Nathalie Sarraute is often linked with the Nouveau Roman." />

<Example fr="Ces romanciers ont remis en question l'intrigue et le personnage." en="These novelists questioned plot and character." />

## Writing under constraint

<VocabTable group="constraint" />

**L'Oulipo.** The Oulipo was founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais. Its members write with *une contrainte d'écriture* (a writing constraint): a rule chosen in advance, which, for its members, can be a way of finding new ideas.

**La Disparition.** The best-known example is Georges Perec's *La Disparition* (1969), a whole novel without *la lettre e* (the letter e). A text that never uses one letter is called *un lipogramme* (a lipogram). In French, *e* is a very frequent letter, so a lipogram in *e* forbids *le*, *de*, *et*, *est* and most feminine endings.

<Example fr="Un lipogramme en e interdit les mots « le », « de » et « est »." en="A lipogram in e forbids the words 'le', 'de' and 'est'." />

## The literary season and its prizes

<VocabTable group="books" />

**La rentrée littéraire.** At the end of the summer, French *éditeurs* (publishers) bring out a large number of novels in a few weeks. This is *la rentrée littéraire*. *Les libraires* (booksellers) choose which books to put forward, and the press publishes its lists.

**Le prix Goncourt.** In the autumn come *les prix littéraires* (literary prizes). The prix Goncourt, one of the best known, was first awarded in 1903.

**Le prix Nobel de littérature.** Several writers in French have received the Nobel Prize in Literature: Camus in 1957, Sartre in 1964 (he declined it), Claude Simon in 1985, J. M. G. Le Clézio in 2008, Patrick Modiano in 2014 and Annie Ernaux in 2022.

<Example fr="Le prix Goncourt est décerné à l'automne, pendant la rentrée littéraire." en="The prix Goncourt is awarded in the autumn, during the literary season." />

<Example fr="Sartre a refusé le prix Nobel de littérature en 1964." en="Sartre declined the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1964." />

## Talking about living authors

As a rule of thumb, when you write about a living author:

- **Name the work and the date**, not the private life.
- **Paraphrase** the ideas in your own words; don't quote.
- **Attribute a label** (*souvent associé à*, *selon la critique*) instead of stating it as a fact.

<Example fr="Une étiquette ne dit pas tout d'un écrivain : beaucoup d'auteurs refusent celle qu'on leur donne." en="A label doesn't say everything about a writer: many authors reject the one they're given." />

## Paraphrasing instead of quoting

Since none of these authors can be quoted, you'll often need to report an idea in your own words. Three habits keep a paraphrase honest:

- **Name the source** at the start: *selon Breton…*, *pour les membres de l'Oulipo…*, *d'après Doubrovsky…*
- **Keep the strength of the idea**: if the writers say a constraint *can* help, don't write that it always does.
- **Use your own sentence**, not the author's with a few words changed; a close copy is still a quotation.

<Example fr="Pour les membres de l'Oulipo, une contrainte peut aider à trouver des idées." en="For the members of the Oulipo, a constraint can help you find ideas." />

## The model: an article on the literary season

The article below is original, written for the lesson.

*La rentrée littéraire. Chaque année, à la fin de l'été, les éditeurs publient un grand nombre de romans : c'est la rentrée littéraire. Les libraires font leurs choix, les journaux publient leurs listes, et les lecteurs se demandent par où commencer. Pour beaucoup d'entre eux, c'est un grand moment de l'année. À l'automne viennent les prix littéraires, dont le prix Goncourt, décerné pour la première fois en 1903.*

*Cette saison réunit des écrivains de toutes les générations, et des héritages très différents. Certains pensent à Proust et à son long roman de la mémoire, publié de 1913 à 1927. D'autres rappellent le Nouveau Roman des années 1950, qui se méfiait de l'intrigue et du personnage. D'autres encore viennent de l'autofiction, un mot forgé par Serge Doubrovsky en 1977 pour des livres qui mêlent la vie de l'auteur et la fiction. La critique classe ces livres en courants, mais les auteurs n'acceptent pas toujours ces étiquettes.*

*Et puis il y a les joueurs : les héritiers de l'Oulipo, fondé en 1960 par Raymond Queneau et François Le Lionnais, qui écrivent sous contrainte. Le modèle reste La Disparition de Georges Perec, en 1969, un roman entier sans la lettre e. Pour montrer à quoi ressemble ce jeu, voici, ci-dessous, un court texte écrit pour cette leçon sur le même principe.*

(The literary season. Every year, at the end of summer, publishers bring out a large number of novels: this is the rentrée littéraire. Booksellers make their choices, the papers publish their lists, and readers wonder where to begin. For many of them, it's a high point of the year. In the autumn come the literary prizes, among them the prix Goncourt, first awarded in 1903. This season brings together writers of every generation, and very different legacies. Some think of Proust and his long novel of memory, published from 1913 to 1927. Others recall the Nouveau Roman of the 1950s, which distrusted plot and character. Others again come from autofiction, a word coined by Serge Doubrovsky in 1977 for books that mix the author's life and fiction. Critics sort these books into movements, but authors don't always accept these labels. And then there are the players: the heirs of the Oulipo, founded in 1960 by Raymond Queneau and François Le Lionnais, who write under constraint. The model is still Georges Perec's La Disparition, in 1969, a whole novel without the letter e. To show what this game looks like, here is a short text written for this lesson on the same principle.)

## The model: a lipogram

The text below is an original lipogram, written for the lesson, about a young reader at a book fair. It's told in the passé simple, the tense of literary narrative.

Missing letter: *e*.

*Un soir d'août, à Paris, un garçon courut au salon du roman. Il lut durant six mois, il finit Proust, il prit un pari : un roman par jour, sans faillir.*

*Mais là, quoi choisir ? Romans, polars, albums : il paya, il paya, il paya. Pourtant, au fond du stand, un individu souriant lui offrit un roman où manquait un son si commun qu'il fallut un mois au garçon pour y voir clair.*

*Il lut tout, ravi, jusqu'au bout.*

*Aujourd'hui, à son tour, il polit un roman court, sans un a. Il a du travail.*

(One August evening, in Paris, a boy ran to the novel fair. He had read for six months, he had finished Proust, he had made a bet: a novel a day, without fail. But there, what to choose? Novels, crime novels, picture books: he paid, he paid, he paid. Yet, at the back of the stand, a smiling stranger offered him a novel in which a sound so common was missing that it took the boy a month to see it clearly. He read it all, delighted, to the end. Today, in his turn, he is polishing a short novel, without a single a. He has work to do.)

## How the texts work

- **The article** gives each fact its date and its kind (*publié*, *décerné pour la première fois*, *fondé*, *forgé*) and labels nothing by force: the Nouveau Roman is described by what it distrusted, not by a verdict.
- **The lipogram** never uses *e*, *é*, *è*, *ê* or *œ*. The constraint shapes the style: no *le*, *de* or *et*, so the text uses *un*, *du*, *au* and lists without a connector (*il paya, il paya, il paya*). It was checked by script, not by eye.
- **One tense per paragraph**: the passé simple tells the story (*courut*, *lut*, *paya*, *offrit*); the last paragraph is in the present, for today.
- **The joke at the end**: the boy now writes his own lipogram, in *a*. The *a* is just mentioned there, as a letter.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't call a living author a member of a movement">
  When they write about recent literature, learners often turn a critic's label into a fact: *Nathalie Sarraute est une romancière du Nouveau Roman, point final*. Writers often discuss or reject the labels put on them. Attribute the label: **Nathalie Sarraute est souvent associée au Nouveau Roman**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Try a constraint for ten minutes">
  Take any short paragraph you've written in French and rewrite it without one letter: *e* if you're brave, *u* or *a* to start with. You'll have to look for synonyms and new constructions, and you'll find words you didn't know you knew. That's the Oulipo's idea of a constraint.
</Callout>
