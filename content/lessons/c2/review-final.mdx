## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words name where you are now. *La maîtrise* (mastery) of a language is the ability to use it with precision, range and ease. It isn't the same as speaking like *un locuteur natif* (a native speaker): a native speaker doesn't necessarily master every register, and an advanced *apprenant* (learner) may write with more care. And mastery isn't an end: every level, C2 included, leaves room for *un perfectionnement* (further refinement).

<Example fr="La maîtrise d'une langue ne veut pas dire parler comme un locuteur natif." en="Mastering a language doesn't mean speaking like a native speaker." />

<Example fr="Même à ce niveau, chaque apprenant peut viser un perfectionnement." en="Even at this level, every learner can aim for further refinement." />

## What you can now do

At the end of C2, you can do each of the following, with the unit that taught it in mind:

- **Write with exact form**, including the agreements and concordances that careful writers check twice.
- **Choose the exact word**, and avoid a paronym, a false friend or an anglicism a reader would notice.
- **Read and restate** legal, medical, scientific and administrative texts without losing a condition.
- **Read between the lines**: presuppositions, connotations and fallacies, in the press and in literature.
- **Write long texts** (a contraction, an essay, a translation) and revise them sentence by sentence.
- **Mediate and negotiate**: relay a text faithfully, reach an agreement, argue aloud and ask precise questions.
- **Place French in its history**, from the Strasbourg Oaths to the literatures written in French today.
- **Move between registers and styles** on purpose, and imitate a style honestly.

## How to keep improving

C2 isn't a finishing line. As a rule of thumb, three habits keep a high level alive:

- **Read widely**, in genres you don't usually read: a legal text one week, a poem the next.
- **Write regularly**, and revise with the checklists of Unit 5.
- **Notice**: keep a list of the words, idioms and turns of phrase that surprise you, with their register.

## The model: Mon rapport à la langue française

The text below is invented, written for the lesson: a learner who has reached C2 looks back. It isn't by a real person.

*Mon rapport à la langue française (texte inventé). Le français est entré dans ma vie par la petite porte : une chanson entendue à la radio, dont je ne comprenais pas un mot. J'ai voulu savoir ce qu'elle disait, et je n'ai plus arrêté. J'avais un dictionnaire, beaucoup de patience, et aucune idée du chemin qui m'attendait.*

*Les premières années, la langue m'a résisté de toutes les façons possibles. Je confondais les registres, j'accordais mal les participes, et je traduisais mot à mot des expressions qui ne se traduisent pas. Je croyais aussi les mots qui ressemblent à l'anglais, sans penser aux faux amis. Un jour, j'ai écrit à une administration une lettre que je croyais polie ; elle était surtout trop familière. J'ai appris, ce jour-là, que la politesse française a ses formules.*

*Puis la langue est devenue une compagne. J'ai lu La Fontaine, dont la morale vient parfois en premier, et j'ai découvert que l'humour français aime jouer sur les sons. J'ai écrit des contractions, des essais, et même un petit pastiche, signalé comme tel. J'ai aussi appris à servir d'intermédiaire, pour expliquer à une voisine un avis de la mairie sans rien oublier.*

*Aujourd'hui, je ne parle pas comme un locuteur natif, et ce n'est plus mon but. Mon accent reste le mien. Ce que je cherche, c'est la justesse : le mot exact, le ton qui convient, la nuance qui change tout. Je relis mes textes en me demandant, comme on me l'a appris, si chaque phrase dit ce que je veux dire, et rien de plus. Je sais aussi changer de registre quand je le veux, et le signaler.*

*La maîtrise d'une langue n'est jamais finie. Un perfectionnement m'attend encore, à l'oral surtout. Mais j'ai gardé de mes débuts ce qui compte le plus : la curiosité, et le plaisir d'entendre une phrase française qui sonne juste.*

(My relationship with the French language (an invented text). French came into my life by the back door: a song heard on the radio, of which I didn't understand a word. I wanted to know what it said, and I never stopped. I had a dictionary, a lot of patience, and no idea of the road that lay ahead of me. In the first years, the language resisted me in every possible way. I mixed up registers, made participles agree wrongly, and translated word for word expressions that don't translate. I also trusted words that look like English, without thinking of false friends. One day, I wrote a letter to an administration that I thought was polite; above all, it was too familiar. I learned that day that French politeness has its formulas. Then the language became a companion. I read La Fontaine, whose moral sometimes comes first, and I discovered that French humour likes to play on sounds. I wrote contractions, essays, and even a short pastiche, labelled as such. I also learned to act as a go-between, to explain a notice from the town hall to a neighbour without leaving anything out. Today, I don't speak like a native speaker, and that's no longer my aim. My accent remains my own. What I'm looking for is accuracy: the exact word, the right tone, the nuance that changes everything. I reread my texts asking myself, as I was taught, whether each sentence says what I mean, and nothing more. I also know how to change register when I want to, and to signal it. Mastering a language is never finished. Further refinement still awaits me, in speaking above all. But I've kept from my beginnings what matters most: curiosity, and the pleasure of hearing a French sentence that rings true.)

## How the closing text works

- **One register throughout**: everyday written French, in the first person, with no familiar word and no deliberate break.
- **What it draws on**: the registers and the formulas of an official letter, mediation (relaying a notice without leaving anything out), idioms that don't translate, La Fontaine's moral placed first, wordplay on sounds, the contraction, the essay and a labelled pastiche.
- **A plan in five steps**: a first contact, the resistance, the companionship, today, and what remains to do. The writing task follows the same plan.
- **No exam, no level claim**: the text speaks of a relationship with a language, not of a test.

<Callout type="warning" title="Mastery isn't a native-speaker imitation">
  At the end of a long course, learners sometimes judge themselves only against native speakers: *je ne parlerai jamais comme un Français, donc je n'ai pas réussi*. Mastery is precision, range and ease in your own voice. Say it as the closing text does: **je ne parle pas comme un locuteur natif, et ce n'est plus mon but**.
</Callout>

</Callout>
