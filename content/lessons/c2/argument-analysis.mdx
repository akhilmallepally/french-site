## What you already know

You know *réfuter* (to refute) from <LessonLink id="b2-debating" text="the lesson on debating" />, and presuppositions from <LessonLink id="c2-implicit-meaning" text="the last lesson" />. This lesson gives each move its name, so that you can analyse an argument rather than just answer it.

## Premises and conclusions

<VocabTable group="terms" />

An argument starts from *une prémisse* (a premise) and draws a conclusion. Many arguments leave a premise unstated: that's *une prémisse implicite* (an implicit premise). *Il est jeune, donc il ne comprend pas* rests on an unstated claim, that young people don't understand. Making that premise explicit is often the best way to answer: once it's said aloud, it has to be defended.

<Example fr="Cela suppose que les jeunes ne comprennent rien, et c'est justement ce qu'il faudrait prouver." en="That assumes that young people understand nothing, which is exactly what would have to be proved." />

<Example fr="Un raisonnement fallacieux peut sembler solide quand on l'entend vite." en="A fallacious piece of reasoning can seem sound when you hear it quickly." />

*Un sophisme* (a fallacy) is an argument that looks valid but isn't. Its conclusion may even be true; the problem is that the reasons given don't support it. You answer a fallacy with *une objection* (an objection), and often with *un contre-exemple* (a counterexample), a single case that contradicts a general claim.

## Two valid moves and their limits

<VocabTable group="valid" />

**L'argument par l'exemple** (the argument from example) supports a claim with a concrete case: *à Saint-Ours, le festival a fait revivre les commerces*. It's legitimate, but one example illustrates rather than proves. The question to ask is whether the example is *représentatif* (representative).

**L'argument par analogie** (the argument from analogy) compares the situation with another one that the listener already judges: *un festival, c'est comme une fête de famille*. It's persuasive, and it works as long as the two situations are alike in the way that matters. To answer it, show where the comparison stops: a family party lasts one evening, a festival several days.

<Example fr="L'argument par analogie compare deux situations qui se ressemblent." en="The argument from analogy compares two situations that are alike." />

You already know **l'argument d'autorité**: it relies on who says something rather than on why. It's valid when the authority is competent on that very question, and weak otherwise.

<Example fr="Cet exemple est intéressant, mais un seul village ne suffit pas pour conclure." en="This example is interesting, but one village isn't enough to draw a conclusion." />

## Five fallacies

<VocabTable group="fallacies" />

These five are the most common in debates and opinion pieces. Each has a typical answer.

- **The ad hominem argument** (*l'argument ad hominem*) attacks the person instead of the idea: *elle n'habite même pas ici*. Answer by bringing the discussion back to the argument: *revenons à ce qu'elle a dit*.
- **The slippery slope** (*la pente glissante*) claims that a first step will lead, step by step, to a disaster: *un festival cette année, trois l'an prochain, et le parc deviendra une salle de concert*. Answer by asking for the evidence of each link.
- **The false dilemma** (*le faux dilemme*) offers only two options, as if no third existed: *soit on protège notre calme, soit on vend le village*. Answer with a third option.
- **The hasty generalization** (*la généralisation hâtive*) turns one or two cases into a general rule. Answer with a counterexample, or by asking how many cases there are.
- **The appeal to the majority** (*l'appel à la majorité*) presents what many people think as proof: *tout le monde veut ce festival*. Answer that a number of supporters isn't a reason.

<Example fr="Il existe une troisième solution : un festival plus court, qui se termine à minuit." en="There's a third solution: a shorter festival that ends at midnight." />

A fallacy isn't always deliberate. In a heated debate, careful speakers make them too. When you analyse a text, name the move and say why it doesn't support the conclusion; don't accuse the speaker of lying.

## How to analyse an argument

As a rule of thumb, analyse each argument in four steps.

1. **Find the conclusion**: what does the speaker want the listener to accept?
2. **Find the reason given**, and any premise left unstated.
3. **Name the move**: an example, an analogy, an authority, or one of the five fallacies.
4. **Judge its strength**: is the example representative, the analogy relevant, the authority competent? If the move is a fallacy, say what's missing.

An analysis that only lists the names of the moves isn't enough. What matters is the last step: why this argument does or doesn't support this conclusion.

## The model: a debate in a village

<VocabTable group="debate" />

The debate below is original. The village, the festival and the speakers are invented. It was held in the village hall of Valbrune, and the moderator, M. Faure, opens it.

*M. Faure : Ce soir, une seule question : Valbrune doit-elle accueillir un festival de musique dans le parc, cet été ? Rappelons les faits : le comité des fêtes propose une semaine de concerts en juillet, et la mairie n'a pas encore donné son avis. Madame Lambert, vous êtes pour.*

*Mme Lambert : Oui. À Saint-Ours, un village de la même taille, le festival a fait revivre les commerces en trois ans. Et un festival, c'est comme une fête de famille : un peu de bruit, mais beaucoup de liens entre les gens.*

*M. Rivière : Je ne suis pas d'accord. D'abord, Mme Lambert n'habite même pas près du parc : elle ne peut pas comprendre le problème.*

*M. Faure : Monsieur Rivière, revenons au festival, s'il vous plaît.*

*M. Rivière : Très bien. Si on accepte un festival cette année, il y en aura trois l'année prochaine, et le parc deviendra une salle de concert permanente. Mon voisin a été réveillé à deux heures du matin par un concert, l'été dernier : les festivals empêchent tout le monde de dormir. Au fond, c'est simple : soit on protège notre calme, soit on vend le village aux touristes.*

*Mme Lambert : Il existe une troisième solution : un festival de trois jours, qui se termine à minuit. Et un seul voisin ne fait pas une règle générale : à Saint-Ours, les habitants du centre ont eux-mêmes demandé un deuxième festival.*

*M. Faure : Une habitante, Mme Benali, demande la parole.*

*Mme Benali : J'ai lu en ligne des centaines de messages : tout le monde veut ce festival. La question est donc réglée.*

*Mme Lambert : Je vous remercie, mais le nombre de messages ne prouve rien. Ce qui compte, ce sont les raisons, et les résultats de Saint-Ours sont publics : chacun peut les lire.*

*M. Faure : Merci à tous. Les habitants voteront le mois prochain, à la mairie, après une réunion publique.*

(Mr Faure: Tonight, one question only: should Valbrune host a music festival in the park this summer? Let's recall the facts: the festivals committee is proposing a week of concerts in July, and the town hall hasn't given its view yet. Mrs Lambert, you're in favour. Mrs Lambert: Yes. In Saint-Ours, a village of the same size, the festival brought the shops back to life within three years. And a festival is like a family party: a little noise, but a lot of ties between people. Mr Rivière: I don't agree. First, Mrs Lambert doesn't even live near the park: she can't understand the problem. Mr Faure: Mr Rivière, let's come back to the festival, please. Mr Rivière: Very well. If we accept a festival this year, there will be three next year, and the park will become a permanent concert hall. My neighbour was woken up at two in the morning by a concert last summer: festivals stop everyone sleeping. Basically, it's simple: either we protect our peace, or we sell the village to tourists. Mrs Lambert: There's a third solution: a three-day festival that ends at midnight. And one neighbour doesn't make a general rule: in Saint-Ours, the residents of the centre themselves asked for a second festival. Mr Faure: A resident, Mrs Benali, is asking to speak. Mrs Benali: I've read hundreds of messages online: everyone wants this festival. So the question is settled. Mrs Lambert: Thank you, but the number of messages proves nothing. What counts are the reasons, and the results from Saint-Ours are public: anyone can read them. Mr Faure: Thank you, everyone. The residents will vote next month, at the town hall, after a public meeting.)

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

## How the debate works

- **An argument from example**, in Mme Lambert's first line: the case of Saint-Ours. It's legitimate, but is one village representative?
- **An argument from analogy**, in the same line: the festival compared with a family party. It stops working when you remember that a festival lasts several days.
- **An ad hominem argument**, in M. Rivière's first line: it attacks where Mme Lambert lives. The moderator answers it correctly, by coming back to the subject.
- **A slippery slope**, in his second line: one festival, then three, then a permanent concert hall, with no evidence for the steps.
- **A hasty generalization**, next: one neighbour woken up becomes "everyone".
- **A false dilemma**, at the end of the same line: protect the peace or sell the village. Mme Lambert answers it with a third option, and the generalization with the obvious point that one case isn't a rule.
- **An appeal to the majority**, from Mme Benali: hundreds of messages are presented as proof. Mme Lambert answers it the right way, by coming back to the reasons.

## En français

Chloé and Malo are preparing an analysis of the Valbrune debate for their class. They use *tu*.

- *Malo, tu as compté combien de procédés dans le débat ?* (Malo, how many moves did you count in the debate?)
- *Sept, avec la remarque de Mme Benali. Mme Lambert commence par un argument par l'exemple, avec Saint-Ours.* (Seven, with Mrs Benali's remark. Mrs Lambert starts with an argument from example, with Saint-Ours.)
- *Mais ce n'est pas un sophisme, si ?* (But that's not a fallacy, is it?)
- *Non, c'est un argument solide. Il faut seulement se demander si l'exemple est représentatif.* (No, it's a sound argument. You just have to ask whether the example is representative.)
- *Et la fête de famille ?* (And the family party?)
- *C'est une analogie. Elle convainc, mais une fête dure une soirée, pas trois jours.* (It's an analogy. It's convincing, but a party lasts an evening, not three days.)
- *M. Rivière, lui, critique Mme Lambert parce qu'elle n'habite pas près du parc.* (Mr Rivière criticizes Mrs Lambert because she doesn't live near the park.)
- *C'est un argument ad hominem. Le modérateur a raison de revenir au sujet.* (That's an ad hominem argument. The moderator is right to come back to the subject.)
- *Ensuite, il dit qu'un festival en entraînera trois.* (Then he says one festival will lead to three.)
- *Une pente glissante : il ne prouve aucune des étapes.* (A slippery slope: he doesn't prove any of the steps.)
- *Et l'histoire du voisin ?* (And the story about the neighbour?)
- *Une généralisation hâtive. Un seul cas, et il conclut pour tout le monde.* (A hasty generalization. A single case, and he draws a conclusion for everyone.)
- *Il reste le « soit… soit ». C'est un faux dilemme ?* (There's still the "either… or". Is that a false dilemma?)
- *Oui, et Mme Lambert y répond très bien, avec une troisième solution.* (Yes, and Mrs Lambert answers it very well, with a third solution.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Analyse the argument, not the speaker">
  When learners first meet fallacies, they often write *M. Rivière ment* or *M. Rivière est malhonnête*. That's itself an attack on the person. Describe the move and its weakness instead: **M. Rivière généralise à partir d'un seul cas**, **son raisonnement repose sur une prémisse implicite**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Say the hidden premise aloud">
  For each argument that seems convincing but leaves you uneasy, complete the sentence *cela suppose que…* out loud. If the completed sentence is something the speaker would never dare to state (*cela suppose que les jeunes ne comprennent rien*), you've found the weak point of the argument.
</Callout>
