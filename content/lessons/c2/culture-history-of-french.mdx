**Dates in the history of a language are approximate.** The periods below are the usual ones, and the lesson says "roughly" or "around" where historians do.

## From Latin to French

<VocabTable group="origins" />

**Le latin vulgaire.** French comes from the Latin spoken in Gaul, usually called *le latin vulgaire* (Vulgar Latin): the everyday Latin of the people, not the Latin of written works. Over several centuries, this spoken Latin changed so much that it became a different language. Spanish, Italian and the other Romance languages come from the same source.

**Les Serments de Strasbourg (842).** In 842, two grandsons of Charlemagne swore *des serments* (oaths) of alliance at Strasbourg, each in the language of the other's army. The text that has come down to us, in *un manuscrit* (a manuscript), is often described as the oldest surviving text in an early form of French. Its first words are quoted in the model text below, for recognition only.

**La langue d'oïl et la langue d'oc.** In the Middle Ages, the dialects of the north and those of the south were grouped by their word for "yes": *oïl* in the north, which became *oui*, and *oc* in the south. The French of today grew from the *langue d'oïl*; the *langue d'oc* is the source of Occitan, which you know as a regional language.

<Example fr="Le français vient du latin vulgaire, le latin que parlait le peuple." en="French comes from Vulgar Latin, the Latin the people spoke." />

## The periods of French

<VocabTable group="periods" />

Historians usually divide the history of French into four *périodes* (periods). The limits are approximate, and each *siècle* (century) is written in Roman numerals:

- **Old French**, *l'ancien français*: roughly from the IXe to the XIVe siècle.
- **Middle French**, *le moyen français*: from the XIVe to the XVIe siècle.
- **Classical French**, *le français classique*: the XVIIe and XVIIIe siècles.
- **Modern French**, *le français moderne*: after that, up to today.

<Example fr="Le moyen français se situe environ entre le XIVe et le XVIe siècle." en="Middle French sits roughly between the 14th and the 16th century." />

## The state and the language

<VocabTable group="policy" />

**L'ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts (1539).** In 1539, King François Ier issued *une ordonnance* (an ordinance) at Villers-Cotterêts. It's usually summed up as requiring French instead of Latin in legal and administrative acts. Its wording says these acts must be delivered *en langage maternel françois*. Historians have debated whether that phrase meant French alone, or also the other languages spoken in the kingdom.

<Example fr="« prononcés, enregistrés et délivrés aux parties en langage maternel françois et non autrement » (ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts, article CXI)" en="&quot;pronounced, registered and delivered to the parties in the French mother tongue and not otherwise&quot; (our translation)" />

**L'Académie française (1635).** The Académie française was founded in 1635. Its first dictionary, *le dictionnaire de l'Académie*, appeared in 1694. You know from the lesson on false friends that it still gives advice on usage today.

**Le rapport de l'abbé Grégoire (1794).** During the Revolution, the abbé Grégoire presented a report on the *patois*, the word then used for the regional languages and dialects, and on spreading the use of French. The word *patois* is often dismissive, so a neutral text reports it as the word of the time.

**Les lois Jules Ferry (1881–1882).** The school laws of 1881–1882, named after the minister Jules Ferry, organized primary school across the country. As the lesson on regional languages says, the school of the Republic taught in French, and many children learned it there rather than at home, alongside their *langue maternelle* (mother tongue).

**1992 et 1994.** Since 1992, Article 2 of the Constitution has said that French is the language of the Republic. The Loi Toubon (1994) organizes the use of French in public life. You know both from earlier lessons, and the 1990 spelling rectifications from Unit 1.

<Example fr="Une loi peut imposer l'emploi d'une langue dans les actes administratifs." en="A law can impose the use of a language in administrative acts." />

## Writing about language history neutrally

The history of a language is also the history of the people who spoke other languages. As a rule of thumb, when you write about it:

- **Give the date and its kind**: issued, founded, published, adopted.
- **Attribute an interpretation** to historians, and present a debate as a debate.
- **Report a word of the time** (*patois*) as a word, without taking it over as a verdict.

## The model: an article on the history of French

The article below is original, written for the lesson. It quotes the first words of the Strasbourg Oaths in the original, for recognition only, with our own modern French and English translations.

*Douze siècles de français. Le français n'est pas né d'un jour. Il vient du latin vulgaire, le latin que parlait le peuple de Gaule, qui s'est transformé peu à peu pendant plusieurs siècles. En 842, deux petits-fils de Charlemagne prononcent à Strasbourg des serments que le manuscrit nous a conservés. On les présente souvent comme le plus ancien texte dans une forme ancienne du français. Ils commencent ainsi : « Pro Deo amur et pro christian poblo et nostro commun salvament ».*

*Au Moyen Âge, on parle au nord la langue d'oïl et au sud la langue d'oc, deux groupes nommés d'après leur mot pour dire « oui ». Chacun de ces deux groupes réunit lui-même de nombreux dialectes, qui changent d'une région à l'autre. C'est l'époque de l'ancien français, puis du moyen français, à partir du XIVe siècle. En 1539, l'ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts demande que les actes de justice soient rédigés en français : on la résume souvent ainsi, même si le sens exact de ses mots est discuté par les historiens.*

*Le XVIIe siècle est celui du français classique. L'Académie française est fondée en 1635, et son premier dictionnaire paraît en 1694. Pendant la Révolution, en 1794, l'abbé Grégoire présente un rapport sur les patois, mot qu'on employait alors pour les langues régionales. Un siècle plus tard, les lois Jules Ferry, en 1881 et 1882, organisent l'école primaire dans tout le pays, et c'est en français qu'on y enseigne.*

*Aujourd'hui, la Constitution dit, depuis 1992, que la langue de la République est le français, et la loi Toubon de 1994 en organise l'emploi dans la vie publique. Mais une langue ne se résume pas à ses lois : elle continue de changer chaque jour, dans la bouche de ceux qui la parlent.*

*Les premiers mots des Serments, en français moderne (notre traduction) : « Pour l'amour de Dieu et pour le peuple chrétien et notre salut commun ».*

(Twelve centuries of French. French wasn't born in a day. It comes from Vulgar Latin, the Latin spoken by the people of Gaul, which changed little by little over several centuries. In 842, two grandsons of Charlemagne swear oaths at Strasbourg that the manuscript has preserved for us. They are often presented as the oldest text in an early form of French. They begin: "Pro Deo amur et pro christian poblo et nostro commun salvament". In the Middle Ages, people speak the langue d'oïl in the north and the langue d'oc in the south, two groups named after their word for "yes". Each of these two groups itself brings together many dialects, which change from one region to another. This is the time of Old French, then of Middle French, from the 14th century. In 1539, the ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts requires legal acts to be written in French: that's how it's often summed up, even though historians debate the exact meaning of its words. The 17th century is that of classical French. The Académie française is founded in 1635, and its first dictionary appears in 1694. During the Revolution, in 1794, the abbé Grégoire presents a report on the patois, the word then used for the regional languages. A century later, the Jules Ferry laws, in 1881 and 1882, organize primary school across the country, and it is in French that it teaches. Today, the Constitution has said since 1992 that the language of the Republic is French, and the 1994 Loi Toubon organizes its use in public life. But a language can't be summed up by its laws: it keeps changing every day, in the mouths of those who speak it. / The first words of the Oaths, in modern French (our translation): "For the love of God and for the Christian people and our common salvation".)

## How the article works

- **One narrative tense per paragraph**: the historical present (*prononcent*, *demande*, *est fondée*, *présente*, *organisent*) tells the past events; the present of today (*dit*, *continue*) tells the present.
- **The dates and their kind**: *fondée en 1635*, *paraît en 1694*, *présente un rapport* in 1794; the 1539 ordinance is summed up, and the debate about its words is attributed to historians.
- **The quotation**: the first words of the Oaths, for recognition, with our own modern French translation at the end.
- **The word of the time**: *patois, mot qu'on employait alors*, reported, not adopted.
- **No verdict**: the article doesn't say whether these policies were good or bad for the regional languages.

<Callout type="warning" title="Old French isn't modern French with old spelling">
  Learners sometimes read the Strasbourg Oaths as modern French badly spelt, and try to say them aloud word by word. *Pro Deo amur* isn't a spelling of *pour l'amour de Dieu*: it's an earlier state of the language, with other words and another order. Read it with a translation: **« Pro Deo amur » veut dire « pour l'amour de Dieu »**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Hang the history on five dates">
  To remember the stages, learn five dates and one fact each: 842, the Oaths; 1539, Villers-Cotterêts; 1635, the Académie; 1794, Grégoire's report; 1881–1882, the Ferry laws. Every other fact in this lesson fits before, between or after them.
</Callout>
