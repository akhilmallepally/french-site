## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words describe the work of the whole unit. *Une lecture critique* (a critical reading) looks at what a text does, not only at what it says. To *décrypter* (decode) a text is to bring out what it suggests without saying. *Une grille d'analyse* (an analysis grid) is a fixed list of questions you put to every text. And *un parti pris* is a deliberate stance, or a bias: every opinion piece has one, and that's not a fault.

<Example fr="Une lecture critique ne cherche pas à dire si l'auteur a raison, mais comment il essaie de convaincre." en="A critical reading doesn't try to say whether the writer is right, but how they try to convince." />

<Example fr="Avec une grille d'analyse, on peut décrypter n'importe quelle tribune en quelques minutes." en="With an analysis grid, you can decode any opinion piece in a few minutes." />

<Example fr="Un parti pris n'est pas un défaut dans une tribune : c'est sa raison d'être." en="A deliberate stance isn't a fault in an opinion piece: it's what it's for." />

## One grid for every text

The unit gives you four questions, one per kind of analysis. Put them to the text in this order, because each answer helps the next.

1. **What genre is it?** Look at the signature, the heading and the first lines. A dépêche promises facts, a tribune a position.
2. **What does it imply?** Look for presuppositions (*enfin*, *encore*), connoted words, judging verbs, scare quotes and the conditionnel de prudence.
3. **How do its arguments work?** Name each move: an example, an analogy, an authority, or a fallacy. Then judge its strength.
4. **How does the style serve the argument?** Look at the length of the sentences, the rhythm, the questions, the end.

The grid is a tool for reading, not a plan for writing. When you write your analysis, you may follow it, or group your remarks by idea, as in a commentary.

## A critical reading isn't a verdict

The aim of the analysis is to show how a text works, not to agree or disagree with it. A good analysis of a tribune you dislike shows its strengths; a good analysis of one you like shows its weaknesses. Keep the vocabulary of description (*l'autrice s'appuie sur…*, *cet exemple illustre…*, *cette phrase repose sur une prémisse implicite*) and leave your own view for another exercise.

## From the grid to a written analysis

Your notes follow the grid; your written analysis can follow it too, in four short paragraphs, one per question. Each paragraph starts with a claim, gives one or two short quotations, and says what they do. A few formulas help you stay descriptive.

- **To present the genre**, write *ce texte est une tribune*, *il s'agit d'un billet d'humeur*, or *le texte relève du genre de la chronique*.
- **To point out the implicit**, write *cette formule présuppose que…*, *les guillemets mettent à distance…*, or *le texte laisse entendre que…*.
- **To describe an argument**, write *l'autrice s'appuie sur un exemple*, *ce raisonnement repose sur une prémisse implicite*, or *cet argument prend la forme d'une pente glissante*.
- **To link style and effect**, write *ce rythme ternaire donne de la force à…*, or *la question rhétorique invite le lecteur à…*.

Keep the present tense throughout, as in a commentary, and refer to the writer by name or as *l'autrice* or *l'auteur*. One quotation per claim is usually enough.

<Example fr="Cette formule présuppose que la métropole tardait à rendre son plan public." en="This phrase presupposes that the authority was slow to make its plan public." />

## The model: an opinion piece

The tribune below is original. The city, the bus line, the association and its president, Claire Morin, are invented.

*Tribune. La ligne 12 ne doit pas disparaître. La métropole a enfin dévoilé son plan : supprimer le bus de nuit, la ligne 12, dès janvier. Officiellement, il s'agit de « faire des économies ». En réalité, on demande encore une fois aux mêmes de payer.*

*Qui prend la ligne 12 ? Des infirmières, des cuisiniers, des étudiants qui travaillent tard. Ceux qui ne peuvent pas s'offrir un taxi. Pour eux, ce bus n'est pas un confort : c'est le seul moyen de rentrer.*

*À Saint-Ours, la ville voisine, la suppression d'une ligne semblable aurait fait chuter la fréquentation des commerces du centre. Aujourd'hui, on supprime un bus ; demain, ce seront les trains du dimanche, puis les bibliothèques le soir. Où s'arrêtera-t-on ?*

*La métropole nous dit qu'il faut choisir : soit un budget équilibré, soit des transports pour tous. C'est faux. D'autres villes ont réduit le nombre de leurs bus sans supprimer la nuit. Nous demandons simplement qu'on écoute ceux qui travaillent quand les autres dorment. Un bus. Une ligne. Une nuit de travail qui se termine bien.*

*Claire Morin, présidente de l'association Nuit et Ville.*

(Opinion. Line 12 must not disappear. The metropolitan authority has finally unveiled its plan: to cut the night bus, line 12, from January. Officially, it's about "saving money". In reality, the same people are being asked to pay, once again. Who takes line 12? Nurses, cooks, students who work late. Those who can't afford a taxi. For them, this bus isn't a comfort: it's the only way to get home. In Saint-Ours, the neighbouring town, cutting a similar line reportedly made footfall in the town-centre shops fall. Today, a bus is cut; tomorrow, it will be the Sunday trains, then the libraries in the evening. Where will it stop? The authority tells us we have to choose: either a balanced budget, or transport for everyone. That's wrong. Other cities have reduced the number of their buses without cutting the night service. We simply ask that people listen to those who work while others sleep. One bus. One line. A night's work that ends well. Claire Morin, president of the Nuit et Ville association.)

## The model analysis

*Ce texte est une tribune : il est signé par une personne extérieure au journal, la présidente d'une association, et il défend une position. Son titre, une phrase au présent avec « ne… pas », annonce déjà la thèse.*

*L'implicite y joue un grand rôle. « Enfin dévoilé » présuppose que la métropole tardait à rendre son plan public, et les guillemets autour de « faire des économies » mettent l'argument officiel à distance. Le conditionnel « aurait fait chuter » présente l'exemple de Saint-Ours avec prudence.*

*Les arguments sont de valeur inégale. L'exemple des infirmières et des cuisiniers est concret et touche le lecteur. En revanche, la phrase sur les trains et les bibliothèques est une pente glissante. L'autrice répond par ailleurs à un faux dilemme, celui de la métropole, avec un contre-exemple : d'autres villes ont réduit leurs bus sans supprimer la nuit.*

*Le style, enfin, sert la thèse : la question rhétorique « Où s'arrêtera-t-on ? », puis la fin en trois phrases brèves, au rythme ternaire, laissent le lecteur sur une image simple et forte.*

(This text is a tribune: it's signed by someone outside the paper, the president of an association, and it defends a position. Its title, a sentence in the present with "ne… pas", already announces the thesis. The implicit plays a large part. "Enfin dévoilé" presupposes that the authority was slow to make its plan public, and the quotation marks around "faire des économies" keep the official argument at a distance. The conditional "aurait fait chuter" presents the Saint-Ours example with caution. The arguments are of unequal value. The example of the nurses and cooks is concrete and touches the reader. On the other hand, the sentence about the trains and libraries is a slippery slope. The writer also answers a false dilemma, the authority's, with a counterexample: other cities have reduced their buses without cutting the night service. Finally, the style serves the thesis: the rhetorical question "Où s'arrêtera-t-on ?", then the ending in three short sentences, with a ternary rhythm, leave the reader with a simple, strong image.)

## How the analysis uses the grid

- **The genre** comes first, with its signs: the signature, the position defended, the title.
- **The implicit** is shown with three precise examples, each quoted briefly: a presupposition, scare quotes, the conditionnel de prudence.
- **The arguments** are named and judged: one strong (the example), one weak (the slippery slope), and one fallacy that the writer answers well (the false dilemma).
- **The style** is linked to an effect: the question and the ternary ending leave a strong image.
- **No verdict**: the analysis never says whether the line should be kept.

## Your turn

Write an analysis of 100 to 150 words of the second paragraph of the tribune (*Qui prend la ligne 12 ?…*), with the four questions of the grid. Keep your quotations short and embedded, and describe without judging. The writing task gives you a word count, a checklist and a model to compare with when you've finished.

<Callout type="warning" title="Analyse the text, don't answer it">
  Learners who disagree with a tribune often write *l'autrice a tort, car les bus de nuit coûtent cher*. That's a reply, not an analysis. Describe how the text works: **l'autrice s'appuie sur un argument par l'exemple**, **sa conclusion repose sur une pente glissante**. Your own opinion belongs in a different exercise.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Time your four questions">
  When you practise, give yourself a fixed time for each question of the grid, for example three minutes each, and write your notes in four columns. The limit stops you from spending all your time on the style and forgetting the arguments, and after a few texts, the grid becomes a reflex.
</Callout>
