By the end of this lesson you'll be able to spot French wordplay, explain it accurately (the sound or the set phrase, the two meanings, and the context that joins them), see why it resists translation, and write a headline pun of your own.

## What you already know

You know *un jeu de mots*, *un calembour* (a pun), *une contrepèterie* (a spoonerism) and *intraduisible* (untranslatable) from <LessonLink id="c1-culture-satire-and-humour" text="the lesson on satire and humour" />. You know *le sens propre / le sens figuré* from <LessonLink id="c2-polysemy" text="the lesson on polysemy" />, *avoir du pain sur la planche* from <LessonLink id="b2-idioms-everyday" text="the lesson on everyday idioms" />, and *la titraille* from <LessonLink id="c2-reading-the-press" text="the lesson on reading the press" />.

**Every headline in this lesson is invented**, and none is about a real person or group. The lesson keeps to puns and set phrases. Many well-known *contrepèteries* hide a cheeky second meaning, so this lesson leaves them aside.

## Same sound, different words

<VocabTable group="sound" />

*L'homophonie* (homophony) is the raw material of the French pun. Many French words *se prononcent de la même façon* (are pronounced the same way) but have different meanings, often with different spellings. *Un homophone* is one of these words:

- **Verre, vert, ver, vers**: a glass, green, a worm, towards.
- **Sang, cent, sans**: blood, a hundred, without.
- **Mer, mère, maire**: the sea, a mother, a mayor.

A pun built on homophones works best aloud, or in a headline that the reader "hears" as they read. In writing, the different spellings make the second meaning visible.

<Example fr="« Sang », « cent » et « sans » se prononcent de la même façon." en="Sang, cent and sans are pronounced the same way." />

<Example fr="Pendant la journée du don du sang, chaque donneur reçoit un café." en="During the blood donation day, each donor gets a coffee." />

## Set phrases read literally

<VocabTable group="idioms" />

The second great source of wordplay is the set phrase read in its *sens propre* (literal sense). To *détourner une expression* (twist a set phrase), a writer uses it where its literal meaning also fits. The result is *un double sens* (a double meaning):

- **Avoir du pain sur la planche** (to have a lot of work) becomes a pun about bakers.
- **Se jeter à l'eau** (to take the plunge) becomes a pun about a sea festival.
- **Faire un tabac** (to be a big hit, familiar) becomes a pun about a film against *le tabac* (tobacco).

Both meanings must make sense in the context. If only the figurative one fits, there's no pun; if only the literal one fits, the reader sees a strange sentence.

<Example fr="Ce titre a un double sens : il parle du travail des boulangers et de leur pain." en="This headline has a double meaning: it speaks of the bakers' work and of their bread." />

<Example fr="Un bon titre de presse détourne une expression que tout le monde connaît." en="A good headline twists a set phrase that everyone knows." />

## Wit

<VocabTable group="wit" />

*Un mot d'esprit* (a witty remark) is a short, clever reply or comment, often with a play on words. To *faire un bon mot* is to make one. French comic tradition has long valued it, from conversation to the stage, where performers built whole routines on double meanings. A joke that nobody laughs at *tombe à plat* (falls flat). In a headline or a column, the pun often comes at the end, as *la chute* (the punchline), a word you know from <LessonLink id="c1-reading-literary-narrative" text="the lesson on reading literary narrative" />.

As a rule of thumb, a pun works when the reader finds the second meaning quickly. If it needs a long explanation, it usually falls flat.

<Example fr="Un mot d'esprit fait rire parce qu'il est court et rapide." en="A witty remark makes people laugh because it is short and quick." />

<Example fr="Pendant le dîner, elle a fait un bon mot qui a fait rire toute la table." en="During dinner, she made a witty remark that made the whole table laugh." />

<Example fr="La plaisanterie est tombée à plat : personne n'a compris le double sens." en="The joke fell flat: nobody understood the double meaning." />

## Why wordplay resists translation

A pun lives in one language's sounds and set phrases. *Verre* and *vert* sound alike in French; *glass* and *green* don't in English. So a translator has three options:

- **Explain it** in a note, which keeps the meaning but loses the laugh.
- **Choose one meaning**, which keeps the sentence natural but loses the play.
- **Use *la compensation*** (compensation): create another pun, in the target language, at the same place or elsewhere in the text.

You know from the lesson on version that a translation keeps the meaning before the effect. With wordplay, the effect is part of the meaning, which is why translators often choose compensation.

<Example fr="Le traducteur a choisi la compensation : il a créé un autre jeu de mots en anglais." en="The translator chose compensation: he created another pun in English." />

## How to explain a pun

To explain a pun accurately, give three things:

- **The source**: the sound shared by two words (*verre / vert*), or the set phrase (*faire un tabac*).
- **The two meanings**, both of them: the literal one and the figurative one, or the two homophones.
- **The context** that makes both meanings fit at once: a wine fair, a blood donation day, a film against smoking.

## The model: a column of invented headlines

The column below is original, written for the lesson. Every headline is invented; none is about a real person, place in the news, or group.

*Les titres de la semaine. Chaque lundi, notre rubrique choisit les meilleurs jeux de mots de la presse. Cette semaine, tous les titres sont inventés, mais ils suivent les mêmes règles que les vrais.*

*Premier titre : « Salon du vin : un verre plus vert ». Au salon, les viticulteurs bio étaient nombreux. Le titre joue sur deux homophones : on boit un verre, et le salon devient plus vert, plus écologique. Les deux sens vont ensemble, et c'est ce qui fait rire.*

*Deuxième titre : « Don du sang : cent donneurs, sans attendre ». Trois mots, sang, cent et sans, se prononcent de la même façon. À l'oral, la phrase semble répéter le même son ; à l'écrit, l'orthographe montre les trois sens.*

*Troisième titre : « Fête de la mer : le maire se jette à l'eau ». Ici, deux jeux se croisent. Le maire et la mer sont des homophones, et se jeter à l'eau veut dire prendre un risque. Pendant une fête de la mer, l'expression retrouve aussi son sens propre.*

*Quatrième titre : « Boulangeries : du pain sur la planche pour Noël ». Avoir du pain sur la planche, c'est avoir beaucoup de travail. Pour des boulangers, le pain est bien réel, et l'expression a donc deux sens à la fois.*

*Cinquième titre : « Marché de Noël : les commerçants font leur beurre ». Faire son beurre, en français familier, c'est gagner beaucoup d'argent. Sur un marché où l'on vend du beurre, des gâteaux et du fromage, l'expression retrouve son sens propre.*

*Dernier titre, notre titre de une : « Un film contre la cigarette fait un tabac ». Faire un tabac, c'est avoir un grand succès. Mais le mot tabac rappelle aussi ce que le film combat. C'est la chute parfaite : un succès contre le tabac, qui fait un tabac.*

*Un bon jeu de mots se comprend vite. S'il faut l'expliquer trop longtemps, il tombe à plat : c'est pour cela que notre rubrique ne garde que les plus courts. Rendez-vous lundi prochain.*

(The headlines of the week. Every Monday, our column picks the best puns in the press. This week, all the headlines are invented, but they follow the same rules as real ones. First headline: "Wine fair: a greener glass" (verre and vert sound the same). At the fair, there were many organic wine growers. The headline plays on two homophones: you drink a glass, and the fair becomes greener, more ecological. The two meanings go together, and that's what makes it funny. Second headline: "Blood donation: a hundred donors, without waiting" (sang, cent and sans sound the same). Three words, sang, cent and sans, are pronounced the same way. Aloud, the sentence seems to repeat the same sound; in writing, the spelling shows the three meanings. Third headline: "Sea festival: the mayor takes the plunge" (maire and mer sound the same). Here, two puns cross. Le maire and la mer are homophones, and se jeter à l'eau means to take a risk. During a sea festival, the phrase also gets back its literal sense. Fourth headline: "Bakeries: plenty of work for Christmas" (literally, bread on the board). To have bread on the board is to have a lot of work. For bakers, the bread is very real, so the phrase has two meanings at once. Fifth headline: "Christmas market: the stallholders make a killing" (literally, make their butter). Faire son beurre, in familiar French, means to earn a lot of money. At a market where butter, cakes and cheese are sold, the phrase gets back its literal sense. Last headline, our front-page headline: "A film against cigarettes is a smash hit" (faire un tabac, with tabac also meaning tobacco). Faire un tabac means to be a great success. But the word tabac also recalls what the film is fighting. It's the perfect punchline: a success against tobacco, which is a smash hit. A good pun is understood quickly. If it takes too long to explain, it falls flat: that's why our column keeps only the shortest. See you next Monday.)

## How the column works

- **Each headline** is followed by its explanation: the source (a sound or a set phrase), the two meanings, and the context.
- **Two kinds of pun**: homophones (*verre / vert*; *sang / cent / sans*; *maire / mer*) and set phrases read literally (*se jeter à l'eau*, *du pain sur la planche*, *faire son beurre*, *faire un tabac*). The third headline uses both.
- **The punchline** comes last, in the front-page headline, with a final twist (*un succès contre le tabac, qui fait un tabac*).
- **The translation** in brackets shows the problem of the lesson: the English keeps the meaning, but the puns have to be explained.

<Callout type="warning" title="Give both meanings, not just the figurative one">
  When they explain a pun, learners often give only the meaning of the set phrase: *« du pain sur la planche » veut dire beaucoup de travail*. That explains the phrase, not the pun. Add the literal meaning that the context brings back: **ici, l'expression veut dire beaucoup de travail, et, pour des boulangers, du vrai pain**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read headlines aloud">
  When you read a French paper, read the headlines aloud, slowly. Many puns in the press are built on sound, and the eye, which sees two different spellings, misses what the ear hears at once. If a headline sounds odd aloud, look for the second word hiding in the sound.
</Callout>
