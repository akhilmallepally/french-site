## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These words help you write about a literature over time. *Un siècle littéraire* (a literary century) is a century seen through its writers: *le XVIe siècle littéraire*. *Une filiation* (a line of descent) links a work to the works it comes from. A writer who *se réclame de* (claims to follow) a model says so openly. Between two works, you can look for *la continuité* (continuity) or *une rupture* (a break). And *la clarté* (clarity) is a quality French writers have long claimed for their language.

<Example fr="Beaucoup de romanciers se réclament de Proust, mais tous ne lisent pas son œuvre de la même façon." en="Many novelists claim Proust as a model, but they don't all read his work the same way." />

<Example fr="Le XVIe siècle littéraire est celui de l'humanisme et de la Pléiade." en="The 16th century, in literature, is that of humanism and the Pléiade." />

<Example fr="On peut voir une filiation entre Du Bellay et les auteurs de la créolité." en="One can see a line of descent between Du Bellay and the authors of créolité." />

<Example fr="Le Nouveau Roman se présente comme une rupture avec le roman traditionnel." en="The Nouveau Roman presents itself as a break with the traditional novel." />

## How the unit fits together

The five lessons of the unit look like five subjects, but they tell one story. The history of the language explains why the Chanson de Roland needs a translation and Ronsard only a few notes. The Renaissance poets who wanted to defend French belong to the same history as the Académie française, founded less than a century later. The writers of the twentieth century, who questioned the novel or wrote under constraint, played with a language that the laws of 1992 and 1994 also protect. And the francophone writers have shown that this language no longer belongs to one country.

When you write about any part of the unit, place it on that line: what came before it, what it changed, and what came after.

## Quoting and paraphrasing across the unit

Every quotation in the unit follows the same rules. The Strasbourg Oaths, the ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts, the Chanson de Roland, Ronsard and Rivarol are all on the safe list: their texts are anonymous, or their authors died before 1900. Each quotation is under 15 words, copied from a public edition, in its own spelling, with our own translation. Every author of the twentieth century and of today is paraphrased, never quoted.

## Writing about a filiation

A filiation is a reading: it's what you see when you put two works side by side. It's a fact only when the writers themselves claim it. As a rule of thumb:

- **Say who sees the link**: the writer (*il se réclame de…*), critics (*on rapproche souvent…*), or you (*on peut voir une filiation*).
- **Say what continues and what changes**: *ce qui continue, c'est…; ce qui change, c'est…*
- **Keep the dates and the attributions** from the lessons: each movement in its own writers' terms, labels as "often linked with".
- **Quote only safe-list authors**, under 15 words, from a public edition, and paraphrase everyone else.

## The model: an essay on French, changing and lasting

The essay below is original, written for the lesson. It quotes one author on the safe list, Rivarol, from a public edition of his *De l'universalité de la langue française*, with our own translation.

*Une langue qui change et qui dure. Entre les Serments de Strasbourg, en 842, et les romans de la dernière rentrée littéraire, le français a tant changé qu'un lecteur d'aujourd'hui ne comprend plus ses premiers textes sans traduction. Les mots ont changé, la prononciation aussi, et même l'ordre des mots dans la phrase. Pourtant, quelque chose dure : un fil qui va d'un siècle littéraire à l'autre.*

*Ce fil, c'est d'abord une volonté de défendre la langue. En 1539, l'ordonnance de Villers-Cotterêts demande le français dans les actes de justice. En 1549, Du Bellay veut montrer que le français peut porter une grande poésie. En 1635, l'Académie française est fondée. Plus tard, Rivarol écrit cette phrase célèbre : « ce qui n'est pas clair n'est pas français ». On peut discuter cette idée : la clarté dépend de celui qui écrit, et une phrase obscure reste possible dans toutes les langues.*

*Ce fil, c'est aussi le jeu. Rabelais aimait déjà les jeux de mots, et l'Oulipo, fondé en 1960, en a fait une méthode : Perec écrit en 1969 un roman entier sans la lettre e. La presse d'aujourd'hui, avec ses titres à double sens, appartient à la même famille. Le jeu de mots n'est pas un luxe : il montre qu'une langue est bien vivante.*

*Enfin, la langue n'appartient plus à un seul pays. Des écrivains de la négritude, dans les années 1930, aux auteurs de l'Éloge de la créolité, en 1989, beaucoup ont écrit en français tout en revendiquant d'autres racines. Est-ce une rupture ou une continuité ? Sans doute les deux : chacun fait du français une langue à lui, comme Du Bellay voulait en faire une langue de grande poésie.*

*La phrase de Rivarol, en français moderne : elle n'a pas besoin de traduction. En anglais (notre traduction) : « what is not clear is not French ».*

(A language that changes and lasts. Between the Strasbourg Oaths, in 842, and the novels of the latest literary season, French has changed so much that a reader today can no longer understand its first texts without a translation. The words have changed, so has the pronunciation, and even the order of words in the sentence. And yet something lasts: a thread that runs from one literary century to the next. This thread is, first, a wish to defend the language. In 1539, the ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts requires French in legal acts. In 1549, Du Bellay wants to show that French can carry great poetry. In 1635, the Académie française is founded. Later, Rivarol writes this famous sentence: "what is not clear is not French". This idea can be discussed: clarity depends on the person who writes, and an obscure sentence remains possible in every language. This thread is also play. Rabelais already loved puns, and the Oulipo, founded in 1960, turned play into a method: in 1969, Perec writes a whole novel without the letter e. Today's press, with its double-meaning headlines, belongs to the same family. Wordplay isn't a luxury: it shows that a language is truly alive. Finally, the language no longer belongs to a single country. From the writers of Négritude, in the 1930s, to the authors of the Éloge de la créolité, in 1989, many have written in French while claiming other roots. Is it a break or a continuity? Probably both: each one makes French a language of their own, as Du Bellay wanted to make it a language of great poetry. / Rivarol's sentence, in modern French: it needs no translation. In English (our translation): "what is not clear is not French".)

## How the essay works

- **One thesis**: French changes and lasts, announced in the first paragraph, with *un fil* (a thread) as its image.
- **Three threads**, one per paragraph: defending the language, play, and a language that belongs to many places.
- **The dates** come from the lessons of the unit, with no new one. Rivarol's sentence is placed with *plus tard*, without a date.
- **The quotation**: one sentence by Rivarol (an author on the safe list), under 15 words, copied from a public edition; the essay discusses it instead of taking it as a fact.
- **The filiations** are readings, presented with *on peut*, *sans doute* and a question; no writer is made to say what they didn't say.

## Your turn

The writing task asks you for one paragraph on a filiation between two works or movements of the unit. Follow the model of the essay: give the dates, say what continues and what changes, attribute each idea to its authors, and say clearly that the filiation is your reading. The writing-task model links Du Bellay and the authors of the *Éloge de la créolité*, and says that neither presents the link that way.

<Callout type="warning" title="A filiation you see isn't one the writers claimed">
  When they write about literary history, learners often turn their own comparison into a fact: *les auteurs de la créolité continuent Du Bellay*. Unless the writers say so, the link is yours. Present it as a reading: **on peut voir une filiation entre Du Bellay et les auteurs de la créolité**.
</Callout>

</Callout>
