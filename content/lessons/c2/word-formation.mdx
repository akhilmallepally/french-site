By the end of this lesson you'll be able to take a French word apart into its stem, prefix and suffix, guess the meaning of a word you've never seen, and avoid forms that look regular but don't exist.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-nominalisation" text="the lesson on nominalisation" />, you turned verbs into nouns with suffixes such as *-tion* and *-ment* (*l'arrivée*, *la fermeture*). In <LessonLink id="c1-abstract-nouns" text="the lesson on abstract nouns" />, you learned nouns such as *le changement* and *l'efficacité*. This lesson gives you the whole system, so that you can read new words and build the ones that exist.

## The parts of a word

<VocabTable group="terms" />

Most French words that aren't very short are built from parts. *Un radical* (a stem) carries the main meaning. *Un préfixe* (a prefix) comes before it and changes its meaning; *un suffixe* (a suffix) comes after it and usually changes its class, from verb to noun or from adjective to verb. Building a word this way is *la dérivation* (derivation).

<Example fr="Dans le mot « illisible », le radical est lis-, le préfixe il- et le suffixe -ible." en="In the word 'illisible', the stem is lis-, the prefix il- and the suffix -ible." />

The stem isn't always the infinitive. *Lisible* comes from *lis-* (*nous lisons*), and *faisable* from *fais-* (*nous faisons*). When you build a word, start from the *nous* form of the present if the infinitive doesn't work.

## Prefixes

<VocabTable group="prefixes" />

A prefix keeps the class of the word: a verb stays a verb, an adjective stays an adjective. These are the ones you'll meet most.

- **The prefixes dé- and dés- reverse an action**, as in *faire* / *défaire*, *obéir* / *désobéir*.
- **The prefix in- means "not"**, and it changes before some letters: *il-* before *l* (*illégal*), *ir-* before *r* (*irréaliste*), *im-* before *b*, *m* or *p* (*impossible*).
- **The prefix re- (usually ré- before a vowel) means "again"**: *refaire*, *réécrire*.
- **The prefix pré- means "before"**: *prévoir*, *la préhistoire*.
- **The prefixes sur- and sous- mean "over" and "under"**: *surestimer*, *sous-estimer*.
- **The prefixes contre-, anti- and auto- mean "against", "against" and "self"**: *un contre-argument*, *un antivirus*, *un autoportrait*.

<Example fr="Le ministre a sous-estimé le coût du projet." en="The minister underestimated the cost of the project." />

<Example fr="Cet élève a désobéi au règlement de l'école." en="This pupil disobeyed the school rules." />

## Suffixes

<VocabTable group="suffixes" />

A suffix usually changes the class of the word. The main ones work like this.

- **For nouns from verbs**, the suffixes are *-age* (*nettoyer*, *le nettoyage*), *-ment* (*changer*, *le changement*) and *-tion* (*créer*, *la création*).
- **For nouns from adjectives**, the suffix is often *-ité* (*simple*, *la simplicité*; *rapide*, *la rapidité*).
- **For movements and the people in them**, the suffixes are *-isme* and *-iste* (*le journalisme*, *un journaliste*).
- **For "that can be done"**, the suffixes are *-able* and *-ible* (*lavable*, *faisable*, *lisible*).
- **For verbs from adjectives or nouns**, the suffixes are *-iser* and *-ifier* (*moderne*, *moderniser*; *simple*, *simplifier*).

<Example fr="Le nettoyage des rues commence à six heures." en="Street cleaning starts at six." />

<Example fr="La mairie veut simplifier les démarches en ligne." en="The town hall wants to simplify the online procedures." />

**The suffix isn't predictable.** *Nettoyer* gives *le nettoyage*, but *changer* gives *le changement*, *arriver* gives *l'arrivée*, and *créer* gives *la création*. A word that looks regular may not exist: *la nettoyation* and *l'arrivement* aren't French. Before you use a word you've built, check it in a dictionary.

## Learned roots

<VocabTable group="roots" />

Some words are built from Greek or Latin roots that French shares with English. Four of them are worth knowing, because they appear in new words too.

- **The root -logie means "the study of"**: *la sociologie*, *la biologie*.
- **The root -cratie means "power"**: *la démocratie*, *la bureaucratie*.
- **The root -phobe means "who fears"**: *claustrophobe* (afraid of closed spaces).
- **The root -cide means "that kills"**: *un insecticide*.

<Example fr="La sociologie étudie la vie des groupes et des sociétés." en="Sociology studies the life of groups and societies." />

<Example fr="Cet insecticide est interdit dans les jardins publics." en="This insecticide is banned in public gardens." />

<Example fr="Mon frère est claustrophobe : il ne prend jamais l'ascenseur." en="My brother is claustrophobic: he never takes the lift." />

## Compounds, mots-valises and new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

Besides derivation, French makes new words by joining two words, as you saw with *un porte-monnaie* and *un gratte-ciel*. A more playful kind is *un mot-valise* (a blend), which joins the start of one word to the end of another: *le franglais* (*français* + *anglais*), *un courriel* (*courrier* + *électronique*).

Any new word is *un néologisme* (a neologism). The press creates many of them, mostly with the tools you've just seen: a prefix (*déconnecter*), a suffix (*la numérisation*), or a mot-valise. Some last, and some disappear after a few months. As a rule of thumb, read new words freely, but use them in your own formal writing only when a dictionary has them.

<Example fr="La numérisation des vieux journaux de la ville a pris trois ans." en="The digitization of the town's old newspapers took three years." />

## The model: a column with three invented words

The column below is original. Its three new words are **invented for this lesson**: you won't find them in a dictionary, and you shouldn't use them as real words.

*Notre langue manque de mots pour décrire la vie de bureau. Je propose donc trois néologismes. Le premier, la réunionocratie, veut dire une entreprise où les réunions ont pris le pouvoir : on n'y décide plus rien sans en organiser une autre.*

*Le deuxième, un notificophobe, est un employé qui a peur de ses notifications et qui coupe son téléphone dès neuf heures. Le troisième est un verbe : déréunionner, c'est annuler une réunion qui ne sert à rien, et c'est le verbe le plus agréable de la semaine.*

(Our language lacks words to describe office life. So I'm proposing three neologisms. The first, la réunionocratie, means a company where meetings have taken power: nothing is decided there any more without organizing another one. The second, un notificophobe, is an employee who is afraid of their notifications and switches off their phone from nine o'clock. The third is a verb: déréunionner is to cancel a meeting that serves no purpose, and it's the most pleasant verb of the week.)

## How the column builds its words

- **The invented word réunionocratie** is *réunion* + the root *-cratie* (power), like *démocratie*.
- **The invented word notificophobe** is *notification* + the root *-phobe* (who fears), like *claustrophobe*.
- **The invented verb déréunionner** is the prefix *dé-* (reversing an action) + *réunion* + the verb ending *-er*, like *défaire*.

Each one is clear because each part keeps its usual meaning. That's what makes a neologism work, and what lets you read one you've never seen.

## The whole list

- A word = a stem, with or without a prefix and a suffix.
- Prefixes keep the class: *dé-*, *in-* (*il-*, *ir-*, *im-*), *re-*, *pré-*, *sur-*, *sous-*, *contre-*, *anti-*, *auto-*.
- Suffixes change the class: *-age*, *-ment*, *-tion* (nouns from verbs), *-ité* (nouns from adjectives), *-isme*, *-iste*, *-able*, *-ible*, *-iser*, *-ifier*.
- The suffix isn't predictable: check every word you build.
- Learned roots: *-logie*, *-cratie*, *-phobe*, *-cide*.
- Compounds and mots-valises (*le franglais*, *un courriel*); neologisms in the press.

## En français

Nora is translating a report and asks her colleague Théo about some words. They use *tu*.

- *Théo, j'ai trouvé « numérisation » dans le rapport. C'est un vrai mot ?* (Théo, I found "numérisation" in the report. Is it a real word?)
- *Oui : numérique, plus -iser, plus -ation. C'est le fait de tout passer en numérique.* (Yes: numérique, plus -iser, plus -ation. It's putting everything into digital form.)
- *Et « irréaliste », avec deux r ?* (And "irréaliste", with two r's?)
- *Oui. In- devient ir- devant un r : in- et réaliste donnent irréaliste.* (Yes. In- becomes ir- before an r: in- and réaliste give irréaliste.)
- *J'ai voulu écrire « le changeage des horaires ». Mon logiciel le souligne en rouge.* (I wanted to write "le changeage des horaires". My software underlines it in red.)
- *Il a raison : on dit le changement. Le suffixe -age ne marche pas avec tous les verbes.* (It's right: we say le changement. The suffix -age doesn't work with every verb.)
- *Comment je peux savoir quel suffixe choisir ?* (How can I know which suffix to choose?)
- *Tu ne peux pas le deviner : il faut vérifier dans un dictionnaire.* (You can't guess it: you have to check in a dictionary.)
- *Et ce mot, « réunionite », c'est sérieux ?* (And this word, "réunionite", is it serious?)
- *C'est familier et ironique : une maladie des réunions trop nombreuses.* (It's familiar and ironic: an illness of too many meetings.)
- *Alors je ne le mets pas dans mon texte.* (Then I won't put it in my text.)
- *Mets plutôt « un trop grand nombre de réunions ». Le rapport est formel.* (Put "un trop grand nombre de réunions" instead. The report is formal.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the suffix before you use the word">
  Learners who know the pattern often build words that don't exist: *la nettoyation*, *l'arrivement*, *le changeage*. The suffix depends on the word, and you can't predict it. Check, then write: **le nettoyage**, **l'arrivée**, **le changement**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read long words from the middle">
  When you meet a long unknown word, find the stem first, in the middle, then read the prefix and the suffix around it. *Inutilisable* is *utilis-* (use), *in-* (not) and *-able* (that can be): that can't be used. With practice, most long words in a formal text stop being unknown words.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un mot est souvent formé d'un radical, d'un préfixe et d'un suffixe. La dérivation est la formation d'un mot avec un préfixe ou un suffixe. Le préfixe ne change pas la classe du mot : dé- inverse une action, in- veut dire non et devient il-, ir- ou im- devant certaines lettres, re- veut dire de nouveau, sur- et sous- veulent dire trop et pas assez. Le suffixe change souvent la classe : nettoyer donne le nettoyage, changer donne le changement, simple donne la simplicité et simplifier, et moderne donne moderniser. Les adjectifs en -able et en -ible, comme lavable ou lisible, veulent dire qu'on peut faire l'action. On ne peut pas deviner le suffixe : il faut vérifier chaque mot dans un dictionnaire. Les racines savantes, comme -logie, -cratie, -phobe et -cide aident à comprendre des mots nouveaux. Un mot-valise, comme le franglais, réunit deux mots. Enfin, un néologisme est un mot nouveau, et la presse en crée souvent.
