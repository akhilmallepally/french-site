By the end of this lesson you'll be able to name common jobs for men and women, say where you work, and ask someone what they do.

## Jobs for men and women

Most French job titles have a masculine and a feminine form. A few patterns cover many of them. You already know *étudiant / étudiante* from <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="the lesson on être" />, which just adds *-e*, and *le médecin* from <LessonLink id="a2-at-the-doctor" text="the lesson on the doctor and pharmacy" />.

**Often -eur becomes -euse.**

<VocabTable group="eur" />

**Often -teur becomes -trice.**

<VocabTable group="teur" />

**-ier becomes -ière, and -er becomes -ère.** The grave accent appears in the feminine.

<VocabTable group="ier" />

**Some words stay the same**, and only the article changes.

<VocabTable group="same" />

These patterns are a guide, so learn each job as a pair.

## Words that are changing

The feminine forms of a few jobs are still settling, and you'll see more than one version.

<VocabTable group="varies" />

Today, *professeure*, *ingénieure* and *écrivaine* are widely used, but you'll also meet the older *une professeur*, *une ingénieur* and *une écrivain*. *Médecin* usually stays the same for women: *une médecin*, and you'll also hear *un médecin* used of a woman. People's own preferences vary, so follow what you hear.

## No article after être

When you say what job someone does with *être*, French doesn't use a word for "a".

<Example fr="Je suis infirmière." en="I'm a nurse. (said by a woman)" />
<Example fr="Il est boulanger." en="He's a baker." />
<Example fr="Elle est journaliste." en="She's a journalist." />

The article comes back as soon as you add an adjective. That's covered in <LessonLink id="a2-cest-vs-il-est" text="the next lesson" />.

## Where you work

Use *à* with an article for most workplaces, as in <LessonLink id="a1-contractions-a-de" text="the lesson on à and de with articles" />. You know *le restaurant* and *l'école* already, as well as *le bureau* from <LessonLink id="a1-plural-nouns" text="the lesson on plural nouns" /> and *l'hôpital* from <LessonLink id="a1-places-in-town" text="the lesson on places in town" />.

## Saying it all at once

A useful answer often has two parts: what you do, and where you do it. Start with *je suis* and a job, with no article, and then add *je travaille* and a place. For example: *Je suis serveur. Je travaille au restaurant* (I'm a waiter. I work at the restaurant). If you're looking for work, you can say so simply: *Je cherche du travail* (I'm looking for work).

## En français

- *Bonjour. Quel est votre métier ?* (Hello. What's your job?)
- *Je suis dentiste. Et vous ?* (I'm a dentist. And you?)
- *Je suis vendeuse. Je travaille au supermarché.* (I'm a shop assistant. I work at the supermarket.)
- *Et votre mari ?* (And your husband?)
- *Il cherche du travail.* (He's looking for work.)

<Callout type="warning" title="No 'a' after je suis">
  English says "I am **a** nurse", so it's natural to write *Je suis une infirmière*. With *être* and a plain job title, French drops the article: **Je suis infirmière**, **Il est serveur**. This also applies to *étudiant*: **Je suis étudiant**, as you've seen before.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The job is inside the verb">
  Many job titles contain a verb you know. *Un vendeur* sells (*vendre*), *un serveur* serves, *un cuisinier* cooks (*cuisiner*), and *un boulanger* works in *la boulangerie*. When you meet a new job title, look for the verb or the place inside it. You'll often be able to guess what the person does.
</Callout>
