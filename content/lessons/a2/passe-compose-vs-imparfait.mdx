By the end of this lesson you'll be able to choose between the two past tenses, and combine them in a single sentence.

## Two jobs

You know how to form both tenses, from <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the lesson on the passé composé" /> and <LessonLink id="a2-imparfait-formation" text="the lesson on the imparfait" />. Now you need to know which to use.

| Imparfait | Passé composé |
|---|---|
| background and descriptions | single events |
| habits and repeated actions | actions with a clear start and end |
| actions still going on at that moment | actions that are finished |

Think of it like this: the **imparfait** tells you how things were, and the **passé composé** tells you what happened next.

<Example fr="Il neigeait et les rues étaient vides." en="It was snowing and the streets were empty." />
<Example fr="Le train est parti à huit heures." en="The train left at eight o'clock." />
<Example fr="Tous les étés, nous visitions un musée." en="Every summer, we visited a museum." />
<Example fr="Cet été-là, nous avons visité Rome." en="That summer, we visited Rome." />

## Signal words

Some words point towards one tense or the other. Words of repetition go with the imparfait: *tous les jours* (every day), from <LessonLink id="a1-review-everyday-life" text="the review of everyday life" />; *souvent* (often), from <LessonLink id="a1-review-free-time" text="the review of free time" />; *d'habitude* (usually), from <LessonLink id="a2-imparfait-formation" text="the lesson on the imparfait" />; and *le samedi* (on Saturdays), from <LessonLink id="a1-days-months-dates" text="the lesson on days, months and dates" />.

*Toujours* (always) isn't a reliable signal on its own: *J'ai toujours habité ici* (I've always lived here) is common French.

Words that pick out one moment go with the passé composé. *Hier* (yesterday) is from <LessonLink id="a2-time-expressions-past" text="the lesson on time expressions for the past" />, and irregular participles such as *pris* are from <LessonLink id="a2-past-participles-irregular" text="the lesson on irregular past participles" />.

<VocabTable group="passe-compose" />

These words are a strong guide, but they aren't the whole story. The meaning of the sentence comes first.

<Example fr="D'habitude, je rentrais à pied." en="I usually walked home." />
<Example fr="Une fois, j'ai pris un taxi." en="Once, I took a taxi." />

## When one interrupts the other

Often, both tenses appear in one sentence. An action is going on, in the imparfait, when something happens, in the passé composé. Learn this as a pattern with *quand*:

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="Je lisais quand Paul est arrivé." en="I was reading when Paul arrived." />
<Example fr="Elles mangeaient quand Léa a crié." en="They were eating when Léa shouted." />

The imparfait is the longer background, and the passé composé is the sudden event. Use *quand* only in this simple pattern for now. <LessonLink id="b1-time-connectors" text="The lesson on time connectors" /> looks at this kind of word in more detail.

## A model story

Here's a short story with the verbs in bold.

| French | English | Tense |
|---|---|---|
| Il **faisait** beau. | The weather was nice. | imparfait: background |
| Les enfants **jouaient** au parc. | The children were playing in the park. | imparfait: in progress |
| Soudain, un chien **est arrivé**. | Suddenly, a dog arrived. | passé composé: event |
| Marie **a crié**. | Marie shouted. | passé composé: event |
| Les enfants **sont partis**. | The children left. | passé composé: event |

## En français

- *Tu faisais quoi quand j'ai téléphoné ?* (What were you doing when I phoned?)
- *Je dormais !* (I was sleeping!)
- *D'habitude, tu ne dors pas à midi.* (You don't usually sleep at noon.)
- *Non, mais ce matin-là, j'étais très fatigué.* (No, but that morning, I was very tired.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't choose by the English">
  English "I played" can be either tense. *Tous les jours, je jouais au foot* (Every day, I played football) is a habit, so it's the imparfait. *Hier, j'ai joué au foot* (Yesterday, I played football) is one event, so it's the passé composé. The English verb is the same in both. Ask what's happening in the sentence, not how it translates.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Think like a film camera">
  Imagine filming your story. The opening shot shows the scene: the weather, the people, what they were doing. That's the imparfait. Then the action starts: someone arrives, something falls, somebody shouts. Each of those moves the story forward, and that's the passé composé. If nothing has moved on, you're still in the opening shot.
</Callout>
