By the end of this lesson you'll be able to count into the thousands and millions, say years and dates of birth, read larger prices, and use words like "first" and "third".

## Hundreds, thousands, millions

You can already count to a hundred, thanks to <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-21-100" text="the lesson on numbers 21 to 100" />. The bigger numbers build on that.

<VocabTable group="big" />

Note that "a hundred" and "a thousand" need no *un* in French: *cent euros*, *mille euros*. Numbers are written with a space or a point between thousands, never a comma: *2 000* or *2.000*.

Three spelling rules cover nearly everything.

**Cent takes -s only when it's multiplied and ends the number.** So *deux cents* and *trois cents* take -s, but *deux cent trois* doesn't, because another number follows. *Cent* on its own has no -s.

**Vingt follows the same rule in eighty.** You know this one already: *quatre-vingts*, but *quatre-vingt-trois*.

**Mille never changes.** It's *deux mille*, *dix mille*. *Million*, on the other hand, is a noun. It takes -s in the plural and needs **de** before another noun: *un million d'euros* (a million euros), *deux millions de personnes* (two million people).

<Example fr="Ce vélo coûte cent cinquante euros." en="This bike costs a hundred and fifty euros." />
<Example fr="Le billet coûte deux cents euros." en="The ticket costs two hundred euros." />
<Example fr="Il y a trois mille personnes." en="There are three thousand people." />
<Example fr="Paris a deux millions d'habitants." en="Paris has two million inhabitants." />

## Years

French says years as full numbers. Use **en** for "in".

<VocabTable group="years" />

For 2000 onward, there's only one way: *deux mille dix-neuf*. For the 1900s, you have two, and both are common: *mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-cinq* or *dix-neuf cent quatre-vingt-cinq*. The *cent* rule applies as usual, so 1900 itself is *mille neuf cents*.

<Example fr="Elle est née en mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-dix." en="She was born in 1990." />
<Example fr="Je suis né le 3 mai 2001." en="I was born on 3 May 2001." />

For dates of birth, you say the day and month as you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-days-months-dates" text="the lesson on days, months and dates" />, then add the year. *Je suis né* and *Je suis née* are fixed phrases for now, and you'll see how they work in <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the lesson on the passé composé with être" />.

## Ordinals: first, second, third

To make an ordinal, add **-ième** to the number. If the number ends in *e*, drop it first: *quatre* becomes *quatrième*, and *onze* becomes *onzième*.

<VocabTable group="ordinals" />

Keep an eye on these:

- **First** is special: *premier* for a masculine noun, *première* for a feminine one. It has its own word, not a form of *un*.
- **Cinq** adds a u: *cinquième*.
- **Neuf** changes its f to v: *neuvième*.
- **In larger numbers**, 21st is *vingt et unième*, not *vingt et premier*. The same goes for 31st, 41st and so on.

There's a second word for "second": *second* or *seconde*. It exists and you'll see it, but **deuxième** is always a safe choice.

Ordinals go before the noun: *la deuxième rue*, *le troisième jour*. In writing, they're often shortened: *1er*, *1re*, *2e*, *3e*.

<Example fr="C'est la troisième fois." en="It's the third time." />
<Example fr="Elle habite au cinquième étage." en="She lives on the fifth floor." />

Floors use ordinals too, with one word for the level at the street.

## Putting it together

- *Tu es née quand ?* (When were you born?)
- *Je suis née le 12 juin 1998. Et toi ?* (I was born on 12 June 1998. And you?)
- *Moi, en deux mille un.* (Me, in 2001.)
- *Tu habites à quel étage ?* (What floor do you live on?)
- *Au deuxième.* (On the second.)

Remember the prices you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-shopping-for-clothes" text="the lesson on clothes, colours and prices" />: they work the same way above a hundred. *349 €* is *trois cent quarante-neuf euros*.

<Callout type="warning" title="The -s on cents disappears">
  It's easy to learn *deux cents* and then write *deux cents trois*. The -s only survives when *cent* is the last thing in the number. Check what comes after it: nothing, keep the -s (**cinq cents**); another number, drop it (**cinq cent vingt**). *Mille* never gets an -s at all, so *deux milles* is wrong.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The first floor is upstairs">
  If you're from North America, French floors will catch you out. The level you walk in on is **le rez-de-chaussée**. **Le premier étage** is one floor up, what Americans call the second floor. When someone tells you *au deuxième*, climb two flights.
</Callout>
