By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use eleven common irregular verbs in the present. They look like a long list, but most of them fall into small groups that behave alike.

You already know the regular families from <LessonLink id="a1-ir-re-verbs-present" text="the lesson on -ir and -re verbs" />, and three irregular verbs that take an infinitive from <LessonLink id="a1-modal-verbs" text="the lesson on pouvoir, vouloir and devoir" />. The verbs below follow patterns of their own. In each table, *il* stands for *il*, *elle* and *on*, and *ils* for *ils* and *elles*. Each table gives all six forms.

## Group 1: prendre and mettre

These two end in *-re*, and their singular forms look like a regular *-re* verb: *-s*, *-s*, and nothing for *il*. The plurals are where they differ.

<VocabTable group="prendre-mettre" />

*Prendre* loses its d in the plural and has a double n in the *ils* form. *Mettre* has one t in the singular and two in the plural.

*Prendre* is used for transport and for food and drink. That's the verb inside the phrase you learned as a chunk: *Je vais prendre un café* (I'll have a coffee). *Mettre* means "to put", and also "to put on" clothes.

<Example fr="Je prends le métro le matin." en="I take the metro in the morning." />
<Example fr="Qu'est-ce que vous prenez ?" en="What will you have?" />
<Example fr="Elle met du lait." en="She's adding some milk." />

Two common verbs are built on *prendre* and work exactly like it: *apprendre* (to learn) and *comprendre* (to understand). *Je comprends* (I understand), *nous comprenons* (we understand), *ils comprennent* (they understand).

## Group 2: partir, sortir and dormir

These end in *-ir*, but they don't have the *-iss-* of *finir*. In the singular, they lose the consonant before the ending. In the plural, they keep it, and you can hear it in the *ils* form.

<VocabTable group="partir" />

<Example fr="Le bus part à midi." en="The bus leaves at noon." />
<Example fr="Tu sors ce soir ?" en="Are you going out tonight?" />
<Example fr="Je dors mal." en="I sleep badly." />

## Group 3: dire, lire and écrire

These three have the same singular endings: *-s*, *-s*, *-t*, all silent. In the plural, each one adds a consonant to its stem: an s sound for *dire* and *lire*, and a v for *écrire*.

<VocabTable group="dire" />

Watch out for **vous dites**. It's one of only three common verbs whose *vous* form doesn't end in *-ez*. You already know the other two: *vous êtes* and *vous faites*.

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="Il dit bonjour à la voisine." en="He says hello to the neighbour." />
<Example fr="Nous lisons le journal." en="We read the newspaper." />
<Example fr="J'écris une lettre à Sophie." en="I'm writing a letter to Sophie." />

## Group 4: venir, voir and boire

These three don't share a pattern, but each has one thing to remember.

<VocabTable group="others" />

- **Venir** changes *e* to *ie* except with *nous* and *vous*. *Ils viennent* has a double n. *Revenir* (to come back) and *devenir* (to become) work the same way.
- **Voir** has a y in *nous voyons* and *vous voyez*. The other four forms all sound the same.
- **Boire** has a completely different stem with *nous* and *vous*: *buv-*. The *ils* form goes its own way too: *boivent*.

<Example fr="Tu viens au cinéma ?" en="Are you coming to the cinema?" />
<Example fr="On voit Marc samedi." en="We're seeing Marc on Saturday." />
<Example fr="Mes parents boivent du jus d'orange." en="My parents drink orange juice." />

## En français

- *Salut ! Tu sors ce soir ?* (Hi! Are you going out tonight?)
- *Non, je ne sors pas. Je lis, et je dors tôt.* (No, I'm not going out. I'm reading, and I'm getting an early night.)
- *Tu viens samedi ? Nous prenons le train pour Lille.* (Are you coming on Saturday? We're taking the train to Lille.)
- *Il part à quelle heure ?* (What time does it leave?)
- *À neuf heures. Tu mets un pull, il fait froid !* (At nine. Put on a jumper, it's cold!)
- *D'accord, je viens.* (OK, I'll come.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Partir isn't finir">
  Both verbs end in *-ir*, so it's tempting to use the *-iss-* pattern: *nous partissons*, *ils dormissent*. Those forms don't exist. It's **nous partons**, **ils dorment**, and **elles sortent**. The *-iss-* pattern belongs to verbs like *finir* and *choisir*, so check which family a verb is in before you write its plural.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Three forms unlock a verb">
  You don't have to memorize six separate forms. For a new irregular verb, learn just *je*, *nous* and *ils*. The *tu* form almost always sounds like *je*, *il* usually sounds like them too, and *vous* normally follows *nous*. With those three, you can work out nearly all the rest. The exception to watch for is **vous dites**, with no *-ez*: *être*, *faire* and *dire* are the only common verbs without it, and only *dire* is in this lesson.
</Callout>
