By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say what you did at the weekend, ask someone what happened, and say what you didn't do, using the most common past tense in French.

## Two parts

The **passé composé** is the everyday tense for finished actions. As its name suggests, it's "composed" of two parts: an auxiliary and a past participle.

- The **auxiliary** is usually *avoir* in the present. You know it from <LessonLink id="a1-avoir-present" text="the lesson on avoir" />.
- The **past participle** is a form of the main verb.

One form of French covers several in English. *J'ai parlé* can mean "I spoke", "I have spoken" or "I did speak".

<VocabTable group="forms" />

## Making the participle

For regular verbs, the participle comes from the infinitive, and each family from <LessonLink id="a1-er-verbs-present" text="the lesson on regular -er verbs" /> onward has its own ending.

<VocabTable group="participles" />

The participle doesn't change here, whoever did the action (more on this later: one exception arrives in <LessonLink id="a2-pronouns-in-passe-compose" text="the lesson on pronouns in the passé composé" />): *j'ai parlé*, *elle a parlé*, *ils ont parlé*.

<Example fr="J'ai joué au tennis samedi." en="I played tennis on Saturday." />
<Example fr="Elle a choisi un film." en="She chose a film." />
<Example fr="Nous avons attendu Sophie." en="We waited for Sophie." />
<Example fr="Tu as dormi ?" en="Did you sleep?" />

A few new verbs will come in handy for your practice.

<VocabTable group="words" />

Not all verbs in <LessonLink id="a2-irregular-verbs-present" text="the lesson on common irregular verbs" /> have participles like these. Most of them are irregular, and you'll learn them in the next lesson. Others, including *partir* and *sortir*, use *être* instead of *avoir*, and you'll see those soon too. *Dormir* is the easy one, with its regular *dormi*.

## Saying what you didn't do

*Ne…pas* wraps around **avoir**, the part that's conjugated. The participle comes after *pas*. Because all forms of *avoir* start with a vowel, *ne* always becomes *n'*.

<Example fr="Je n'ai pas travaillé dimanche." en="I didn't work on Sunday." />
<Example fr="Ils n'ont pas regardé le match." en="They didn't watch the match." />
<Example fr="Tu n'as pas oublié les clés ?" en="You didn't forget the keys?" />

*Un*, *une*, *des* and *du* still change to *de* after a negative verb, as they do in the present: *J'ai acheté une voiture* (I bought a car) becomes *Je n'ai pas acheté de voiture* (I didn't buy a car).

## Asking about the past

The question styles you already know all work here.

- *Tu as mangé ?* (Did you eat?)
- *Est-ce que tu as mangé ?* (Did you eat?)
- *As-tu mangé ?* (Did you eat?)

With inversion, the pronoun goes right after *avoir*, and the participle stays at the end. The first two are the ones you'll use most.

## En français

- *Salut, Léa ! Tu as travaillé samedi ?* (Hi, Léa! Did you work on Saturday?)
- *Non, j'ai joué au tennis, et j'ai regardé un film.* (No, I played tennis and watched a film.)
- *Et dimanche ?* (And Sunday?)
- *J'ai dormi ! Je n'ai pas travaillé, je n'ai pas cuisiné.* (I slept! I didn't work and I didn't cook.)
- *Tu as mangé au restaurant ?* (Did you eat at a restaurant?)
- *Oui, et j'ai acheté un gâteau.* (Yes, and I bought a cake.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Pas comes before the participle">
  English puts "not" right before the main verb, and French learners often place *pas* at the end: *Je n'ai mangé pas*. In the passé composé, the negative wraps around *avoir* only: **Je n'ai pas mangé**. And don't leave the auxiliary out. *Je mangé* isn't French, even though "I ate" is only two words in English.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The vendre test">
  *Manger* and *mangé* sound exactly alike, so it's hard to know which to write. Replace the verb in your head with *vendre*. If *vendre* sounds right, you need the infinitive: *Je vais vendre*, so *Je vais manger*. If *vendu* sounds right, you need the participle: *J'ai vendu*, so *J'ai mangé*.
</Callout>
