By the end of this lesson you'll be able to make an appointment, tell a doctor what's wrong, and understand what you're told to do at the surgery and the pharmacy.

## Making an appointment

In France, you'll usually ring or book online to see a doctor. A few phrases get you through it. As always, start with *bonjour*, and use *vous*.

<VocabTable group="booking" />

To agree on a time, you can use *À quelle heure ?* from <LessonLink id="a1-question-words" text="the lesson on question words" />.

<Example fr="Bonjour, je voudrais prendre rendez-vous avec le docteur Martin." en="Hello, I'd like to make an appointment with Dr Martin." />
<Example fr="Mardi à dix heures, c'est possible ?" en="Is Tuesday at ten possible?" />

*Le médecin traitant* and *la carte Vitale* are good words to recognize if you live in France. <LessonLink id="a2-body-and-health" text="The lesson on the body and health" /> gave you what you need to explain the problem itself.

## What the doctor asks

These are the questions you're most likely to hear.

<VocabTable group="questions" />

*Depuis quand ?* asks how long something has been happening. Learn it as a fixed phrase for now. It has <LessonLink id="a2-depuis-pendant-il-y-a" text="its own lesson" /> later on. You can answer with a day: *Depuis lundi* (Since Monday).

<Example fr="J'ai mal au ventre et j'ai de la fièvre." en="My stomach hurts and I have a temperature." />
<Example fr="Où avez-vous mal ? — Ici, au genou." en="Where does it hurt? — Here, in my knee." />

## Treatment

After the examination, you'll probably hear some of these.

<VocabTable group="treatment" />

Instructions use the *vous* command that you know from <LessonLink id="a1-imperative" text="the lesson on the imperative" />. *Prenez* is from *prendre*, and *Buvez* is from *boire*.

<Example fr="Prenez deux comprimés le soir." en="Take two tablets in the evening." />
<Example fr="Buvez de l'eau et ne travaillez pas lundi." en="Drink some water and don't work on Monday." />

The doctor will often give you **une ordonnance**, which you take to the pharmacy. *Il faut se reposer* is a useful fixed phrase for now, and you'll see the structure in the next lessons. *Ce n'est pas grave* is the reassuring one you're hoping to hear.

## At the pharmacy

At the pharmacy, the same politeness applies. Hand over your prescription, or explain the problem, and listen for how often to take something.

- *Bonjour, j'ai une ordonnance.* (Hello, I have a prescription.)
- *Voici le médicament. Prenez un comprimé deux fois par jour.* (Here's the medicine. Take one tablet twice a day.)
- *Le matin et le soir ?* (Morning and evening?)
- *Oui, c'est ça.* (Yes, that's right.)

## En français

- *Bonjour, docteur.* (Hello, doctor.)
- *Bonjour. Qu'est-ce que vous avez ?* (Hello. What's wrong?)
- *J'ai mal à la gorge, et je tousse.* (My throat hurts and I'm coughing.)
- *Depuis quand ?* (Since when?)
- *Depuis lundi.* (Since Monday.)
- *Vous avez de la fièvre ?* (Do you have a temperature?)
- *Non.* (No.)
- *Ce n'est pas grave. C'est un rhume. Il faut se reposer.* (It's not serious. It's a cold. You need to rest.)

<Callout type="warning" title="A médicament, not a médecine">
  English "medicine" can mean something you swallow, but *la médecine* in French is the subject you study to become a doctor. The tablets you take are **un médicament**. And don't mix up **le médecin** (the doctor) with **le médicament** (the medicine), which look alike and sit close together in any conversation about health.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Try the pharmacist first">
  For small problems such as a cough or a sore throat, you can often go straight to *la pharmacie*. French pharmacists are used to giving advice, and you don't need an appointment. Explain what's wrong, using *J'ai mal à…* and they'll tell you if you really need to see a doctor.
</Callout>
