By the end of this lesson you'll be able to choose between *c'est* and *il est* or *elle est*. It's a choice that English doesn't make, since "he's a doctor" and "he's tall" both start the same way.

## Look at what comes next

The choice depends almost entirely on the word that follows. You know both forms from <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="the lesson on être" />, and the jobs come from <LessonLink id="a2-jobs-and-workplace" text="the lesson on jobs and the workplace" />.

## C'est before a noun with a small word

Use **c'est** when you have a noun with a determiner in front of it: *un*, *une*, *le*, *la*, or *mon*, *ma*, as in <LessonLink id="a1-possessive-adjectives" text="the lesson on mon, ma and mes" />. That's true even if there's an adjective too. Use it before a name as well.

<Example fr="C'est un médecin." en="He's a doctor." />
<Example fr="C'est ma sœur." en="She's my sister." />
<Example fr="C'est une bonne dentiste." en="She's a good dentist." />
<Example fr="C'est Karim." en="It's Karim." />

## Il est and elle est

Use **il est** or **elle est** in two cases, when you're talking about a person.

Before a **job with no article**:

<Example fr="Il est médecin." en="He's a doctor." />
<Example fr="Elle est vendeuse." en="She's a shop assistant." />

And before an **adjective** that describes the person, which agrees as you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-adjective-agreement" text="the lesson on adjective agreement" />:

<Example fr="Elle est grande et sportive." en="She's tall and sporty." />
<Example fr="Il est timide." en="He's shy." />

Notice that *Il est médecin* and *C'est un médecin* mean the same thing. What changes is the structure.

## C'est with an adjective

To comment on a thing or a situation, rather than a person, use **c'est** with an adjective. The adjective stays **masculine singular**, whatever you're talking about.

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="C'est difficile." en="It's difficult." />
<Example fr="C'est important !" en="It's important!" />
<Example fr="C'est facile." en="It's easy." />

## The plural

In the plural, *c'est* becomes **ce sont**, which you met in <LessonLink id="a1-indefinite-articles" text="the lesson on un, une and des" />, and *il est* becomes **ils sont** or **elles sont**.

<Example fr="Ce sont des boulangers." en="They're bakers." />
<Example fr="Ce sont mes parents." en="They're my parents." />
<Example fr="Elles sont actrices." en="They're actresses." />

## Summary

| What comes next | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| article or *mon / ma* + noun | *c'est* / *ce sont* | *C'est un serveur.* (He's a waiter.) |
| a name | *c'est* | *C'est Léa.* (It's Léa.) |
| job with no article | *il est* / *elle est* | *Il est serveur.* (He's a waiter.) |
| adjective, about a person | *il est* / *elle est* | *Elle est drôle.* (She's funny.) |
| adjective, about a thing or situation | *c'est* | *C'est super.* (It's great.) |

## En français

- *C'est qui ?* (Who is it?)
- *C'est Madame Roche. Elle est professeure.* (It's Mrs Roche. She's a teacher.)
- *Elle est sympa ?* (Is she nice?)
- *Oui, c'est une très bonne professeure.* (Yes, she's a very good teacher.)
- *Et le travail ?* (And the work?)
- *C'est difficile, mais c'est super !* (It's hard, but it's great!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Elle est une bonne infirmière is wrong">
  English "she is a good nurse" pulls you towards *Elle est une bonne infirmière*. As soon as there's an article, you need *c'est*: **C'est une bonne infirmière**. Without the article and the adjective, use **Elle est infirmière**. In the same way, *Il est un médecin* and *C'est médecin* are both wrong. It's **Il est médecin** or **C'est un médecin**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Look for the little word">
  When you're unsure, cover the first two words and look at what's left. Does it start with a small word like *un*, *une* or *mon*? Then you need *c'est*. Does it start straight away with a job or an adjective about a person? Then you need *il est* or *elle est*. One glance at the next word is enough.
</Callout>
