By the end of this lesson you'll understand how French schooling is organized, you'll be able to follow a conversation about school, and you won't be caught out by the way classes are named.

## The stages

French children go through a series of stages. Exact ages vary a little, so treat these as a guide.

<VocabTable group="stages" />

- **L'école maternelle** is for the youngest children.
- **L'école élémentaire** is primary school, and it's where pupils learn to read and write.
- **Le collège** takes children from about eleven to fifteen.
- **Le lycée** takes teenagers from about fifteen to about eighteen.
- **Le bac** comes at the end of *le lycée*. It's the exam that opens the way to higher education, and it's a big moment in French family life.

## Classes that count down

The class names use ordinals, from <LessonLink id="a2-numbers-years-ordinals" text="the lesson on numbers over 100, ordinals and years" />, but in a surprising direction. **They go down**, not up.

<VocabTable group="classes" />

So a child starts *le collège* in *sixième*, then goes on to *cinquième*, *quatrième* and *troisième*. *Le lycée* continues with *seconde*, *première* and *terminale*. It's the opposite of the way most English-speaking countries count, where the number rises each year.

<Example fr="Ma fille est en cinquième." en="My daughter is in the second year of middle school." />
<Example fr="Tu es en quelle classe ? — En seconde." en="What year are you in? — The first year of lycée." />

Notice that French says *en* with no article.

## The school week and marks

The school week isn't the same everywhere: it varies by town and by school. In many places, younger children have no lessons on Wednesdays, or only part of the day, but that isn't fixed. Lunch is an important break, and many pupils eat at **la cantine**.

Marks are usually given out of 20, so a mark of 10 is around the halfway point. A very high mark is rare, and it means a great deal.

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="J'ai une note de quinze sur vingt." en="I have a mark of fifteen out of twenty." />
<Example fr="Mon frère a redoublé sa seconde." en="My brother repeated the first year of lycée." />
<Example fr="Il y a un cours de maths à dix heures." en="There's a maths lesson at ten o'clock." />

## Memories of school

You can use what you learned in <LessonLink id="a2-childhood-memories" text="the lesson on childhood memories" /> to talk about your own school days.

- *Quand j'étais au lycée, j'avais des devoirs tous les soirs.* (When I was at secondary school, I had homework every evening.)
- *À l'école, je mangeais à la cantine.* (At school, I ate in the canteen.)
- *Ma prof de français était très gentille.* (My French teacher was very kind.)

## En français

- *Tu es en quelle classe ?* (What year are you in?)
- *En troisième. Et toi ?* (In the last year of collège. And you?)
- *En terminale. Je passe le bac cette année !* (In the final year. I'm taking the bac this year!)
- *Tu as des devoirs ?* (Do you have homework?)
- *Oui, tous les soirs.* (Yes, every evening.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Collège isn't college">
  *Le collège* is for children of about eleven to fifteen, not for adults in higher education. If a French teenager says *Je suis au collège*, they mean middle school. For higher education, French uses *l'université* or other words. The *lycée* is also school, not university.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Think of a countdown to the bac">
  The class numbers make sense if you picture a rocket launch. Six, five, four, three: the years of *collège* are counting down. Then come *seconde*, *première*, and finally *terminale*, the last stage before lift-off with the *bac*. Once you see it as a countdown, you won't forget the order.
</Callout>
