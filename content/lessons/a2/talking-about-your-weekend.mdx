By the end of this lesson you'll be able to ask someone about their weekend, tell them about yours, and keep the conversation going with a question in return.

## The Monday question

On a Monday morning, colleagues and friends in France ask the same thing as they do everywhere: how was your weekend? This lesson brings together <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the passé composé with être" /> and <LessonLink id="a2-time-expressions-past" text="the time expressions for the past" />, and gives you the phrases that make it sound like a real chat.

<VocabTable group="asking" />

*Qu'est-ce que tu as fait ?* uses the participle *fait*, from <LessonLink id="a2-past-participles-irregular" text="the lesson on irregular past participles" />. With someone you call *vous*, it's *Vous avez passé un bon week-end ?*

## Answering

A real answer has two parts: a quick reaction, then a detail or two.

<VocabTable group="answering" />

*C'était* means "it was". It belongs to a tense you'll learn later, so for now, keep it as a fixed phrase.

<Example fr="Oui, super ! Samedi, j'ai joué au foot avec des amis." en="Yes, great! On Saturday, I played football with some friends." />
<Example fr="Bof, rien de spécial. J'ai fait les courses." en="Meh, nothing special. I did the shopping." />
<Example fr="C'était bien. Dimanche, je suis allée au marché." en="It was good. On Sunday, I went to the market. (said by a woman)" />
<Example fr="Je suis restée à la maison et j'ai lu." en="I stayed at home and read. (said by a woman)" />

Note the mix: *j'ai joué* with *avoir*, *je suis allée* with *être*. Think about each verb on its own, because a single sentence can have both.

## Using on

In everyday conversation, French speakers say **on** much more often than *nous*. When *on* means "we" and the verb takes *être*, the participle usually agrees as it would with *nous*: **on est allés** for a group of men or a mixed group, and **on est allées** for a group of women.

<Example fr="On est partis samedi matin." en="We left on Saturday morning." />

With *avoir*, nothing changes: *On a mangé au restaurant* (We ate at a restaurant).

## Avoir or être with passer

*Passer* is the verb you'll hear in the Monday question, and it can go either way.

<VocabTable group="passer" />

With a direct object, such as a length of time or a weekend, it takes **avoir**: *J'ai passé un bon week-end* (I had a good weekend).

When it means "to drop by", there's no direct object, and it takes **être**. *Chez* (at someone's home) is a fixed part of this phrase for now.

<Example fr="Je suis passé chez Léa." en="I dropped by Léa's." />

## En français

- *Salut, Karim ! Tu as passé un bon week-end ?* (Hi, Karim! Did you have a good weekend?)
- *Oui, super ! On est allés à Annecy, et on a fait du vélo. Et toi ?* (Yes, great! We went to Annecy and we cycled. And you?)
- *Rien de spécial. J'ai fait la grasse matinée samedi, et je suis restée à la maison.* (Nothing special. I had a lie-in on Saturday, and I stayed at home.)
- *Tu n'es pas sortie ?* (You didn't go out?)
- *Si ! Dimanche, je suis passée chez Léa.* (Yes I did! On Sunday, I dropped by Léa's.)

Notice the *si*: it answers a negative question, as you learned in <LessonLink id="a1-yes-no-questions" text="the lesson on yes/no questions" />. Notice too how the second speaker asks *Et toi ?* A good answer always hands the conversation back.

<Callout type="warning" title="You visit places, not people">
  English says "I visited my friend", but *visiter* in French is only for places: *J'ai visité Lyon*. For a person, say **Je suis allé voir Léa** (I went to see Léa), using *aller voir*. *J'ai visité Léa* sounds as if you toured Léa like a museum.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Have your Monday answer ready">
  The same question comes round every week, so prepare for it. On Sunday evening, pick two things you did and say them to yourself in French: one with *avoir*, and one with *être*. On Monday, you won't be searching for the words, and you can pay attention to what the other person says.
</Callout>
