By the end of this lesson you'll be able to describe your day in the past with reflexive verbs, say how you hurt yourself, and talk about what people did together.

## Always être

You know reflexive verbs in the present from <LessonLink id="a1-daily-routine-reflexive" text="the lesson on daily routines" />. In the passé composé, **every reflexive verb takes être**. You don't need the picture of a house from <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-etre" text="the lesson on the passé composé with être" /> for these: if the verb has *se*, it takes *être*.

The reflexive pronoun goes right before the auxiliary.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

Before the vowel of *es* and *est*, *te* and *se* shorten to *t'* and *s'*.

<Example fr="Je me suis réveillé à six heures et quart." en="I woke up at quarter past six." />
<Example fr="Tu t'es levé à quelle heure ?" en="What time did you get up?" />
<Example fr="Nous nous sommes dépêchés." en="We hurried." />
<Example fr="Elles se sont reposées." en="They rested." />

With *on* meaning "we", the participle usually agrees as it would with *nous*, as in <LessonLink id="a2-talking-about-your-weekend" text="the lesson on talking about your weekend" />: *on s'est levés* (we got up).

## The negative

*Ne* goes before the pronoun, and *pas* goes after the auxiliary. So the negative wraps around both the pronoun and the auxiliary.

<Example fr="Je ne me suis pas lavé." en="I didn't wash. (said by a man)" />
<Example fr="Elle ne s'est pas couchée." en="She didn't go to bed." />

## Agreement

As a rule of thumb, the reflexive pronoun is the direct object, so the participle agrees with the subject. Add -e, -s or -es as you would with the verbs you've already seen.

<Example fr="Léa s'est habillée." en="Léa got dressed." />
<Example fr="Paul et Marc se sont lavés." en="Paul and Marc had a wash." />

**There's one exception to learn now.** When a direct object follows the verb, the participle doesn't agree. That's the case with parts of the body, such as *le bras*, *la main* and *le genou* from <LessonLink id="a2-body-and-health" text="the lesson on the body and health" />, in sentences like these.

<VocabTable group="injury" />

<Example fr="Elle s'est cassé le bras." en="She broke her arm." />
<Example fr="Il s'est lavé les mains." en="He washed his hands." />
<Example fr="Elle s'est brossé les dents." en="She brushed her teeth." />

Compare that with a sentence where what follows isn't a direct object.

<Example fr="Elle s'est blessée au genou." en="She hurt her knee." />

*Au genou* has *à* in it, so it isn't a direct object, and *blessée* agrees with *elle*. There's more on this later, in <LessonLink id="a2-pronouns-in-passe-compose" text="the lesson on pronouns in the passé composé" />.

## Each other

Some reflexive verbs mean that two or more people do something to each other. These are **reciprocal** verbs. In the present, they work like other reflexive verbs.

<VocabTable group="reciprocal" />

- *Nous nous voyons samedi.* (We're seeing each other on Saturday.)
- *Ils se téléphonent le soir.* (They phone each other in the evening.)

In the passé composé, *se voir* and *se rencontrer* agree with the subject, using the participles *vu* and *rencontré*. *Se téléphoner* doesn't agree, because in *téléphoner à quelqu'un*, the person comes after *à*.

<Example fr="Ils se sont vus lundi." en="They saw each other on Monday." />
<Example fr="Elles se sont rencontrées à Lille." en="They met in Lille." />
<Example fr="Ils se sont téléphoné." en="They phoned each other." />

## En français

- *Tu t'es levée à quelle heure ?* (What time did you get up?)
- *À huit heures, et je me suis dépêchée.* (At eight, and I hurried.)
- *Et ton frère ?* (And your brother?)
- *Il s'est cassé le bras dimanche !* (He broke his arm on Sunday!)
- *Non ! Il s'est reposé ?* (No! Did he rest?)
- *Oui. Nous nous sommes téléphoné hier.* (Yes. We phoned each other yesterday.)

<Callout type="warning" title="No agreement before a body part">
  After practising agreement, it's tempting to write *Elle s'est cassée le bras*. When a direct object such as *le bras*, *les mains* or *les dents* comes after the verb, the participle stays as it is: **Elle s'est cassé le bras**. Without that direct object, it agrees as usual: **Elle s'est lavée**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask 'what?' after the verb">
  To decide whether to agree, read as far as the participle and ask "what?" *Elle s'est cassé…* What? *Le bras.* There's a direct answer straight after the verb, so there's no agreement. *Elle s'est blessée…* Where? *Au genou.* That doesn't answer "what?", so the participle agrees. It's a quick test that fits the sentences in this lesson.
</Callout>
