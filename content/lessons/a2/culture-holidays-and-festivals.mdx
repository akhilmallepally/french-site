By the end of this lesson you'll know the main celebrations of the French year, and what to say at each one.

## Winter

**Noël** is on 25 December, but the big meal is often the evening before, on 24 December. This is *le réveillon*, and it's usually *un repas de famille*. Many homes have *un sapin*, and people give each other presents. You know *un cadeau* from <LessonLink id="a2-indirect-object-pronouns" text="the lesson on indirect object pronouns" />. The meal often ends with *la bûche*, a cake shaped like a log.

**Le Nouvel An** starts with another *réveillon*, on 31 December, known as *le réveillon de la Saint-Sylvestre*. Then, in January, friends and colleagues wish each other a good year.

**La galette des rois** appears in early January. It's a flat cake with a small charm, *la fève*, hidden inside. Whoever finds it wears a paper crown, *la couronne*.

**La Chandeleur** is on 2 February, and people make *des crêpes* at home.

<VocabTable group="winter" />

<Example fr="Le 24 décembre, on mange en famille." en="On 24 December, people eat with their family." />
<Example fr="On coupe la galette et on cherche la fève." en="We cut the cake and we look for the charm." />

## Spring and summer

**Pâques** is in spring, and its date changes each year. Children often look for chocolate eggs in the garden.

**Le 1er mai** is a public holiday, *un jour férié*. Many people offer each other a small bunch of lily of the valley.

**La Fête de la musique** is on 21 June. Musicians play in the streets and in bars all over France, and concerts are often free.

**Le 14 juillet** is *la fête nationale*. There's a military parade in Paris, and many towns have *des feux d'artifice* in the evening.

<VocabTable group="spring-summer" />

<Example fr="Le 14 juillet, on regarde le défilé à la télévision." en="On 14 July, people watch the parade on television." />
<Example fr="Le 21 juin, on écoute de la musique dans la rue." en="On 21 June, people listen to music in the street." />

Notice how often *on* appears when talking about customs. It's the natural way to say what people do in general: *on mange*, *on offre*, *on fête*.

## Festivals around France

Many towns have a famous festival of their own. Use *à* with a city, as you learned in <LessonLink id="a2-prepositions-with-places" text="the lesson on prepositions" />.

- **Le carnaval de Nice** takes place in winter, *à Nice*, with floats and flowers.
- **La Fête des Lumières** lights up the streets *à Lyon* in December.
- **Le Festival d'Avignon**, in summer, is about theatre, *à Avignon*.
- **Le Festival de Cannes**, in spring, is about cinema, *à Cannes*.

## What to say

<VocabTable group="greetings" />

You'll write these in cards and in messages, and you'll say them to people you meet. *Bonnes fêtes* covers the whole period at the end of December, so it's a good choice if you aren't sure what someone celebrates.

## A memory

You can use the passé composé, and the imparfait from <LessonLink id="a2-imparfait-formation" text="the lesson on the imparfait" /> to talk about celebrations you remember.

- *Quand j'étais enfant, on fêtait Noël chez ma grand-mère.* (When I was a child, we celebrated Christmas at my grandmother's.)
- *Une année, il a neigé le 24 décembre.* (One year, it snowed on 24 December.)

## En français

- *Tu fais quoi pour le réveillon ?* (What are you doing for New Year's Eve?)
- *On va chez des amis. Et toi ?* (We're going to some friends' place. And you?)
- *Je reste en famille. Alors, bonnes fêtes !* (I'm staying with my family. So, happy holidays!)
- *Merci, à toi aussi !* (Thanks, you too!)

## Your turn

Write four sentences about a celebration in your country, on paper or in a notes app. Use this checklist:

1. the name of the celebration, with *en* or *le* and a date or month
2. one thing *on mange*
3. one thing *on fait*
4. the greeting you would use

<Callout type="warning" title="Bonne fête isn't Bonnes fêtes">
  The singular, **Bonne fête !**, is what you say to someone on their saint's day, or on a special day like Mother's Day. The plural, **Bonnes fêtes !**, is for the end-of-year holidays. One letter separates them, so be careful when you write a card.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="January is one long greeting">
  In France, you can go on wishing people *Bonne année* for most of January, the first time you see them each year. Don't worry if it's already the fifteenth. A colleague or a neighbour will be happy to hear it, and you'll get one back.
</Callout>
