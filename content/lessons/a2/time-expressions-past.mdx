By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say when something happened, using words such as "yesterday", "last week" and "two days ago", and say that you've already done something or haven't done it yet.

## Yesterday and before

You can already form the past with <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-avoir" text="the lesson on the passé composé with avoir" />. Time words tell your listener when it happened.

<VocabTable group="days" />

These words usually go at the very beginning or the very end of a sentence.

<Example fr="Hier, j'ai regardé un match." en="Yesterday I watched a match." />
<Example fr="Nous avons travaillé hier soir." en="We worked last night." />
<Example fr="Avant-hier, Léa a acheté un vélo." en="The day before yesterday, Léa bought a bike." />

## Last week, last year

"Last" is **dernier**, and it's an adjective that matches its noun. With these time phrases, it goes **after** the noun.

<VocabTable group="last" />

<Example fr="Le mois dernier, j'ai vendu ma voiture." en="Last month I sold my car." />
<Example fr="Tu as oublié les clés lundi dernier !" en="You forgot the keys last Monday!" />

For years, use **en** with the numbers from <LessonLink id="a2-numbers-years-ordinals" text="the lesson on numbers over 100, ordinals and years" />.

<Example fr="En deux mille dix-huit, nous avons habité à Dijon." en="In 2018, we lived in Dijon." />

## Ago

To say "ago", French puts **il y a** in front of the length of time. For now, treat it as a chunk that you can learn whole. <LessonLink id="a2-depuis-pendant-il-y-a" text="The lesson on depuis, pendant and il y a" /> looks at how it compares with similar words.

<VocabTable group="ago" />

<Example fr="Il y a trois jours, j'ai perdu mon téléphone." en="Three days ago, I lost my phone." />

Don't confuse this with the *il y a* you know that means "there is". The context, and the length of time after it, tell you which one you're looking at.

## Already and not yet

Two short adverbs behave differently from the words above. They go in the **middle** of the passé composé, not at the start or the end.

<VocabTable group="adverbs" />

**Déjà** goes between the auxiliary and the participle.

<Example fr="J'ai déjà fini le travail." en="I've already finished the work." />
<Example fr="Tu as déjà mangé du poulet aux champignons ?" en="Have you ever eaten chicken with mushrooms?" />

As the second example shows, in a question *déjà* can also mean "ever".

**Pas encore** means "not yet". The *ne* goes in front of *avoir*, as usual, and *pas encore* takes the place of *pas*.

<Example fr="Je n'ai pas encore mangé." en="I haven't eaten yet." />
<Example fr="Elle n'a pas encore choisi." en="She hasn't chosen yet." />

## Using them together

A time word and an adverb can share a sentence. The time word stays at the edge, and the adverb stays in the middle: *Hier soir, j'ai déjà fini le travail* (Last night, I already finished the work).

## En français

- *Tu as déjà fini le livre ?* (Have you already finished the book?)
- *Non, je n'ai pas encore fini. Et toi ?* (No, I haven't finished yet. And you?)
- *Moi, j'ai fini hier soir.* (Me, I finished last night.)
- *Et le film ?* (And the film?)
- *J'ai regardé le film la semaine dernière.* (I watched the film last week.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Déjà doesn't go at the end">
  English can say "I've eaten already", so it's tempting to write *J'ai mangé déjà*. In French, *déjà* goes between the two parts of the verb: **J'ai déjà mangé**. The same is true of *pas encore*, which sits right after *avoir*: **Je n'ai pas encore mangé**. *Hier*, on the other hand, can't go in the middle: it's **Hier, j'ai mangé**, or **J'ai mangé hier**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Put the time word first">
  When you're telling a story, try starting each sentence with the time word: *Hier, … Le mois dernier, … Il y a deux jours, …* It sets the scene for your listener. It also reminds you to use the past, because once you've said *hier*, it's much harder to carry on in the present by mistake.
</Callout>
