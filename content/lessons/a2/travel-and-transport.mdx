By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say how you're travelling, buy a ticket, and find your way around a station or an airport.

## Getting around

You already know *le train*, *le bus*, *la voiture*, *le métro* and *la gare* from <LessonLink id="a1-places-in-town" text="the lesson on places in town" /> and earlier lessons, as well as *le billet* (the ticket). Here are some more.

<VocabTable group="vehicles" />

## How you're travelling

To say how you're getting somewhere, use **en** or **à**, with no article.

- **En** for something enclosed, that you sit inside: *en voiture*, *en train*, *en avion*, *en bus*, *en métro*.
- **À** for something you ride on, or on foot: *à vélo*, *à moto*, *à pied*.

<Example fr="Je vais à Marseille en avion." en="I'm going to Marseille by plane." />
<Example fr="Elle va à l'école à pied." en="She walks to school." />
<Example fr="Nous partons à Nice en voiture." en="We're leaving for Nice by car." />

You can also use **prendre** with the article, from <LessonLink id="a2-review-work" text="the review of work and studies" /> and the lessons before it: *prendre le train*, *prendre l'avion*, *prendre le bus*.

<Example fr="Je prends le métro à huit heures." en="I'm taking the metro at eight o'clock." />

## When plans change

Delays and cancellations are part of travelling, so these are worth having ready. *Le train a du retard* tells you it's late, and you can learn it as a whole phrase. *Le vol est annulé* means it's cancelled. If you're changing trains, *la correspondance* is the word to listen for: you may hear it in an announcement, or see it on a ticket. When something goes wrong, you can go to the desk and use a simple question, such as *Il y a un train pour Lyon ce soir ?* (Is there a train to Lyon this evening?).

## At the ticket office

- *Bonjour. Un aller-retour pour Lyon, s'il vous plaît.* (Hello. A return to Lyon, please.)
- *Vous partez quand ?* (When are you leaving?)
- *Samedi matin. Le train part à quelle heure ?* (On Saturday morning. What time does the train leave?)
- *À neuf heures quinze. Ça fait quarante euros.* (At 9:15. That's forty euros.)
- *Merci. C'est quel quai ?* (Thank you. Which platform is it?)
- *Quai trois.* (Platform three.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Not 'sur le train'">
  English says "on the train" and "on the bus", but French doesn't use *sur* for that. Say **en train** to say how you travel. Note, too, that *en train* (by train) has nothing to do with *être en train de*, from <LessonLink id="a2-venir-de-en-train-de" text="the lesson on the recent past and actions in progress" />.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Wait for the platform">
  At a big French station, the platform is often only shown on the departures board a short time before the train leaves. Don't be surprised if your train has no *quai* listed when you arrive. Find the board, keep your bags close, and be ready to move quickly once the number appears.
</Callout>
