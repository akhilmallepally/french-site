By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say how you feel, ask someone what's wrong, and react when a friend gives you news.

## Saying how you feel

Most of the time you'll use *être* with an adjective, and the adjective matches the person, as in <LessonLink id="a1-adjective-agreement" text="the lesson on adjective agreement" />. You already know *content* and *heureux* from that lesson, and *fatigué* from <LessonLink id="a1-etre-present" text="the lesson on être" />. Here are some more.

<VocabTable group="feelings" />

Look at how the feminine forms change, because some of them show up in the sound as well.

- **No change.** *Triste* already ends in -e, so it's the same for a man and a woman.
- **Just -e.** *Fâchée*, *déçue*, *énervée* and *stressée* look different but sound the same as their masculine forms.
- **A new sound at the end.** You can hear the last consonant of *inquiète*, but not of *inquiet*. The same goes for *jalouse* and *surprise*.
- **A new accent.** *Inquiet* becomes *inquiète*, and *fier* becomes *fière*. The accent is grave, and *fier* and *fière* sound the same.
- **Never any change.** *En colère*, *de bonne humeur* and *de mauvaise humeur* stay exactly as they are, whoever you're talking about.

<Example fr="Karim est très stressé au travail." en="Karim is very stressed at work." />
<Example fr="Ma sœur est déçue." en="My sister is disappointed." />
<Example fr="Nous sommes de bonne humeur ce matin." en="We're in a good mood this morning." />

## How much?

Add *très*, *trop* or *un peu* before the adjective to say how strong a feeling is. They're from <LessonLink id="a2-adverbs-ment" text="the lesson on adverbs" />, and they never change.

<Example fr="Elle est très fière de son fils." en="She's very proud of her son." />
<Example fr="Je suis un peu inquiet." en="I'm a bit worried." />

## Being afraid

Fear uses *avoir*, not *être*. You met *avoir peur* in <LessonLink id="a1-imperative" text="the lesson on the imperative" />. To say what you're afraid of, add *de* and a noun, and remember that *de* joins up with *le* and *les*.

<Example fr="J'ai peur des chiens." en="I'm afraid of dogs." />
<Example fr="Mon fils a peur du noir." en="My son is afraid of the dark." />

## Asking how someone feels

If a friend looks a bit off, you have several ways of asking about it.

<VocabTable group="asking" />

*Qu'est-ce que tu as ?* is for someone you call *tu*, and it's about that person. *Qu'est-ce qu'il y a ?* is more general: it can mean "What's wrong?" or "What's happening?". You can also ask directly: *Tu es de mauvaise humeur ?* (Are you in a bad mood?).

Talking about yesterday's mood? Use *j'étais*, the form you know from <LessonLink id="a2-imparfait-formation" text="the lesson on the imparfait" />: *Hier, j'étais très fatigué* (Yesterday I was very tired, a man speaking).

## En français

- *Ça ne va pas ? Qu'est-ce que tu as ?* (Is something wrong? What's the matter?)
- *Je suis stressée. J'ai un examen demain.* (I'm stressed. I have an exam tomorrow.)
- *Tu as peur ?* (Are you scared?)
- *Oui, un peu. Mais je suis contente de te voir.* (Yes, a bit. But I'm happy to see you.)
- *Moi aussi ! Ça me fait plaisir.* (Me too! I'm so pleased.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Watch inquiète and fière">
  Two adjectives gain a grave accent in the feminine, so don't just add -e. It's **inquiète**, not *inquiete*, and **fière**, not *fiere*. And don't try to make *en colère* agree with anybody: **Marie est en colère**, **Paul et Marie sont en colère**. It's a phrase, not an adjective with endings.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Give a better answer than ça va">
  Next time you practise and someone asks *Ça va ?*, try not to stop at *Ça va bien*. Choose one exact feeling and one small detail: *Je suis un peu stressé, j'ai trop de travail.* It only takes a few words more, and it gives the other person something to reply to.
</Callout>
