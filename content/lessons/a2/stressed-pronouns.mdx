By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use the pronouns that can stand on their own: after a word like *avec*, as a one-word answer, and for emphasis.

## A second set of pronouns

You know the subject pronouns from <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the lesson on subject pronouns" />. They always stay with a verb. **Stressed pronouns** do the jobs that subject pronouns can't.

| Subject | Stressed |
|---|---|
| je | **moi** |
| tu | **toi** |
| il | **lui** |
| elle | elle |
| nous | nous |
| vous | vous |
| ils | **eux** |
| elles | elles |

Four of them look exactly like the subject pronouns. Only the ones in bold are new.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

*Eux* is for a group of men or a mixed group. *Elles* is for a group of women. *Lui* here means "him". You'll see a different use of *lui* in <LessonLink id="a2-indirect-object-pronouns" text="the lesson on indirect object pronouns" />.

## After a preposition

After a small word like *avec* (with), *pour* (for), *sans* (without) or *chez* (at the home of), you need a stressed pronoun.

<VocabTable group="words" />

<Example fr="Tu viens avec moi ?" en="Are you coming with me?" />
<Example fr="C'est pour toi." en="It's for you." />
<Example fr="Je pars sans eux." en="I'm leaving without them." />
<Example fr="Léa travaille avec elles." en="Léa works with them." />

For this lesson, use *chez* only with these pronouns: *Je suis chez moi* (I'm at home), *Il reste chez lui* (He's staying at home).

## After c'est

After *c'est*, use a stressed pronoun.

<Example fr="C'est moi !" en="It's me!" />
<Example fr="C'est nous." en="It's us." />

*C'est eux* is the normal way to say "It's them".

## On their own

A stressed pronoun can be a complete answer.

<Example fr="Qui veut du café ? — Moi !" en="Who wants coffee? — Me!" />

## With et

When a pronoun is joined to someone else with *et*, it has to be a stressed one: *Paul et moi* (Paul and I), *toi et lui* (you and him).

<Example fr="Toi et lui, vous êtes en retard !" en="You and he are late!" />

## For emphasis

English stresses a word with the voice: "**I'm** tired." French adds a stressed pronoun, and the subject stays.

<Example fr="Moi, je suis fatiguée." en="I'm tired. (said by a woman)" />
<Example fr="Toi, tu aimes ça ?" en="Do you like that?" />

The verb doesn't change. It's *Moi, je suis…*, and never *Moi suis…*. The stressed pronoun is an extra, and *je* still does its normal job.

## Me too, me neither

To agree with someone, you need to look at whether their statement is positive or negative.

- *J'adore le chocolat.* — *Moi aussi.* (I love chocolate. — Me too.)
- *Je ne travaille pas lundi.* — *Moi non plus.* (I'm not working on Monday. — Me neither.)

You can change the pronoun, too: *Lui aussi* (Him too), *Elle non plus* (Her neither).

## En français

- *Qui vient avec moi au cinéma ?* (Who's coming with me to the cinema?)
- *Moi ! Et Paul ?* (Me! And Paul?)
- *Lui, il reste chez lui.* (He's staying at home.)
- *Je n'aime pas les films tristes.* (I don't like sad films.)
- *Moi non plus.* (Me neither.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Moi aussi or moi non plus?">
  *Moi aussi* agrees with a positive statement. *Moi non plus* agrees with a negative one: *Je n'aime pas le thé. — **Moi non plus**.* It's easy to say *Moi aussi* after a negative, because you're agreeing, but that doesn't work. Also remember that *eux* is masculine or mixed only. For a group of women, it's **elles**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Emphasis needs a word">
  In English you can change the meaning by leaning on one word with your voice: "*I* did the shopping." French doesn't work like that nearly as much. So when you want to underline who you mean, reach for a stressed pronoun at the start. *Moi, je fais les courses.* It's how French shows that you're contrasting yourself with somebody else.
</Callout>
