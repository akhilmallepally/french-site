By the end of this lesson you'll be able to make adverbs from adjectives, say how often and how well you do something, and put these words in the right place.

## What an adverb does

An adjective describes a noun, as you know from <LessonLink id="a1-adjective-agreement" text="the lesson on adjective agreement" />. An **adverb** describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb. It tells you how, how often or how much. Unlike adjectives, adverbs never change their ending.

## Making adverbs with -ment

Many adverbs are made by taking the **feminine** adjective and adding **-ment**, which is like "-ly" in English.

<VocabTable group="ment" />

If the masculine form ends in *-e*, like *rapide* or *facile*, that's the form you use anyway.

Here are the verbs and nouns you'll need for this lesson.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

<Example fr="Elle marche rapidement." en="She walks quickly." />
<Example fr="Heureusement, ce n'est pas grave." en="Fortunately, it's not serious." />

## Special patterns

Two groups of adjectives work differently, and a couple of everyday adverbs are exceptions.

<VocabTable group="special" />

- Adjectives in **-ent** make adverbs in **-emment**: *évident* becomes *évidemment*. *Lent* is an exception, as you've seen: it's *lentement*.
- Adjectives in **-ant** make adverbs in **-amment**: *constant* becomes *constamment*.
- These two endings **sound the same**, with the vowel of *a*, so you have to remember the spelling.
- When the masculine adjective ends in a vowel, add *-ment* to it directly: *vrai* gives *vraiment*, and *poli* gives *poliment*.

<Example fr="Tu as vraiment mal ?" en="Does it really hurt?" />

## Bien, mal, vite

Three of the most common adverbs have no *-ment* at all.

<VocabTable group="everyday" />

*Bien* and *mal* are adverbs. Their adjectives are *bon* and *mauvais*.

<Example fr="Il dort bien." en="He sleeps well." />
<Example fr="Elle parle vite." en="She speaks fast." />

## How often and how much

You already know *souvent* (often) from <LessonLink id="a1-review-free-time" text="the review of free time" />. Here are some more.

<VocabTable group="frequency" />

For *toujours* and *jamais*, see <LessonLink id="a2-negative-expressions" text="the lesson on other negative expressions" />.

- **Très** goes before adjectives and adverbs: *très fatigué*, *très bien*. It doesn't go before a verb.
- **Trop** means "too" before an adjective, and "too much" after a verb: *C'est trop cher*, *Il mange trop*.
- **Assez** means "fairly" before an adjective, and "enough" after a verb: *Il est assez grand* (He's fairly tall), *Il mange assez* (He eats enough).

<Example fr="Je marche souvent et je mange parfois à midi." en="I often walk, and I sometimes eat at noon." />

## Where they go

In the present, an adverb normally comes right after the verb.

<Example fr="Nous dormons mal." en="We sleep badly." />

In the passé composé, use this as a guide. **Short everyday adverbs** go between the auxiliary and the participle. **Most -ment adverbs** go after the participle.

<Example fr="J'ai bien mangé." en="I ate well." />
<Example fr="Il a mal compris." en="He misunderstood." />
<Example fr="J'ai trop bu." en="I drank too much." />
<Example fr="Elle a parlé doucement." en="She spoke quietly." />

The participles here come from <LessonLink id="a2-passe-compose-avoir" text="the lesson on the passé composé with avoir" /> and <LessonLink id="a2-past-participles-irregular" text="the lesson on irregular past participles" />.

## En français

- *Tu as bien dormi ?* (Did you sleep well?)
- *Non, j'ai mal dormi. Je suis très fatigué.* (No, I slept badly. I'm very tired.)
- *Tu travailles trop !* (You work too much!)
- *Évidemment !* (Obviously!)
- *Tu marches parfois ?* (Do you sometimes walk?)
- *Oui, souvent, mais lentement.* (Yes, often, but slowly.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Bien or bon?">
  In English, "good" sometimes gets used as an adverb. In French, keep them apart. **Bon** is an adjective, and it goes with a noun: **C'est un bon lit**. **Bien** is an adverb, and it goes with a verb: **Il dort bien**. The same goes for **mauvais** and **mal**. *Il dort bon* isn't French.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Spot the -ly">
  French *-ment* usually lines up with English "-ly". So when you meet a long word ending in *-ment* in a text, try swapping the ending for "-ly": *rapidement*, *rapidly*; *généralement*, *generally*. Often you'll understand it straight away, without looking it up. Just be careful with nouns such as *un appartement*, which also end in *-ment*.
</Callout>
