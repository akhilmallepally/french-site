By the end of this lesson you'll be able to answer the phone, ask for someone, leave a message, and spell your name or give a number.

## Answering and asking

A professional phone call usually uses *vous*, and short set phrases. Commands come from <LessonLink id="a1-imperative" text="the lesson on the imperative" />, and you can make a request with *pouvoir* from <LessonLink id="a1-modal-verbs" text="the lesson on pouvoir, vouloir and devoir" />, in the inverted style from <LessonLink id="a1-yes-no-questions" text="the lesson on yes/no questions" />.

<VocabTable group="answering" />

*Je vous passe…* is a fixed phrase for now, and you can use it with a name: *Je vous passe Madame Roche.*

<Example fr="Allô ? Oui, bonjour." en="Hello? Yes, good morning." />
<Example fr="C'est Karim à l'appareil." en="This is Karim speaking." />
<Example fr="Je voudrais parler à Madame Roche, s'il vous plaît." en="I'd like to speak to Mrs Roche, please." />

## When things don't go to plan

Sometimes the person isn't available, or the line isn't good.

<VocabTable group="problems" />

<Example fr="Monsieur Martin est en réunion." en="Mr Martin is in a meeting." />
<Example fr="Madame Roche est absente." en="Mrs Roche is out." />

*Occupé* and *absent* are adjectives, so they match the person.

## Leaving a message

<VocabTable group="messages" />

<Example fr="Je voudrais laisser un message." en="I'd like to leave a message." />
<Example fr="J'ai un SMS de Paul." en="I have a text from Paul." />

*Un SMS* and *un texto* mean the same thing, and both are common. Your own short messages can follow the patterns in <LessonLink id="a1-writing-a-short-message" text="the lesson on writing a short message" />.

## Names and numbers

To give your name, spell it using the letters from <LessonLink id="a1-alphabet-and-accents" text="the lesson on the alphabet and accents" />. *Comment ça s'écrit ?* (How do you spell that?) is the usual question.

French phone numbers are read in **pairs**, using the numbers from <LessonLink id="a1-numbers-21-100" text="the lesson on numbers 21 to 100" />. So 06 12 34 56 78 is *zéro six, douze, trente-quatre, cinquante-six, soixante-dix-huit*.

## A model call

| French | English |
|---|---|
| Allô, bonjour. | Hello, good morning. |
| Bonjour, je voudrais parler à Madame Roche, s'il vous plaît. | Hello, I'd like to speak to Mrs Roche, please. |
| C'est de la part de qui ? | Who's calling, please? |
| C'est Paul Martin à l'appareil. | This is Paul Martin speaking. |
| Ne quittez pas. Je vous passe Madame Roche. | Hold the line. I'll put you through to Mrs Roche. |

## Taking a message

| French | English |
|---|---|
| Madame Roche est en réunion. | Mrs Roche is in a meeting. |
| Je peux laisser un message ? | Can I leave a message? |
| Oui, bien sûr. | Yes, of course. |
| Pouvez-vous rappeler plus tard ? | Can you call back later? |
| Oui. Mon numéro, c'est le zéro six, vingt-deux, quarante-cinq, dix, trente. | Yes. My number is 06 22 45 10 30. |
| Pouvez-vous répéter, s'il vous plaît ? | Can you repeat that, please? |

<Callout type="warning" title="Allô is for the phone only">
  *Allô* looks like "hello", so it's tempting to say it when you meet someone. In French, it's only for the phone. When you meet someone face to face, say **bonjour** or **salut**. And don't drop *à l'appareil* for *Je suis Paul* on a professional call: **C'est Paul à l'appareil** is what people say.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ne quittez pas means hold the line">
  *Ne quittez pas* looks like "don't quit", and it's easy to wonder what you're being told not to give up. It means "hold the line". Literally, it's "don't leave", because *quitter* means "to leave". If you hear it, wait, and someone will come on the line in a moment.
</Callout>
