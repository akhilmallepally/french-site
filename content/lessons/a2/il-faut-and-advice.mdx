By the end of this lesson you'll be able to say what someone has to do, give simple advice, and tell someone what they must not do.

## Il faut

**Il faut** means that something is necessary. It's a fixed form: the *il* doesn't refer to anyone, so you can't use it with *je* or *tu*. In English, it might be "you have to", "we need to" or "one must", depending on the context.

<VocabTable group="core" />

After *il faut*, use the infinitive, just as you did after the verbs in <LessonLink id="a1-modal-verbs" text="the lesson on pouvoir, vouloir and devoir" />.

*Se reposer* comes from <LessonLink id="a1-daily-routine-reflexive" text="the lesson on daily routines" />, and *boire* and *dormir* come from <LessonLink id="a2-irregular-verbs-present" text="the lesson on common irregular verbs" />. Here are some more words for this lesson.

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

<Example fr="Il faut boire de l'eau en été." en="You have to drink water in summer." />
<Example fr="Il faut dormir huit heures." en="You need to sleep for eight hours." />
<Example fr="Tu as mal au dos ? Il faut marcher." en="Does your back hurt? You need to walk." />

You can also put a noun after *il faut*, to say what's needed. Use *du*, *de la* or *un* as you'd expect.

<Example fr="Il faut du temps." en="It takes time." />
<Example fr="Il faut une ordonnance." en="You need a prescription." />

## Advice with devoir

You already know *devoir* from <LessonLink id="a1-modal-verbs" text="the lesson on pouvoir, vouloir and devoir" />. It's more personal than *il faut*, since it's addressed to a particular person.

<Example fr="Tu dois manger." en="You have to eat." />
<Example fr="Vous devez prendre un comprimé." en="You have to take a tablet." />
<Example fr="Je dois aller à la pharmacie." en="I have to go to the pharmacy." />

Advice often starts from a problem, using the words in <LessonLink id="a2-body-and-health" text="the lesson on the body and health" />.

<Example fr="J'ai mal à la gorge. Il faut boire de l'eau." en="My throat hurts. You need to drink water." />
<Example fr="J'ai de la fièvre. Il faut dormir." en="I have a temperature. You need to sleep." />

## Il faut or devoir?

The two often overlap. Use *il faut* for something that's true in general, or when you don't want to point at anyone in particular. Use *devoir* when you're talking directly about one person or a group. If you're giving advice to a friend, either is fine: *Il faut manger* (You need to eat) or *Tu dois manger* (You have to eat).

## Reflexive verbs

With a reflexive verb, the pronoun depends on what comes before it.

- After **il faut**, it stays **se**: *Il faut se reposer* (You need to rest).
- After **devoir**, it matches the subject: *Tu dois te reposer*, *Vous devez vous reposer*, *Je dois me reposer*.

<Example fr="Il faut se coucher tôt." en="You need to go to bed early." />
<Example fr="Vous devez vous lever." en="You have to get up." />

## Saying "must not"

Put *ne…pas* around *faut*. Be careful, because the meaning may not be what you expect.

<Example fr="Il ne faut pas boire de café le soir." en="You mustn't drink coffee in the evening." />
<Example fr="Il ne faut pas toucher." en="You mustn't touch." />

To say that something **isn't necessary**, use this fixed phrase, followed by an infinitive.

<Example fr="Tu n'as pas besoin de venir." en="You don't have to come." />
<Example fr="Vous n'avez pas besoin de prendre rendez-vous." en="You don't have to make an appointment." />

Learn it as a whole for now. *De* shortens to *d'* before a vowel: *Tu n'as pas besoin d'attendre*.

## En français

- *J'ai mal à la tête.* (My head hurts.)
- *Tu dois te reposer. Il faut dormir.* (You have to rest. You need to sleep.)
- *Je dois travailler !* (I have to work!)
- *Non, tu n'as pas besoin de travailler aujourd'hui.* (No, you don't have to work today.)
- *Et le café ?* (And coffee?)
- *Il ne faut pas boire de café ! Il faut boire de l'eau.* (You mustn't drink coffee! You need to drink water.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Il ne faut pas doesn't mean 'you don't have to'">
  In English, "you don't have to" means it's optional. In French, **il ne faut pas** is a prohibition: you must not. *Il ne faut pas manger* means "You mustn't eat." If you only want to say that something isn't necessary, use **Tu n'as pas besoin de manger**. Mixing these up can cause real confusion.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Il faut sounds gentler">
  *Il faut* doesn't point at anyone, so *Il faut se reposer* (You need to rest) feels less bossy than *Tu dois te reposer* (You have to rest). When you're giving a friend advice and don't want it to sound like an order, start with *il faut*.
</Callout>
