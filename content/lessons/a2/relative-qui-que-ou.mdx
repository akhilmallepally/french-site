By the end of this lesson you'll be able to join two short sentences into one, so your French sounds less choppy.

## Why join sentences?

Compare these two ways of saying the same thing.

- *J'ai un ami. Cet ami habite à Lille.* (I have a friend. This friend lives in Lille.)
- *J'ai un ami qui habite à Lille.* (I have a friend who lives in Lille.)

The word that joins them is a **relative pronoun**. It stands for a noun that's just been mentioned. French has three simple ones to start with.

<VocabTable group="forms" />

These aren't the question words from <LessonLink id="a1-question-words" text="the lesson on question words" />, even though they look the same. Here, they're in the middle of a sentence, and they aren't asking anything.

## Qui: the subject

**Qui** stands for the subject of the next verb. It can refer to a person or a thing, and it's followed straight away by a verb.

<Example fr="Je connais la femme qui travaille ici." en="I know the woman who works here." />
<Example fr="C'est un train qui part à huit heures." en="It's a train that leaves at eight o'clock." />
<Example fr="Il y a un parc qui est très calme." en="There's a park that's very quiet." />

*Qui* never shortens: *la fille qui arrive*.

## Que: the object

**Que** stands for the direct object of the next verb, as *le* or *la* do in <LessonLink id="a2-direct-object-pronouns" text="the lesson on direct object pronouns" />. It can also refer to a person or a thing, and it's followed by a subject and then a verb.

<Example fr="Le livre que je lis est intéressant." en="The book I'm reading is interesting." />
<Example fr="Le film qu'elle regarde est drôle." en="The film she's watching is funny." />
<Example fr="La femme que je connais habite ici." en="The woman I know lives here." />

*Que* shortens to **qu'** before a vowel or silent *h*.

## Où: places and times

**Où** stands for a place or a time.

<Example fr="La ville où j'habite est petite." en="The town where I live is small." />
<Example fr="C'est le jour où nous sommes arrivés." en="It's the day when we arrived." />

## The test

Look at the word right after the gap.

| What comes next | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a verb | *qui* | *la femme **qui** travaille* (the woman who works) |
| a subject (a noun or a pronoun), then a verb | *que* or *qu'* | *la femme **que** je connais* (the woman I know) |

*Où* needs its own check, because a subject follows it too. Use it when the clause says what happens in that place or at that time: *la ville **où** j'habite*. If the place is the direct object of the next verb, it's still *que*: *la ville **que** j'aime* (the town I love).

## Agreement after que

You've seen in <LessonLink id="a2-pronouns-in-passe-compose" text="the lesson on pronouns in the passé composé" /> that the participle agrees with a direct object that comes before the verb. *Que* is exactly that, so the participle agrees with the noun *que* stands for.

<Example fr="Voici la lettre que j'ai écrite." en="Here's the letter I wrote." />
<Example fr="Voici les clés que j'ai prises." en="Here are the keys I took." />
<Example fr="C'est la photo que j'ai prise." en="It's the photo I took." />
<Example fr="C'est la chose que j'ai faite." en="It's the thing I did." />

With many participles, you can't hear this: *le film que j'ai vu* and *la maison que j'ai vue* sound the same.

## En français

- *Tu connais l'homme qui habite ici ?* (Do you know the man who lives here?)
- *Oui, c'est le voisin que je vois le samedi.* (Yes, he's the neighbour I see on Saturdays.)
- *Et la maison où il habite ?* (And the house where he lives?)
- *C'est la maison que j'adore.* (It's the house I love.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't drop it, and don't use qui for an object">
  English can leave out the relative: "the book I read". French can't: **le livre que je lis**. And don't choose *qui* just because you're talking about a person. If a subject follows, it's *que*: **la femme que je connais**, not *la femme qui je connais*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Two sentences become one">
  When your writing sounds choppy, look for two short sentences side by side that share a noun: *J'ai un chat. Mon chat dort tout le temps.* Replace the second mention with *qui* or *que*, and join them. It's the quickest way to make your French read as if it's a level higher.
</Callout>
