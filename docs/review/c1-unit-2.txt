By the end of this lesson you'll be able to put the verb before its subject where careful written French expects it, and to tell when inversion is required, optional or wrong.

## What you already know

You know inversion in questions (*parlez-vous anglais ?*) from <LessonLink id="a1-yes-no-questions" text="the lesson on yes/no questions" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />, you met complex inversion with a noun subject (*Paul vient-il ?*) and inversion after *peut-être*, *sans doute* and *aussi* at the start of a sentence. You also used *à peine… que* with inversion in <LessonLink id="c1-passe-anterieur" text="the lesson on the passé antérieur" />. This lesson adds the other places where written French inverts.

## Three cases: required, optional, wrong

Stylistic inversion isn't a question. It's a word order that marks careful writing, and it follows a few patterns. In some places it's required: leaving it out sounds like a mistake. In others it's optional: it raises the register, and the plain order is also correct. In a few places it's wrong. Usage varies between writers, so treat the lists in this lesson as rules of thumb for careful modern prose.

The two forms work as in questions. With a pronoun subject, the pronoun follows the verb or the auxiliary, with *-t-* between two vowels. With a noun subject, the noun stays in front and a pronoun follows the verb: this is complex inversion.

<Example fr="Sans doute le témoin s'est-il trompé." en="The witness was probably mistaken. (formal)" />

## After adverbs at the start of a sentence

<VocabTable group="adverbs" />

You know *ainsi* (thus) from <LessonLink id="b2-logical-connectors" text="the lesson on logical connectors" />. At the start of a sentence, it can take inversion. With a comma and the normal order, it's also correct, and less formal.

<Example fr="Ainsi le lecteur comprend-il enfin la fin du roman." en="Thus the reader finally understands the ending of the novel. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Ainsi, le lecteur comprend enfin la fin du roman." en="Thus the reader finally understands the ending of the novel. (standard)" />

*Du moins* corrects or limits what was just said: "or at least". After a comma, inversion is common in writing, but the normal order is also correct.

<Example fr="La romancière a fini son livre, du moins le dit-elle." en="The novelist has finished her book, or at least that's what she says. (formal)" />

*Encore* at the start of a sentence, with inversion, means "still" or "even so": it adds a condition that limits what came before. In this meaning, inversion is required. In the middle of a sentence, *encore* keeps its usual meaning, "still", which you know from <LessonLink id="a2-negative-expressions" text="the lesson on negative expressions" />.

<Example fr="Le plan est bon. Encore faut-il que tout le monde soit d'accord." en="The plan is good. Even so, everyone still has to agree. (formal)" />

*Toujours est-il que* is a fixed phrase: the inversion is frozen inside it, and the clause after *que* keeps normal order. It means "the fact remains that" and moves past a doubt to what is certain.

<Example fr="Je ne sais pas pourquoi il est parti. Toujours est-il qu'il n'est pas revenu." en="I don't know why he left. The fact remains that he hasn't come back." />

When *à peine* opens a written sentence, careful writers invert, in any tense. Inside the sentence, *à peine* follows the auxiliary and there's no inversion.

<Example fr="À peine sommes-nous arrivés qu'il a commencé à pleuvoir." en="No sooner had we arrived than it started to rain. (formal)" />

## After direct speech

<VocabTable group="incises" />

When a reporting verb follows or interrupts a quotation, its subject comes after it. This short clause is called *une incise*. In written French, the inversion is required, whatever the tense.

- With a pronoun subject, the verb and the pronoun are joined by a hyphen, with *-t-* between two vowels: *dit-il*, *demanda-t-elle*, *a-t-il répondu*.
- With a noun subject, there's no extra pronoun: *répondit le témoin*, *dit Paul*. This is simple inversion, unlike complex inversion in questions.
- An incise can also sit inside the quotation, between two commas.

<Example fr="« Je pars demain », dit-elle." en="“I'm leaving tomorrow,” she said." />
<Example fr="« Déjà ? » s'étonna le romancier." en="“Already?” the novelist wondered." />
<Example fr="« Je sais, soupira-t-il, mais c'est trop tard. »" en="“I know,” he sighed, “but it's too late.”" />
<Example fr="« Vous avez tort », rétorqua la lectrice." en="“You're wrong,” the reader retorted." />

Novels use many reporting verbs besides *dire*: *murmurer*, *soupirer*, *s'étonner*, *rétorquer*, and *reprendre* (to go on speaking).

<Example fr="« Il faut partir, reprit-elle. Il faut partir maintenant », murmura-t-elle." en="“We have to leave,” she went on. “We have to leave now,” she whispered." />

In casual speech, you'll hear *« Je pars », il a dit* with normal order. It's fine when you talk, but in writing it reads as a mistake.

## In relative clauses

<VocabTable group="writing" />

After *que*, *où* and *dont*, a noun subject can follow the verb. It's optional, and writers use it when the verb is short and the subject is long, so that the sentence doesn't end on a lone verb.

<Example fr="La maison où vivait la romancière est aujourd'hui un musée." en="The house where the novelist lived is a museum today." />
<Example fr="C'est la règle que suivent beaucoup de lecteurs." en="It's the rule that many readers follow." />

This inversion is wrong in two cases. With a pronoun subject, keep normal order: *le livre qu'il lit*, not *que lit-il*. And when the verb has its own direct object, the subject must stay in front: *le jour où Paul a rencontré sa femme*.

## Where inversion is wrong

As a rule of thumb, don't invert in these places:

- After *peut-être que* and *sans doute que*: *que* replaces the inversion. Say *peut-être qu'il est malade* or *peut-être est-il malade*, never both.
- In a relative clause with a pronoun subject, or with a direct object after the verb.
- After *aussi* in the middle of a sentence, where it means "also".

## The whole list

- Required: in an incise (*dit-il*, *répondit le témoin*), after *encore* meaning "even so", and in *toujours est-il que*.
- Usual in careful writing: after *à peine*, *peut-être* and *sans doute* at the start of a sentence, and after *aussi* meaning "therefore".
- Optional: after *ainsi* and *du moins*, and in a relative clause with a noun subject.
- Wrong: after *peut-être que*, and in a relative clause with a pronoun subject or a direct object.

## En français

Inès reads the short story her colleague Théo wrote for the office newsletter. They speak in everyday spoken French and quote his text.

- *J'ai lu ton histoire. Elle est bien, mais il y a un problème dans les dialogues.* (I've read your story. It's good, but there's a problem in the dialogues.)
- *Quel problème ? J'ai écrit : « Je pars », il a dit.* (What problem? I wrote: "I'm leaving," he said.)
- *À l'écrit, on met le verbe avant le sujet : « Je pars », dit-il.* (In writing, you put the verb before the subject: "Je pars", dit-il.)
- *Et avec un prénom ? « Je pars », Paul dit-il ?* (And with a first name? "Je pars", Paul dit-il?)
- *Non, là, il n'y a pas de pronom : « Je pars », dit Paul.* (No, then there's no pronoun: "Je pars", dit Paul.)
- *D'accord. Et la phrase avec « peut-être » ?* (OK. And the sentence with peut-être?)
- *Tu as écrit « peut-être qu'il est malade ». C'est correct, mais c'est de l'oral.* (You wrote "peut-être qu'il est malade". It's correct, but it's spoken French.)
- *Alors j'écris « peut-être est-il malade » ?* (So I write "peut-être est-il malade"?)
- *Oui, c'est plus soutenu. Mais jamais « peut-être qu'est-il ».* (Yes, it's more formal. But never "peut-être qu'est-il".)
- *Et « toujours est-il que » ? Je l'ai vu dans un roman.* (And "toujours est-il que"? I saw it in a novel.)
- *Ça veut dire « en tout cas ». Par exemple : ton histoire a des erreurs, toujours est-il qu'elle me plaît !* (It means "anyway". For example: your story has mistakes, but the fact remains that I like it!)
- *Merci ! Je change les dialogues ce soir, du moins j'essaie.* (Thanks! I'll change the dialogues tonight, or at least I'll try.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Dit Paul, not Paul dit-il">
  Learners often use complex inversion in an incise because they know it from questions. After a quotation, a noun subject simply follows the verb: **« Je pars », dit Paul**, not *« Je pars », Paul dit-il*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Collect reporting verbs">
  When you read a novel, note every reporting verb you find in an incise, with its sentence: *murmura-t-elle*, *rétorqua le témoin*. After a few chapters, you'll have a list that lets your own dialogues go far beyond *dit-il*.
</Callout>

## En résumé

L'inversion stylistique met le verbe avant le sujet dans la langue écrite soignée. Avec un pronom, on écrit dit-il ou demanda-t-elle. Avec un nom, on garde le nom devant et on ajoute un pronom après le verbe : sans doute le témoin s'est-il trompé. Dans une incise, après une citation, l'inversion est obligatoire, et un nom sujet suit le verbe sans pronom : « Je pars », dit Paul. L'inversion est aussi obligatoire après encore au début de la phrase, quand il ajoute une condition, et dans toujours est-il que. Après à peine, peut-être et sans doute au début de la phrase, elle est la règle à l'écrit soigné. Après ainsi et du moins, elle est possible. Dans une relative, un nom sujet peut suivre le verbe, mais pas un pronom : le livre qu'il lit. Après peut-être que, on garde l'ordre normal.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize the *ne* that doesn't negate, use it in careful writing, and never confuse it with a real negation.

## What you already know

You know *avoir peur que* and *craindre que* + subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b1-subjonctif-wishes-emotions" text="the lesson on the subjunctive of wishes and emotions" />, and *le ne explétif* after *avant que* and *à moins que* from <LessonLink id="b2-subjunctive-conjunctions" text="the lesson on conjunctions with the subjunctive" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-purpose-expressions" text="the lesson on purpose expressions" />, you saw it after *de peur que* and *de crainte que*, and you learned *éviter que*. This lesson completes the list and shows how to tell it from a negation.

## What the ne explétif is

The *ne explétif* is a small *ne* with no negative meaning and no *pas*. It doesn't change what the sentence says: with or without it, the meaning is the same. It marks a careful, formal register, and in most places it's optional. In everyday speech, people usually leave it out.

<Example fr="Partez avant qu'il ne soit trop tard." en="Leave before it's too late. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Partez avant qu'il soit trop tard." en="Leave before it's too late. (standard)" />

It appears where the idea behind the clause is a risk, a fear or something you want to prevent, or after a comparison of inequality.

## After words of fear and prevention

<VocabTable group="triggers" />

After *avoir peur que*, *craindre que*, *redouter que*, *de peur que* and *de crainte que*, the subjunctive is often preceded by the *ne explétif* in writing.

<Example fr="Je redoute qu'il ne fasse une erreur." en="I dread that he'll make a mistake. (formal)" />

*Empêcher que* and *éviter que* express what you want to prevent. They take the subjunctive, and the *ne* is common but optional.

<Example fr="Il faut empêcher que la situation ne devienne grave." en="We must prevent the situation from becoming serious. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Nous avons tout fait pour éviter qu'il ne parte." en="We did everything we could to stop him leaving. (formal)" />

*Il s'en faut de peu que* means "very nearly": something almost happened. You'll mostly meet it in the passé composé, *il s'en est fallu de peu que*, with the subjunctive and usually the *ne*.

<Example fr="Il s'en est fallu de peu que nous ne soyons en retard." en="We very nearly were late. (formal)" />

When the fear itself is denied or questioned (*je ne crains pas que*, *craignez-vous que…?*), the *ne explétif* is usually left out. Treat this as a rule of thumb.

## After a comparison

After a comparison of inequality, such as *plus… que*, *moins… que*, *autre… que* or *meilleur… que*, followed by a clause, careful French adds *ne* before the verb. Here the verb is in the indicative, not the subjunctive. You can also add *le*, which stands for the idea.

<Example fr="L'incendie s'est propagé plus vite que nous ne le pensions." en="The fire spread faster than we thought. (formal)" />
<Example fr="L'issue du match a été moins heureuse que nous ne l'espérions." en="The outcome of the match was less happy than we'd hoped. (formal)" />

After *autant… que* and *aussi… que*, which compare equal things, there's no *ne*.

## Ne explétif or negation?

This is the key point. A real negation has a second word: *pas*, *plus*, *jamais*, *rien*. The *ne explétif* stands alone. With a word of fear, the two can mean opposite things.

| Sentence | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *J'ai peur qu'il ne vienne.* | I'm afraid he'll come. (formal) |
| *J'ai peur qu'il vienne.* | I'm afraid he'll come. (standard) |
| *J'ai peur qu'il ne vienne pas.* | I'm afraid he won't come. |

The same test works after *de crainte que*.

<Example fr="Elle a gardé ses soupçons pour elle, de crainte qu'on ne la croie pas." en="She kept her suspicions to herself, for fear that people wouldn't believe her." />

Here *ne… pas* is a real negation: she was afraid of not being believed.

## Using it in your own writing

In a formal letter, a report or an essay, the *ne explétif* is a mark of care, but it's never required. As a rule of thumb, use it where readers expect it, after *avant que*, *à moins que* and the verbs of fear, and leave it out of quick emails to colleagues and of anything you say aloud.

Don't add it after *bien que*, *pour que* or *après que*. These conjunctions don't express a risk, and a *ne* there reads as a mistake or as a negation without its partner. It never goes with an infinitive either: *avant de partir*, *de peur de glisser*. Remember too that with *avant que* and *de peur que*, when both verbs have the same subject, you normally use the infinitive instead, as you saw at B2.

<Example fr="Je vous écris avant que vous ne preniez votre décision." en="I'm writing to you before you make your decision. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Nous partirons demain, à moins que la tempête ne s'aggrave." en="We'll leave tomorrow, unless the storm gets worse. (formal)" />

## Words for risks

<VocabTable group="words" />

The *ne explétif* is common in formal reports about risks: accidents, damage, a situation that could get worse. The verbs below are useful in such reports, in any tense.

<Example fr="Le vieux pont s'est effondré pendant la tempête." en="The old bridge collapsed during the storm." />
<Example fr="La situation s'aggrave plus vite qu'on ne le pensait." en="The situation is getting worse faster than people thought. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Il faut trouver la fuite avant qu'il ne soit trop tard." en="We must find the leak before it's too late. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Tenez-lui la main : il faut éviter qu'il ne glisse." en="Hold his hand: we must stop him slipping. (formal)" />

## En français

Claire has written a formal letter to a client. She asks her manager, Monsieur Duval, to read it.

- *Monsieur Duval, vous pouvez lire ma lettre au client ?* (Mr Duval, can you read my letter to the client?)
- *Bien sûr. Vous avez écrit : « avant que nous ne fassions une nouvelle offre ».* (Of course. You wrote: "before we make a new offer".)
- *Oui. C'est une erreur ? Il n'y a pas de « pas ».* (Yes. Is that a mistake? There's no "pas".)
- *Non, c'est le ne explétif. Il ne veut pas dire « pas », et dans une lettre, il est très bien.* (No, it's the ne explétif. It doesn't mean "not", and in a letter it's very good.)
- *Et à l'oral ?* (And when speaking?)
- *À l'oral, on ne le dit pas souvent. « Avant que nous fassions une offre » est aussi correct.* (When speaking, people don't say it often. "Avant que nous fassions une offre" is also correct.)
- *Et si j'écris : « Je crains que le client ne vienne » ?* (And if I write: "Je crains que le client ne vienne"?)
- *Ça veut dire que vous avez peur qu'il vienne. Mais « je crains qu'il ne vienne pas », c'est le contraire !* (That means you're afraid he'll come. But "je crains qu'il ne vienne pas" is the opposite!)
- *Je comprends. La lettre m'a pris plus de temps que je ne pensais.* (I understand. The letter took me longer than I thought.)
- *Comme toujours ! Envoyez-la avant que le client ne parte en vacances.* (As always! Send it before the client goes on holiday.)
- *Je l'envoie tout de suite. Merci pour votre aide.* (I'll send it straight away. Thanks for your help.)
- *Avec plaisir. Bonne soirée !* (My pleasure. Have a good evening!)

<Callout type="warning" title="J'ai peur qu'il ne vienne means he might come">
  Learners often read every *ne* as "not". Without *pas*, the *ne* after a word of fear is explétif: **j'ai peur qu'il ne vienne** means "I'm afraid he'll come". To say "I'm afraid he won't come", you need **j'ai peur qu'il ne vienne pas**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Cover the ne and reread">
  When you meet a *ne* in a formal text, first look further on for a partner word (*pas*, *plus*, *jamais*, *rien*). If there's none, cover the *ne* with your finger and reread the sentence: if the meaning stays the same, it's explétif.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le ne explétif est un petit ne sans pas, qui ne fait pas de la phrase une négation. Avec ou sans lui, la phrase veut dire la même chose. On le trouve surtout à l'écrit soutenu, et on peut souvent ne pas le mettre. Il suit les mots de peur et les verbes qui veulent empêcher quelque chose : avoir peur que, craindre que, redouter que, de crainte que, empêcher que, éviter que, avant que, à moins que, il s'en faut de peu que. Après ces mots, le verbe est au subjonctif : avant qu'il ne parte. Après plus… que ou moins… que, le verbe est à l'indicatif : c'est plus difficile que je ne pensais. Attention : avec pas, c'est une vraie négation. J'ai peur qu'il ne vienne et j'ai peur qu'il ne vienne pas ont des sens contraires.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read the negatives of literary and formal French, use a few of them in careful writing, and turn each one into everyday French.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />, you met *ne… guère* (hardly) and *ne* alone with *pouvoir* and *savoir*: *je ne saurais dire*. You know *ne… ni… ni* from <LessonLink id="b1-ne-que-ni-ni" text="the lesson on ne… que and ne… ni… ni" />, and *ne… aucun* from <LessonLink id="b1-indefinite-pronouns" text="the lesson on indefinite pronouns" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-ne-expletif" text="the lesson on the ne explétif" />, you saw a *ne* that doesn't negate. This lesson is about the ones that do.

## Negatives without pas

<VocabTable group="negatives" />

Literary and formal French has several negatives that replace *pas*. All but one keep *ne* before the verb, and none of them takes *pas* as well. The exception is *sans… ni*, which takes neither *ne* nor *pas*: *sans argent ni amis*. You'll read them in novels, in older texts and in very formal letters. In conversation, they sound bookish or ironic.

## Ne… point

*Ne… point* works like *ne… pas*, in the same places. It's literary and a little old-fashioned, and it often sounds stronger: "not at all".

<Example fr="Je ne regrette point notre rencontre." en="I don't regret our meeting in the least. (literary)" />
<Example fr="Sa fierté ne lui permettait point de se plaindre." en="His pride didn't allow him to complain. (literary)" />

## Nullement and aucunement

*Nullement* and *aucunement* mean "not at all, in no way". They follow the verb or the auxiliary, like *pas*, and they can also stand alone as a formal reply.

<Example fr="Je n'en doute nullement." en="I don't doubt it at all. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Il n'était aucunement fâché. Fâché ? Nullement." en="He wasn't angry in any way. Angry? Not at all. (formal)" />

## Nul ne and nul + noun

*Nul*, as a subject, means "nobody". It's the formal equivalent of *personne*, and it takes *ne* without *pas*. As an adjective, *nul* or *nulle* means "no" and agrees with its noun.

<Example fr="Nul ne connaissait son malheur." en="Nobody knew of his misfortune. (literary)" />
<Example fr="Il n'avait nulle envie de partir." en="He had no wish to leave. (literary)" />

You know *c'était nul* (it was rubbish) from <LessonLink id="a2-talking-about-your-weekend" text="the lesson on talking about your weekend" />. That *nul* is an ordinary adjective with a different meaning, and it takes *pas* like any other: *ce n'était pas nul*.

## Ne alone with oser and cesser

With *pouvoir* and *savoir*, you already use *ne* alone in formal French. *Oser* (to dare) and *cesser* (to stop) work the same way. *Ne cesser de* + infinitive means "to keep doing": the action doesn't stop. With all four verbs, *ne* alone (*il ne cesse de pleuvoir*, *je n'ose le dire*) is formal or literary. *Ne… pas* (*il ne cesse pas de pleuvoir*, *je n'ose pas le dire*) is standard French and fully correct.

<Example fr="Je n'ose lui demander de me pardonner." en="I dare not ask him to forgive me. (literary)" />
<Example fr="Il ne cesse de pleuvoir depuis trois jours." en="It's been raining non-stop for three days. (formal)" />

## Ni in formal prose

You know *ne… ni… ni* with nouns: *il n'a ni frère ni sœur*. With two verbs, formal French puts *ne* before the first and *ni ne* before the second.

<Example fr="Il ne mange ni ne dort." en="He neither eats nor sleeps. (formal)" />

After *sans*, formal French joins a second element with *ni*: *sans argent ni amis*. Everyday French repeats *sans*: *sans argent et sans amis*.

<Example fr="Il travailla toute la nuit, sans repos ni plainte." en="He worked all night, without rest or complaint. (literary)" />

## With an infinitive

With an infinitive, both parts of these negatives usually come before it, as with *ne pas*: *pour ne pas être en retard*. The same holds for *ne point* and *ne jamais*. *Nullement* and *aucunement* are different: they follow the infinitive, like an adverb.

<Example fr="Il préféra ne point répondre." en="He preferred not to answer at all. (literary)" />
<Example fr="Elle dit ne regretter nullement son choix." en="She said she didn't regret her choice in the least. (formal)" />

## Which ones to use yourself

You'll read all of these forms, but you don't need to write all of them. In a formal letter or an essay, *ne cesser de*, *nullement*, *nul ne* and *sans… ni* are natural and well received. *Ne… point* belongs to literature and older texts: use it only when you imitate a literary style, or it will sound like a pastiche. In emails, at work and in speech, go back to the everyday forms in the table below.

## Words for the literary register

<VocabTable group="words" />

These words often go with the negatives above in older prose. *Suffire* (to be enough) is also everyday French.

<Example fr="Un mot suffit : nul ne répondit." en="One word was enough: nobody answered. (literary)" />

## The whole list

| Literary or formal | Everyday French |
|---|---|
| *je ne l'ai point vu* | *je ne l'ai pas vu* |
| *je n'en doute nullement / aucunement* | *je n'en doute pas du tout* |
| *nul ne sait* | *personne ne sait* |
| *il n'avait nulle envie* | *il n'avait aucune envie* |
| *je n'ose répondre* | *je n'ose pas répondre* |
| *il ne cesse de pleuvoir* | *il ne cesse pas de pleuvoir*, *il n'arrête pas de pleuvoir* |
| *il ne mange ni ne dort* | *il ne mange pas et il ne dort pas* |
| *sans argent ni amis* | *sans argent et sans amis* |

## En français

Camille is reading a novel from the nineteenth century. Yanis looks over her shoulder. They speak everyday French and quote the book.

- *C'est quoi, ce vieux livre ?* (What's that old book? (familiar))
- *Un roman du dix-neuvième siècle. Écoute : « Je n'ose vous écrire. »* (A novel from the nineteenth century. Listen: "Je n'ose vous écrire.")
- *« Je n'ose » ? Il manque le « pas », non ?* ("Je n'ose"? The "pas" is missing, isn't it?)
- *Non, avec oser, à l'écrit soutenu, « ne » suffit.* (No, with oser, in formal writing, "ne" is enough.)
- *Nous, on dirait « je n'ose pas t'écrire ».* (We'd say "je n'ose pas t'écrire". (spoken))
- *Exactement. Plus loin : « Nul ne connaît mon secret. »* (Exactly. Further on: "Nul ne connaît mon secret.")
- *C'est-à-dire « personne ne connaît mon secret » ?* (Meaning "nobody knows my secret"?)
- *Oui. Et à la fin : « Je ne regrette point notre rencontre. »* (Yes. And at the end: "Je ne regrette point notre rencontre.")
- *« Point », c'est comme « pas » ?* (Is "point" like "pas"?)
- *Oui, mais plus fort et plus littéraire. Aujourd'hui, on ne le dit presque plus.* (Yes, but stronger and more literary. Today, people hardly say it any more.)
- *Il n'arrête pas de se plaindre, ton héros !* (He doesn't stop complaining, your hero!)
- *L'auteur, lui, écrirait : « il ne cesse de se plaindre ».* (The author would write: "il ne cesse de se plaindre".)

<Callout type="warning" title="Nul ne sait, not nul ne sait pas">
  Learners often add *pas* out of habit. *Point*, *nullement*, *aucunement* and *nul* already complete the negation, so *pas* makes the sentence wrong: **nul ne sait**, **je n'en doute nullement**, never *nul ne sait pas*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Look for the partner of ne">
  When you meet *ne* in a literary text, look for its partner: *pas*, *point*, *guère*, *nul*, *ni*, *nullement*. If there's none, check the verb (*oser*, *cesser*, *pouvoir*, *savoir*). If it's none of these, think of the *ne explétif* from the last lesson.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La langue littéraire et la langue soutenue ont plusieurs formes de négation sans pas. Ne… point a le sens de ne… pas, mais il est plus fort et un peu ancien : je ne regrette point notre rencontre. Nullement et aucunement veulent dire pas du tout : je n'en doute nullement. Nul ne a le sens de personne ne, et nul ou nulle devant un nom a le sens d'aucun : nul ne sait, il n'avait nulle envie de partir. Avec oser, cesser, pouvoir et savoir, ne peut rester seul à l'écrit soutenu : je n'ose répondre, il ne cesse de pleuvoir. Avec pas, la phrase est courante et tout à fait correcte. Avec deux verbes, on écrit ne… ni ne : il ne mange ni ne dort. Après sans, le registre soutenu ajoute un second mot avec ni, sans ne et sans pas : sans argent ni amis. Avec point, nullement, aucunement et nul, on n'ajoute jamais pas.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to put the topic first or last in spoken French with the right pronoun, front a word in formal writing, and choose the kind of emphasis that fits the register.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-mise-en-relief" text="the lesson on emphasis" />, you learned *c'est… qui*, *c'est… que* and *ce qui…, c'est*, and you met spoken dislocation briefly: *moi, je préfère le train*, *le café, je l'adore*. In <LessonLink id="b2-colloquial-french" text="the lesson on spoken, everyday French" />, you saw that speakers announce the subject and then repeat it: *mon frère, il travaille à Lyon*. This lesson shows how dislocation works with every kind of pronoun, and what formal writing does instead.

## Left dislocation: the topic first

<VocabTable group="spoken" />

In speech, French often puts *le thème* (the topic, what the sentence is about) at the start, and then takes it up with a pronoun inside the sentence. This is called *la dislocation*. The pronoun is the one the verb needs. Don't confuse *le thème* with *le sujet*, the grammatical subject.

<Example fr="Dans « ce film, je l'ai adoré », le thème est ce film, et le sujet est je." en="In “ce film, je l'ai adoré”, the topic is ce film, and the subject is je." />

| The verb takes… | Pronoun | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a direct object | *le, la, les* | *Ce film, je l'ai adoré.* |
| *à* + a person | *lui, leur* | *Ton frère, je lui ai écrit.* |
| *à* + a thing | *y* | *Ce projet, j'y pense souvent.* |
| *de* + a thing, or an amount | *en* | *Du café, j'en bois trop.* |
| a subject | *il, elle, ils, elles* | *Mon frère, il travaille à Lyon.* |

<Example fr="Cette pièce de théâtre, je l'ai vue deux fois." en="I've seen that play twice. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Tes parents, tu leur as parlé ?" en="Have you spoken to your parents? (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Des comédiens comme ça, il y en a peu." en="There aren't many actors like that. (spoken)" />

The past participle agrees as usual when a direct object pronoun comes before it: *la pièce… je l'ai vue*.

## Ça for a general idea

When the topic is a noun in a general sense, especially with verbs of liking, speakers often take it up with *ça*, not with *le*. Treat this as a rule of thumb.

<Example fr="Le chocolat, j'adore ça." en="I love chocolate. (spoken; chocolate in general)" />
<Example fr="Ce chocolat, je l'adore." en="I love this chocolate. (spoken; one particular chocolate)" />

## Right dislocation: the topic at the end

The topic can also come at the end, after a pronoun that announces it. This is common when the topic is already clear from the situation, or as an afterthought.

<Example fr="Je l'ai adoré, ce film." en="I loved that film. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Il est où, ton sac ?" en="Where's your bag? (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Elle m'a bouleversé, cette représentation." en="That performance moved me deeply. (spoken; a man speaking)" />

## Moi, je… and two topics at once

A stressed pronoun (*moi*, *toi*, *lui*, *nous*…) at the start marks a contrast with other people: *moi, je reste*. You can combine it with a second topic, which gives a sentence with two dislocations.

<Example fr="Moi, ce décor, je l'ai trouvé très beau." en="Personally, I found that set very beautiful. (spoken)" />

## A spoken feature

Dislocation is normal in conversation and in informal writing such as messages. In a report, an essay or a formal letter, it sounds like speech. There, use the plain order, or *c'est… que*, or *quant à* for a new topic.

<Example fr="J'ai beaucoup aimé la mise en scène." en="I liked the staging very much." />
<Example fr="C'est la mise en scène que j'ai le plus aimée." en="It was the staging that I liked most." />

## Fronting in formal writing

<VocabTable group="written" />

Formal writing has its own ways of putting a word first. They don't repeat anything with a pronoun: the word moves, and the subject often comes after the verb.

**Rares sont ceux qui** and **nombreux sont ceux qui** put the adjective first, then *être*, then the subject. The adjective agrees with the noun: *rares sont les pièces*, *nombreuses sont les comédiennes*.

<Example fr="Rares sont ceux qui ont vu la première représentation." en="Few people saw the first performance. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Nombreuses sont les critiques qui saluent le metteur en scène." en="Many reviews praise the director. (formal)" />

**Reste** at the start, without *il*, introduces what is still to be dealt with. *Reste à savoir si* means "it remains to be seen whether". With a plural noun, the verb is plural: *restent deux questions*. The impersonal version stays singular: *il reste deux questions*. The standard versions are *il reste à savoir si* and *il reste la question de*.

<Example fr="Reste à savoir si le public suivra." en="It remains to be seen whether the audience will follow. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Reste la question du décor." en="There remains the question of the set. (formal)" />

**Tel est**, **telle est**, **tels sont** and **telles sont** sum up what came before. *Tel* agrees with the noun that follows. In literary narrative, you'll also read it with the passé simple, for recognition: *tel fut son sort* (such was his fate).

<Example fr="Telle est la raison de notre choix." en="That is the reason for our choice. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Il ne revint jamais. Tel fut son sort." en="He never came back. Such was his fate. (literary)" />

You already know *toujours est-il que* (the fact remains that) from <LessonLink id="c1-stylistic-inversion" text="the lesson on stylistic inversion" />. It works the same way: it puts the main point at the front of a formal sentence.

## Choosing the emphasis

| Speech | Standard writing | Formal writing |
|---|---|---|
| *Ce film, je l'ai adoré.* | *C'est ce film que j'ai adoré.* | *Rares sont les films qui m'ont autant touché.* |
| *Des gens comme lui, il y en a peu.* | *Il y a peu de gens comme lui.* | *Rares sont ceux qui lui ressemblent.* |
| *C'est ça, la raison.* | *Voilà la raison.* | *Telle est la raison.* |

In the first row, the formal sentence has the same emphasis but says something different: formal writing has no exact equivalent of *ce film, je l'ai adoré*.

As a rule of thumb, match the emphasis to the register of the rest of your text. A fronted *rares sont ceux qui* in a text message sounds stiff, and a dislocation in a formal letter sounds careless.

## Words for a show

<VocabTable group="words" />

*Le comédien* is an actor on stage, not a comedian. *La critique* is both a review and criticism in general. *Bouleverser* means to move someone deeply or to upset.

<Example fr="Les comédiens ont joué devant une salle pleine." en="The actors played to a full house." />
<Example fr="La critique a été très dure avec le metteur en scène." en="The review was very harsh on the director." />

## The whole list

- Left dislocation: *le thème* (the topic) first, then a pronoun (*le, lui, y, en, il*…): *ce film, je l'ai adoré*.
- *Ça* for a general idea: *le chocolat, j'adore ça*.
- Right dislocation: pronoun first, *le thème* at the end: *je l'ai adoré, ce film*.
- *Moi, je…*: contrast with others.
- All of these are spoken or informal.
- Formal fronting: *rares* or *nombreux sont ceux qui*, *reste à savoir si*, *reste la question de*, *telle est la raison*.

## En français

Malik and Clara are leaving the theatre. Clara writes reviews for a local newspaper.

- *Alors, la pièce, tu l'as aimée ?* (So, did you like the play? (spoken))
- *Moi, je l'ai adorée. Les comédiens, ils étaient très bons.* (I loved it. The actors were very good. (spoken))
- *Le décor, je l'ai trouvé un peu triste.* (I found the set a bit gloomy. (spoken))
- *Tu trouves ? Moi, il m'a beaucoup plu, ce décor.* (Do you think so? I liked that set a lot. (spoken))
- *Et la fin, tu en penses quoi ?* (And what do you think of the ending? (spoken))
- *Elle m'a bouleversée. Je vais écrire une critique pour le journal.* (It moved me deeply. I'm going to write a review for the paper.)
- *Tu vas écrire comme tu parles ?* (Are you going to write the way you speak?)
- *Non ! Écoute : « Rares sont les pièces aussi émouvantes. »* (No! Listen: "Few plays are so moving.")
- *C'est plus joli que « des pièces aussi émouvantes, il y en a peu ».* (That sounds nicer than "plays as moving as that, there aren't many". (spoken))
- *Et à la fin : « Telle est la force de cette pièce : elle vous suit longtemps. »* (And at the end: "Such is the power of this play: it stays with you for a long time.")
- *Et le décor ?* (And the set?)
- *« Reste la question du décor : tout le monde ne l'aimera pas. »* ("There remains the question of the set: not everyone will like it.")
- *Ça, c'est pour moi !* (That one's for me! (spoken))
- *Exactement. Ton avis, je l'ai noté.* (Exactly. I've noted your opinion. (spoken))

<Callout type="warning" title="Ce roman, je l'ai adoré, not je lui ai adoré">
  Learners often pick the pronoun by the person or thing, not by the verb. The pronoun is the one the verb needs: *adorer* takes a direct object, so **ce roman, je l'ai adoré**; *écrire à* takes *lui*, so **ton frère, je lui ai écrit**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Hunt for the comma">
  When you reread a formal text you've written, look for a comma followed by a pronoun near the start of a sentence (*Ce point, il…*, *Le budget, nous l'…*). Each one is probably a dislocation from your speaking habits. Rewrite it in the plain order or with *c'est… que*.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La dislocation est une façon de mettre un mot en relief à l'oral. On met le thème de la phrase au début, puis on le reprend avec le pronom dont le verbe a besoin : ce film, je l'ai adoré ; ton frère, je lui ai écrit ; ce projet, j'y pense ; du café, j'en bois trop. Pour une idée générale, on dit souvent ça : le chocolat, j'adore ça. On peut aussi mettre le mot à la fin : je l'ai adoré, ce film. Moi, je… montre une différence avec les autres. À l'écrit soutenu, on évite la dislocation. On met plutôt un mot en tête sans pronom : rares sont ceux qui, nombreux sont ceux qui, reste à savoir si, reste la question de, telle est la raison. Tel s'accorde avec le nom qui suit. Il faut donc choisir la mise en relief selon le registre du texte.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to move *seulement*, *même* and *aussi* to change what a sentence means, use sentence adverbs to comment on what you say, and place long adverbs in compound tenses.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="a2-adverbs-ment" text="the lesson on adverbs" />, you placed short adverbs between the auxiliary and the participle (*j'ai bien mangé*) and most *-ment* adverbs after it, as a guide. You know *seulement* from <LessonLink id="b1-ne-que-ni-ni" text="the lesson on ne… que and ni… ni" />, and *aussi* with inversion (therefore) from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />, which <LessonLink id="c1-stylistic-inversion" text="the lesson on stylistic inversion" /> came back to. This lesson is about how the position of an adverb can change its meaning.

## Seulement: what it restricts

<VocabTable group="position" />

*Seulement* restricts the word it stands next to, usually the one right after it. Put it where the restriction belongs.

<Example fr="Il travaille seulement le matin." en="He works only in the morning." />
<Example fr="Il a parlé seulement à Marie." en="He spoke only to Marie (to nobody else)." />

Between the auxiliary and the participle, *seulement* can restrict what follows it or the action itself. *Il a seulement lu le résumé* usually means he read the summary and not the book. Said with stress on *lu*, it can mean that he only read it and did nothing more with it. In speech, the voice shows what's restricted; in writing, put *seulement* right before the word you mean, or use *ne… que*: *il n'a lu que le résumé*.

At the start of a sentence, followed by a comma, *seulement* changes meaning: it introduces a drawback, like "the only thing is" or "but".

<Example fr="Le restaurant est très bon. Seulement, il est cher." en="The restaurant is very good. The only thing is, it's expensive." />

## Même: even, or itself

*Même* has two meanings, and its position tells them apart.

- **Before a noun or a pronoun**, *même* means "even": *même Paul a ri* (even Paul laughed).
- **After a stressed pronoun**, joined with a hyphen, it means "himself, herself": *lui-même*, *elle-même*. In the plural, *même* agrees: *nous-mêmes*, *eux-mêmes*.
- **After a noun**, it means "very" or "itself": *le jour même* (that very day).

<Example fr="Même les enfants ont compris." en="Even the children understood." />
<Example fr="Le directeur lui-même est venu nous voir." en="The director himself came to see us." />
<Example fr="Il a reçu la lettre et il est parti le jour même." en="He got the letter and left that very day." />

Compare *le même jour* (the same day), where *même* is an adjective before the noun. After the verb, *même* means "even" and stresses the action: *il a même ri* (he even laughed). So *même Paul a ri* and *Paul a même ri* don't say the same thing: in the first, Paul is the surprise; in the second, the laughing is.

## Aussi: also, or therefore

Inside the sentence, *aussi* means "also": *nous sommes aussi restés à la maison* (we also stayed at home). At the start of a sentence, followed by inversion, it means "therefore" in formal writing.

<Example fr="Il pleuvait. Aussi sommes-nous restés à la maison." en="It was raining. We therefore stayed at home. (formal)" />

In everyday French, say *donc* or *alors* for "therefore", and keep *aussi* for "also".

## Sentence adverbs

<VocabTable group="sentence" />

Some adverbs don't describe how something is done: they comment on the whole sentence. This is *un adverbe de phrase* (a sentence adverb). You know *heureusement* from <LessonLink id="a2-adverbs-ment" text="the lesson on adverbs" />, *franchement* from <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-opinions" text="the lesson on expressing opinions" />, and *apparemment* from <LessonLink id="b1-conditionnel-present" text="the lesson on the conditionnel présent" />.

A sentence adverb often goes at the start with a comma, after the conjugated verb, or at the end after a comma. The meaning stays the same.

<Example fr="Visiblement, elle était fatiguée." en="She was visibly tired." />
<Example fr="Elle était visiblement fatiguée." en="She was visibly tired." />

Some adverbs can be either kind, and the position helps you tell them apart. At the start with a comma, *franchement* and *sérieusement* comment on the sentence. After the verb, with no comma, they describe the manner.

<Example fr="Franchement, ce film est trop long." en="Frankly, this film is too long." />
<Example fr="Il nous a parlé franchement." en="He spoke to us frankly (in a frank way)." />
<Example fr="Sérieusement, tu pars demain ?" en="Seriously, you're leaving tomorrow?" />
<Example fr="Il fait son travail sérieusement." en="He does his work seriously." />

The four sentence adverbs below have different shades:

- **Visiblement** means "from what you can see": *il est visiblement content*.
- **Apparemment** means "from what people say or how it looks": *apparemment, la réunion est annulée*.
- **Décidément** reacts to something that has happened again: *décidément, tu es toujours en retard !*
- **Forcément** means "necessarily", and *pas forcément* means "not necessarily". Put *pas* first: *ce n'est pas forcément vrai*.

<Example fr="Un examen difficile n'est pas forcément un mauvais examen." en="A difficult exam isn't necessarily a bad exam." />

## Long adverbs in compound tenses

<VocabTable group="long" />

At A2, you put most *-ment* adverbs after the participle. At this level, you'll see that many of them can also go between the auxiliary and the participle. Treat what follows as tendencies, not laws.

- Adverbs of degree and completeness (*complètement*, *totalement*) and of time (*longuement*) usually go before the participle, and they can also follow it.
- Sentence adverbs (*visiblement*, *probablement*, *heureusement*) usually go before the participle.
- Adverbs of manner that say how the action was done (*attentivement*, *soigneusement*, *lentement*) more often go after it.

<Example fr="Il a complètement oublié la réunion." en="He completely forgot the meeting." />
<Example fr="J'y ai longuement réfléchi." en="I thought about it at length." />
<Example fr="Elle a probablement raté son train." en="She's probably missed her train." />
<Example fr="Relisez attentivement votre copie avant de la rendre." en="Reread your paper carefully before you hand it in." />

With an infinitive, a short adverb usually comes before it: *il faut bien relire*. A long adverb of manner usually follows: *il faut relire soigneusement chaque phrase*.

## The whole list

- Restriction: *seulement* before the word it restricts; *Seulement,* at the start: "the only thing is".
- Even or very: *même* + noun means even; noun + *même*, very; *lui-même*, himself; verb + *même*, even (the action).
- Also or therefore: *aussi* inside the sentence means also; *Aussi* + inversion at the start, therefore (formal).
- Sentence adverbs: at the start, after the verb, or at the end, with no change of meaning.
- Two kinds at once: *franchement* and *sérieusement* comment at the start, and give the manner after the verb.
- Long adverbs in compound tenses: degree, time and sentence adverbs usually before the participle; manner more often after it.

## En français

Lucas and Emma, two students, have just come out of an exam.

- *Alors, l'examen ? Tu as tout fini ?* (So, the exam? Did you finish everything?)
- *Non, j'ai seulement fini la première partie.* (No, I only finished the first part.)
- *Moi aussi ! Même Julie n'a pas eu le temps.* (Me too! Even Julie didn't have time.)
- *Julie ? Elle finit toujours en avance, pourtant.* (Julie? She always finishes early, though.)
- *Oui, mais aujourd'hui, elle était visiblement stressée.* (Yes, but today she was visibly stressed.)
- *Apparemment, le professeur va donner plus de temps la prochaine fois.* (Apparently, the teacher is going to give more time next time.)
- *Qui t'a dit ça ?* (Who told you that?)
- *Julie elle-même. Elle lui a parlé juste après l'examen.* (Julie herself. She spoke to him right after the exam.)
- *Décidément, elle pense à tout !* (She really does think of everything!)
- *Et toi, tu as relu tes réponses ?* (And you, did you read over your answers?)
- *J'ai seulement relu la fin, et pas très attentivement.* (I only read over the end, and not very carefully.)
- *Ce n'est pas forcément grave. On verra bien !* (That's not necessarily a problem. We'll see!)
- *Seulement, si mes notes sont mauvaises, mes parents vont être déçus.* (The only thing is, if my marks are bad, my parents will be disappointed.)
- *Tu as longuement révisé. Ça va aller.* (You revised for a long time. It'll be fine.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Pas forcément, not forcément pas">
  Learners often put *forcément* where English puts "necessarily". For "not necessarily", *pas* comes first: **ce n'est pas forcément vrai**. *Ce n'est forcément pas vrai* says something else: that it's necessarily untrue.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Move one word, read three sentences">
  Take one short sentence of your own and move *même* or *seulement* into every possible slot: *même Paul a ri*, *Paul a même ri*, *Paul lui-même a ri*. Write the English meaning next to each. Doing this with your own sentences fixes the positions faster than any table.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La place d'un adverbe peut changer le sens de la phrase. Seulement se met devant le mot qu'il limite : il a parlé seulement à Marie. Au début de la phrase, suivi d'une virgule, seulement présente un problème : le restaurant est bon. Seulement, il est cher. Même devant un nom montre une surprise : même Paul a ri. Après un pronom, lui-même ou eux-mêmes insiste sur la personne, et le jour même veut dire ce jour-là, pas un autre. Aussi au milieu de la phrase ajoute une idée, mais au début, avec l'inversion, il veut dire donc : aussi sommes-nous restés à la maison. Un adverbe de phrase, comme visiblement, décidément ou forcément, donne l'avis de la personne qui parle sur toute la phrase. Au passé composé, complètement et longuement se mettent souvent avant le participe, et attentivement plutôt après. Ce sont des habitudes, pas des règles fixes.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use verbs whose meaning changes with the preposition, choose between *de* and *par* after a passive, and use the right preposition with islands, overseas regions and cities that have an article.

## What you already know

You learned that verbs take *à* or *de* before an infinitive in <LessonLink id="a2-verbs-with-a-and-de" text="the lesson on verbs with à and de" />, and the prepositions with cities and countries (*à Nantes*, *en Italie*, *au Japon*, *aux États-Unis*) in <LessonLink id="a2-prepositions-with-places" text="the lesson on prepositions with places" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-passive-voice" text="the lesson on the passive voice" />, you saw *par* for the agent of an action and *de* for a state or a feeling (*entouré de*, *respecté de*). This lesson adds the cases where the choice changes the meaning.

## One verb, two meanings

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

**Manquer à** and **manquer de** are the classic trap. *Manquer de* means "to lack", which you know from <LessonLink id="b1-relative-dont" text="the lesson on dont" />: *il manque de temps* (he's short of time). *Manquer à* means "to be missed by", so the roles are the reverse of English. You know *tu me manques* (I miss you) from <LessonLink id="a2-writing-an-informal-email" text="the lesson on writing an informal email" />.

<Example fr="Paris manque beaucoup à Julie." en="Julie misses Paris a lot." />
<Example fr="Mes amis me manquent." en="I miss my friends." />
<Example fr="Cette équipe manque d'expérience." en="This team lacks experience." />

With no preposition, *manquer* + object means "to miss" in the sense of not catching: *il a manqué son train*.

**Penser à** means to think about, to have in mind; you met it with *y* in <LessonLink id="a2-pronoun-y" text="the lesson on the pronoun y" />. **Penser de** asks for or gives an opinion, mostly in questions and with *en*, as in *qu'est-ce que tu en penses ?* With *penser à*, a person stays after *à* as a stressed pronoun: *je pense à lui*, not *je lui pense*.

<Example fr="Je pense souvent à mes grands-parents." en="I often think about my grandparents." />
<Example fr="Que pensez-vous de ce projet ?" en="What do you think of this project?" />

**Croire à** and **croire en** both translate as "to believe in". As a rule of thumb, *croire à* is about whether something exists or is true, and *croire en* is about trust or faith in a person or a value. Usage overlaps with some nouns (*croire à l'avenir*, *croire en l'avenir*). *Croire quelqu'un*, with no preposition, means to believe what they say. With a thing and no preposition, *croire* means to believe it as it's told: *je ne crois pas son histoire* (I don't believe his story).

<Example fr="Il croit aux fantômes." en="He believes in ghosts." />
<Example fr="Je ne crois pas à son excuse." en="I don't believe his excuse." />
<Example fr="Ma directrice croit en moi." en="My manager believes in me." />

**Rêver de** is the everyday verb, for a wish or a night's dream: *je rêve de voyager*, *j'ai rêvé de toi*. **Rêver à** is literary: to muse on, to let your mind wander to something.

<Example fr="Assis devant la mer, il rêvait à son enfance." en="Sitting by the sea, he was musing on his childhood. (literary)" />

**Tenir à** means to care about, or to insist on, as you saw in <LessonLink id="b2-relative-prepositions" text="the lesson on relatives after prepositions" />. **Tenir de** means to take after a relative.

<Example fr="Je tiens beaucoup à cette maison." en="This house means a lot to me." />
<Example fr="Elle tient de sa mère : elle adore voyager." en="She takes after her mother: she loves travelling." />

**Jouer à** and **jouer de** you've known since A1: *jouer au tennis* for games and sports, *jouer du piano* for instruments. Remember the contractions: *jouer aux cartes*, *jouer de la guitare*.

## De or par after a passive

<VocabTable group="passive" />

The rule from B2 still holds: *par* for an agent that performs an action, *de* for a state, a position or a feeling. At this level, notice that some verbs take both, with a change of meaning.

<Example fr="La maison est entourée d'un grand jardin." en="The house is surrounded by a large garden." />
<Example fr="À six heures du matin, la maison a été entourée par la police." en="At six in the morning, the house was surrounded by the police." />
<Example fr="Le film sera suivi d'un débat." en="The film will be followed by a debate." />
<Example fr="Le suspect a été suivi par deux policiers." en="The suspect was followed by two police officers." />

In each pair, the first sentence describes how things are or come in order, and the second tells what someone did. With verbs of feeling (*aimé*, *respecté*), *de* is the more elegant choice, and *par* is also correct.

## Islands, overseas regions and cities with an article

<VocabTable group="places" />

You know the rule for countries: *en* before a feminine country or one that starts with a vowel, *au* before a masculine one, *aux* before a plural one. So masculine countries that start with a vowel take *en*: *en Iran*, *en Israël*.

Islands follow other habits. Take these as rules of thumb, and learn each name with its preposition.

- **An island whose name has no article** usually takes *à*, whatever its size: *à Cuba*, *à Madagascar*, *à Malte*.
- **A feminine name used with an article** (*la Corse*, *la Sicile*) usually takes *en*: *en Corse*, *en Sicile*.
- **An exception: La Réunion** keeps its article after *à*: *à La Réunion*.
- **Plural groups of islands** take *aux*: *aux Antilles*.

For Martinique and Guadeloupe, both *en Martinique* and *à la Martinique* are correct; *en* is the more usual today.

<Example fr="Elle a passé un an à Madagascar." en="She spent a year in Madagascar." />
<Example fr="Nous partons en Corse cet été." en="We're going to Corsica this summer." />
<Example fr="Mon frère habite à La Réunion." en="My brother lives on Réunion." />

Cities whose name has an article keep it, and *à* or *de* contracts with *le*: *au Havre*, *au Caire*, *du Havre*, *du Caire*. A feminine article doesn't contract: *à La Rochelle*.

To say where someone comes from, use the same contractions with *de*: *du Mexique*, *des Antilles*, *d'Iran*, *de Corse*, *de La Réunion*.

<Example fr="Il est rentré des Antilles la semaine dernière." en="He came back from the Antilles last week." />

## The whole list

- Missing and lacking: *manquer à* (to be missed by), *manquer de* (to lack), *manquer* + object (to miss a train).
- Thinking: *penser à* (to think about; *à lui*), *penser de* (an opinion; *en*).
- Believing: *croire à* (true, real), *croire en* (trust, faith), *croire quelqu'un* (believe what they say), *croire quelque chose* (believe it's true: *je ne crois pas son histoire*).
- Dreaming: *rêver de* (everyday), *rêver à* (literary).
- Holding: *tenir à* (to care about), *tenir de* (to take after).
- Passive: *par* for an action, *de* for a state or a feeling.
- Islands: *à Cuba*, *en Corse*, *à La Réunion*, *aux Antilles*; *en* or *à la Martinique*. Cities with *le*: *au Havre*, *du Caire*.

## En français

Sophie has just moved to Réunion for work. She's on a video call with her friend Karim, in Lyon.

- *Alors, la vie à La Réunion ?* (So, how's life on Réunion?)
- *C'est magnifique, mais Lyon me manque un peu.* (It's beautiful, but I miss Lyon a bit.)
- *Et nous, on ne te manque pas ?* (And what about us, don't you miss us? (spoken))
- *Bien sûr que si ! Je pense souvent à vous.* (Of course I do! I often think about you.)
- *Et ton nouveau travail, qu'est-ce que tu en penses ?* (And your new job, what do you think of it?)
- *Il me plaît. Ma directrice croit en moi, et ça aide.* (I like it. My manager believes in me, and that helps.)
- *Tu vas voyager un peu ? Madagascar n'est pas loin.* (Are you going to travel a bit? Madagascar isn't far.)
- *Oui, je rêve d'aller à Madagascar, et un jour à Cuba.* (Yes, I dream of going to Madagascar, and one day to Cuba.)
- *Toujours l'envie de partir ! Tu tiens de ton père.* (Always wanting to go somewhere! You take after your father.)
- *C'est vrai. Lui, il a habité en Martinique pendant dix ans.* (That's true. He lived in Martinique for ten years. (spoken))
- *Et ta sœur, elle vit toujours aux Antilles ?* (And your sister, does she still live in the Antilles? (spoken))
- *Non, elle est rentrée des Antilles l'année dernière. Elle vit au Havre maintenant.* (No, she came back from the Antilles last year. She lives in Le Havre now.)
- *Au Havre ? Elle doit manquer de soleil !* (In Le Havre? She must be short of sun!)
- *Un peu ! Mais elle joue du piano dans un groupe, et elle est heureuse.* (A bit! But she plays the piano in a band, and she's happy.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Tu me manques means I miss you">
  English speakers often build *manquer à* like "to miss" and swap the roles. The person or thing that's missed is the subject: to say "I miss you", write **tu me manques**, and for "Julie misses Paris", write **Paris manque à Julie**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn the place with its preposition">
  When you meet a new island, region or city, note it with its preposition and its "from" form, as one unit: *à Cuba, de Cuba*; *en Corse, de Corse*; *au Havre, du Havre*. You'll never have to work out the rule again for that place.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Certains verbes changent de sens avec la préposition. Manquer de veut dire ne pas avoir assez de quelque chose, mais manquer à veut dire que quelqu'un est triste sans une personne ou un lieu : Paris manque à Julie, tu me manques. Penser à, c'est avoir quelqu'un ou quelque chose en tête, et penser de donne un avis : que pensez-vous de ce projet ? Croire à porte sur ce qui est vrai, et croire en porte sur la confiance : il croit aux fantômes, elle croit en ses enfants. Sans préposition, on dit aussi : je ne crois pas son histoire. Rêver à est littéraire, et tenir de sa mère, c'est être comme sa mère. Après un passif, on met par pour une action et de pour un état. Avec les îles, on dit à Cuba, en Corse, à La Réunion et aux Antilles, et on dit en Martinique ou à la Martinique. Avec Le Havre ou Le Caire, on dit au Havre et du Caire.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to replace *faire*, *mettre*, *dire*, *avoir* and *il y a* with precise verbs, so that your formal writing says exactly what you mean.

## What you already know

You know precise reporting verbs such as *souligner* and *affirmer* from <LessonLink id="b2-reporting-verbs" text="the lesson on reporting verbs" />, and *mettre en œuvre* from <LessonLink id="b2-formal-report" text="the lesson on the formal report" />. You also know *mener une étude* from <LessonLink id="b2-science-and-research" text="the lesson on science and research" />. This lesson does the same work for the most common general verbs.

## Why precise verbs matter

In conversation, *faire*, *mettre*, *dire* and *avoir* do most of the work, and that's fine. In a report, an essay or a formal letter, a text full of them sounds vague and repetitive. A precise verb says more in fewer words: *effectuer un paiement* tells the reader exactly what kind of "doing" happened.

These verbs aren't more correct than the general ones. They belong to written and careful French. Use them where a reader expects care. *Faire* and *avoir* stay correct everywhere, and they're the natural choice in speech.

## Instead of faire

<VocabTable group="faire" />

*Effectuer* is the all-purpose formal replacement for *faire* with an action: a payment, a visit, works, a journey. *Procéder à* is more official, and it takes *à* + a noun: a check, a vote, an analysis. *Accomplir* stresses that a task was completed. *Élaborer* is for a plan or a project that takes thought and time.

<Example fr="Les travaux seront effectués en juin." en="The works will be carried out in June." />
<Example fr="L'équipe a procédé à un vote." en="The team proceeded to a vote." />
<Example fr="Elle a accompli son travail sans difficulté." en="She carried out her work without difficulty." />
<Example fr="Nous avons élaboré un plan sur trois ans." en="We've drawn up a three-year plan." />

## Instead of mettre

<VocabTable group="mettre" />

*Déposer* is to put something down or hand it in: a file, a parcel, a person at the station. *Instaurer* is to bring in a rule or a system, where speech would say *mettre en place*.

<Example fr="Vous pouvez déposer votre dossier à l'accueil." en="You can hand in your file at reception." />
<Example fr="La mairie a instauré une nouvelle règle pour les parkings." en="The town hall has brought in a new rule for car parks." />

## Instead of dire

<VocabTable group="dire" />

For reporting what someone said, use the verbs from B2: *affirmer*, *souligner*, *préciser*. Two more help when you write about a meeting or a text. *Aborder* is to bring up or deal with a subject, and it takes a direct object. *Formuler* is to put something into words, often a request or a criticism.

<Example fr="Lors de la réunion, nous avons abordé la question du budget." en="During the meeting, we dealt with the question of the budget." />
<Example fr="Les habitants ont formulé plusieurs demandes." en="The residents made several requests." />

## Instead of avoir

<VocabTable group="avoir" />

*Disposer de* means to have something available, ready to use; it always takes *de* in this meaning. *Posséder* is to own. *Comporter* is to consist of or include, and the container is the subject. *Ressentir* is to feel an emotion, where speech says *avoir peur* or *avoir de la peine*.

<Example fr="L'entreprise dispose de trois sites en France." en="The company has three sites in France." />
<Example fr="Sa famille possède une maison en Bretagne." en="Her family owns a house in Brittany." />
<Example fr="Le formulaire comporte deux pages." en="The form consists of two pages." />
<Example fr="Elle a ressenti une grande peur pendant la tempête." en="She felt very afraid during the storm." />

## Instead of il y a

<VocabTable group="ilya" />

*Il y a* is the everyday way to say "there is", and the precise alternatives depend on what you mean.

- **To say that something exists**: *il existe*, in writing.
- **To give a location**: *se trouver*.
- **To say that a name or a fact is on a list or in a document**: *figurer*.
- **To describe a mood or a silence**: *régner*.
- **To say that an event happens**: *se dérouler*. You know the everyday *avoir lieu* from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing your opinion" />.

<Example fr="Il existe plusieurs solutions à ce problème." en="There are several solutions to this problem." />
<Example fr="La gare se trouve près du centre." en="The station is near the centre." />
<Example fr="Votre nom ne figure pas sur la liste." en="Your name isn't on the list." />
<Example fr="Un lourd silence régnait dans la salle." en="A heavy silence filled the room." />
<Example fr="La conférence s'est déroulée dans le calme." en="The conference went off calmly." />

## Cause and effect

<VocabTable group="cause" />

Instead of *faire* or *créer* for a consequence, use a verb that shows what kind. *Susciter* is for a reaction: interest, criticism, worry. *Engendrer* and *entraîner* are for a consequence; both are common in reports. You already know *provoquer* from <LessonLink id="b1-cause-and-consequence" text="the lesson on cause and consequence" />.

<Example fr="Cette décision a suscité de nombreuses critiques." en="This decision gave rise to a lot of criticism." />
<Example fr="Ce retard a engendré des problèmes pour toute l'équipe." en="This delay caused problems for the whole team." />
<Example fr="La hausse des prix entraîne des difficultés pour les familles." en="The rise in prices leads to difficulties for families." />

## Don't overdo it

One precise verb per sentence is usually enough. If every verb is rare and long, the text becomes heavy, and the reader notices the effort instead of the meaning. As a rule of thumb, replace a general verb when the precise one adds information, not just to sound formal.

## The whole list

| General | Precise (written) |
|---|---|
| *faire un paiement, une visite* | *effectuer* |
| *faire une vérification* | *procéder à* |
| *faire un plan* | *élaborer* |
| *mettre en place une règle* | *instaurer* |
| *apporter, laisser un dossier* | *déposer* |
| *parler d'un sujet* | *aborder* |
| *avoir (à sa disposition)* | *disposer de* |
| *il y a trois parties dans* | *comporter* |
| *il y a* | *il existe, se trouver, figurer, régner, se dérouler* |
| *faire, créer (une réaction)* | *susciter, engendrer, entraîner* |

## En français

Thomas, an intern, has written his first report. His manager, Madame Dubois, goes through it with him.

- *Thomas, j'ai lu votre rapport. Il est clair, mais les verbes sont trop simples.* (Thomas, I've read your report. It's clear, but the verbs are too plain.)
- *Trop simples ? Qu'est-ce que je dois changer ?* (Too plain? What should I change?)
- *Vous écrivez « on a fait des travaux ». Écrivez plutôt « nous avons effectué des travaux ».* (You write "on a fait des travaux". Write "nous avons effectué des travaux" instead.)
- *D'accord. Et « il y a trois parties dans le rapport » ?* (OK. And "il y a trois parties dans le rapport"?)
- *« Le rapport comporte trois parties. » C'est plus précis.* ("Le rapport comporte trois parties." It's more precise.)
- *Et ici : « on a parlé du budget pendant la réunion » ?* (And here: "on a parlé du budget pendant la réunion"?)
- *« Nous avons abordé le budget lors de la réunion. »* ("Nous avons abordé le budget lors de la réunion.")
- *Et « le projet a créé beaucoup d'intérêt » ?* (And "le projet a créé beaucoup d'intérêt"?)
- *Écrivez « le projet a suscité un grand intérêt ».* (Write "le projet a suscité un grand intérêt".)
- *Mais à l'oral, je peux garder « faire » et « avoir » ?* (But when I speak, can I keep "faire" and "avoir"?)
- *Bien sûr. Ces verbes précis sont surtout pour l'écrit.* (Of course. These precise verbs are mainly for writing.)
- *Et si je mets un verbe précis dans chaque phrase ?* (And what if I put a precise verb in every sentence?)
- *Alors le texte devient lourd. Gardez-les là où ils apportent quelque chose.* (Then the text becomes heavy. Keep them where they add something.)
- *Merci. Je vous envoie la nouvelle version demain.* (Thank you. I'll send you the new version tomorrow.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Disposer de, not disposer">
  Learners often drop the *de* because "to have" takes a direct object. In the meaning "to have available", *disposer* needs **de**: **nous disposons de deux salles**, **l'entreprise dispose d'un budget important**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Circle the general verbs">
  When you revise a formal text, circle every *faire*, *mettre*, *avoir* and *il y a*. Then replace about one in three, starting with the ones that hide the most information. You'll improve the text without making it heavy.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Dans un texte écrit soigné, on peut remplacer les verbes généraux par des verbes précis. Au lieu de faire, on dit effectuer un paiement ou une visite, procéder à une vérification, accomplir un travail ou élaborer un plan. Au lieu de mettre, on dit déposer un dossier ou instaurer une règle. Pour parler d'un sujet, on l'aborde, et on formule une demande. Au lieu d'avoir, on dit disposer de, posséder, comporter ou ressentir. Au lieu d'il y a, on dit il existe, se trouver, figurer, régner ou se dérouler, selon le sens. Pour une conséquence, on dit susciter, engendrer ou entraîner. Ces verbes ne sont pas meilleurs que faire ou avoir : ils sont plus précis. Un seul verbe précis par phrase suffit souvent.

## What you already know

You know several pairs already. *Une hypothèse*, *mener une étude* and *tirer une conclusion* come from <LessonLink id="b2-science-and-research" text="the lesson on science and research" />, and *prendre une décision* from <LessonLink id="b1-review-hypotheses" text="the review of hypotheses" />. You met *porter plainte* in <LessonLink id="b1-making-a-complaint" text="the lesson on making a complaint" />, and *susciter l'intérêt* in <LessonLink id="c1-precise-verbs" text="the lesson on precise verbs" />.

## What a collocation is

<VocabTable group="term" />

*Une collocation* is a pair of words that naturally go together. Nothing in grammar explains it: you simply don't say *faire une décision* in French, even though English says "make a decision". Collocations are why a sentence can be correct and still sound foreign.

<Example fr="Cette phrase est correcte, mais la collocation est mauvaise." en="This sentence is correct, but the collocation is wrong." />

The safest strategy is to learn the noun with its verb or adjective, as one unit.

## Verb and noun

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

Many English "make", "take" and "have" phrases have their own French verb.

- **A hypothesis is put forward**: *émettre une hypothèse*. *Faire une hypothèse* is also heard.
- **A mistake is committed**: *commettre une erreur* in careful French; *faire une erreur* is fine in everyday French.
- **A part is played**: *jouer un rôle dans*.
- **Something is taken into account**: *prendre en compte* (+ direct object) or *tenir compte de*.
- **Importance is given**: *accorder de l'importance à*.
- **Stock is taken**: *dresser un bilan*; *faire le bilan* is the everyday version.
- **A challenge is met**: *relever un défi*.
- **A project is launched**: *lancer un projet*.

<Example fr="Les chercheurs ont émis une nouvelle hypothèse." en="The researchers put forward a new hypothesis." />
<Example fr="Nous avons tenu compte de toutes vos remarques." en="We've taken account of all your comments." />
<Example fr="La mairie accorde beaucoup d'importance à la sécurité." en="The town hall attaches great importance to safety." />
<Example fr="À la fin de l'année, le directeur dresse un bilan." en="At the end of the year, the director takes stock." />
<Example fr="L'association a lancé un projet pour les jeunes." en="The association has launched a project for young people." />

Watch the constructions: *prendre en compte vos remarques* has no preposition, but *tenir compte de vos remarques* and *jouer un rôle dans le projet* do.

## Adjective and noun

<VocabTable group="adjectives" />

French often intensifies a noun with a set adjective where English uses "big", "strong" or "deep". *Vif* and *vive* go with feelings and interest. *Lourd* goes with a responsibility or a consequence. *Large* goes with a majority. *Profond* goes with a disagreement or respect. *Clé*, after a noun, means "key".

<Example fr="Les habitants ont exprimé une vive inquiétude." en="The residents expressed deep concern." />
<Example fr="Elle porte une lourde responsabilité." en="She bears a heavy responsibility." />
<Example fr="Il existe un profond désaccord entre les deux partis." en="There is a deep disagreement between the two parties." />

Swapping the adjective changes the tone or sounds wrong: *une grande inquiétude* is fine but plainer, while *une lourde inquiétude* isn't a usual pairing. As a rule of thumb, when you're not sure of the adjective, use the plain one (*grand*, *important*) rather than guess.

## Adverb and adjective

<VocabTable group="adverbs" />

A few adverbs go with very few adjectives. *Grièvement* goes mostly with *blessé* (also *brûlé*, burnt); for illness, say *gravement malade*. *Parfaitement* goes naturally with *conscient*.

<Example fr="Mon voisin a été grièvement blessé." en="My neighbour was seriously injured." />
<Example fr="Je suis parfaitement conscient du problème." en="I'm fully aware of the problem." />

## Everyday and careful pairs

Several nouns have two partners: an everyday verb and a more careful one. Both are correct, but they don't sit in the same texts. In a conversation or a quick email, the everyday pair is natural. In a report, an essay or a formal letter, the careful pair shows control of the language.

| Everyday | Careful |
|---|---|
| *faire une erreur* | *commettre une erreur* |
| *faire une hypothèse* | *émettre une hypothèse* |
| *faire le bilan* | *dresser un bilan* |
| *faire attention à une remarque* | *tenir compte d'une remarque* |

<Example fr="Le rapport dresse un bilan de la première année." en="The report takes stock of the first year." />
<Example fr="Pendant la réunion, j'ai fait le bilan avec mon équipe." en="During the meeting, I took stock with my team." />

Don't mix the two levels in one sentence: a careful verb next to a familiar word sounds odd.

## How to learn them

Collocations are best learned from reading, not from rules. When you read a French article, note the pairs, not the single words: write *relever un défi*, not just *défi*. Group them by noun, so that each noun brings its verb and its adjective with it.

## The whole list

| English | French collocation |
|---|---|
| put forward a hypothesis | *émettre une hypothèse* |
| make a mistake | *commettre une erreur* (*faire une erreur*) |
| play a part in | *jouer un rôle dans* |
| take into account | *prendre en compte*, *tenir compte de* |
| attach importance to | *accorder de l'importance à* |
| take stock | *dresser un bilan* (*faire le bilan*) |
| rise to a challenge | *relever un défi* |
| deep concern, keen interest | *une vive inquiétude*, *un vif intérêt* |
| a heavy responsibility, a large majority | *une lourde responsabilité*, *une large majorité* |

## En français

Léa, a journalist, has written an article about a project of the town hall. Her editor, Hugo, reads it with her.

- *Léa, ton article est bon, mais certaines phrases sont trop proches de l'anglais.* (Léa, your article is good, but some sentences are too close to English.)
- *Lesquelles ?* (Which ones?)
- *Ici : « le maire a pris un rôle important ». On dit « il a joué un rôle clé ».* (Here: "le maire a pris un rôle important". We say "il a joué un rôle clé".)
- *D'accord. Et « les habitants ont une grande inquiétude » ?* (OK. And "les habitants ont une grande inquiétude"?)
- *C'est correct, mais « les habitants ont exprimé une vive inquiétude » est plus fort.* (That's correct, but "les habitants ont exprimé une vive inquiétude" is stronger.)
- *Et « nous avons fait une erreur dans nos chiffres » ?* (And "nous avons fait une erreur dans nos chiffres"?)
- *Ça va aussi. À l'écrit, « commettre une erreur » est plus soigné.* (That's fine too. In writing, "commettre une erreur" is more careful.)
- *J'ai aussi écrit : « la mairie va faire attention aux remarques des habitants ».* (I also wrote: "la mairie va faire attention aux remarques des habitants".)
- *Écris plutôt « la mairie va tenir compte des remarques des habitants ».* (Write "la mairie va tenir compte des remarques des habitants" instead.)
- *Le maire a aussi parlé d'un grand défi pour la ville.* (The mayor also spoke of a big challenge for the town.)
- *Alors termine avec une question : « La ville saura-t-elle relever ce défi ? »* (Then end with a question: "Will the town be able to rise to this challenge?")
- *J'aime bien. Et pour le vote ?* (I like it. And for the vote?)
- *« Le projet a été adopté à une large majorité. »* ("The project was passed by a large majority.")
- *Merci. Je vais noter ces expressions.* (Thanks. I'll write these expressions down.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Prendre une décision, not faire une décision">
  English speakers often translate "make a decision" word for word. French takes a decision: **prendre une décision**. In the same way, French says **prendre rendez-vous** and **poser une question**, where English says "make" and "ask".
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="One noun, two partners">
  When you meet an important noun, find two words that often go with it: a verb and an adjective. For *défi*: *relever un défi*, *un grand défi*. Writing them together on one line fixes them much better than writing the noun alone.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Une collocation est un groupe de mots qui vont souvent ensemble. La grammaire ne l'explique pas : il faut l'apprendre. Avec un nom, on choisit le bon verbe : on émet une hypothèse, on commet une erreur, on joue un rôle dans un projet, on prend en compte une remarque ou on en tient compte, on accorde de l'importance à quelque chose, on dresse un bilan, on relève un défi et on lance un projet. Certains adjectifs donnent plus de force au nom : une vive inquiétude, un vif intérêt, une lourde responsabilité, une large majorité, un profond désaccord, un rôle clé. Certains adverbes vont avec très peu d'adjectifs : grièvement blessé, parfaitement conscient. Pour bien les apprendre, il vaut mieux noter le nom avec son verbe et son adjectif.

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words help you talk about style itself. *La mise en valeur* is what emphasis does: it shows one idea to advantage. *Un effet de style* is any choice that gives a sentence a particular tone, such as an inversion or a fronted word. *La sobriété* is restraint, a style without too many effects; you know the adjective *sobre* from <LessonLink id="b2-describing-a-work-of-art" text="the lesson on describing a work of art" />. *Alourdir* is to make a text heavy.

<Example fr="L'inversion permet la mise en valeur de l'idée principale." en="Inversion lets you highlight the main idea." />
<Example fr="Chaque effet de style doit avoir une raison." en="Every stylistic effect needs a reason." />
<Example fr="Un bon éditorial garde une certaine sobriété." en="A good editorial keeps a certain restraint." />
<Example fr="Trop de verbes rares alourdissent le texte." en="Too many rare verbs weigh the text down." />

## From plain to polished

In this lesson, *un effet de style* means a word-order or negation device: fronting, inversion after *sans doute*, *aussi* or *à peine*, a literary negation, or the *ne explétif*. Precise verbs, collocations and the incise after a quotation (*nous a dit une commerçante*) don't count as effects: they're simply good word choice.

Polishing a text isn't about putting every effect of the unit into one paragraph. It's about choosing a few, in the right places. Work in this order.

1. **Replace the vague verbs.** Circle *faire*, *mettre*, *avoir* and *il y a*, and change those that hide information: *effectuer des travaux*, *le plan comporte trois étapes*.
2. **Fix the collocations.** Check each important noun with its verb and adjective: *dresser un bilan*, *une large majorité*.
3. **Put one idea in focus.** Use fronting or inversion once or twice, for the points that matter most: *rares sont ceux qui…*, *aussi la mairie doit-elle…*.
4. **Choose the register of the negations.** In a formal text, one *nul ne* or *ne… nullement* can replace a plain negation. Keep *ne… point* for literary imitation.
5. **Check the small words.** Is *seulement* or *même* next to the word it modifies? Does *manquer à* have the right subject? Does *disposer* have its *de*?

Then reread for *sobriété*. If two effects sit in the same sentence, remove one. As a rule of thumb, a paragraph of five sentences needs no more than two effects.

## One sentence, three steps

Here is how one plain sentence becomes formal, one step at a time. Each step changes a single thing.

<Example fr="Il y a beaucoup de gens qui ont peur pour les commerces." en="There are a lot of people who are worried about the shops." />
<Example fr="Nombreux sont ceux qui ont peur pour les commerces." en="Many people are worried about the shops. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Nombreux sont ceux qui expriment une vive inquiétude pour les commerces." en="Many people are expressing deep concern about the shops. (formal)" />

The first step replaces *il y a beaucoup de gens qui* with fronting. The second replaces *avoir peur* with a collocation. A third step, such as an inversion, would be too much for one sentence.

The same method works for a sentence about a decision:

<Example fr="La mairie n'a pas du tout pensé aux familles." en="The town hall didn't think about families at all." />
<Example fr="La mairie n'a nullement tenu compte des familles." en="The town hall took no account of families whatsoever. (formal)" />

Here, *nullement* replaces *pas du tout*, and *tenir compte de* replaces *penser à*. Two changes are enough.

## A model editorial

This is an original editorial about an invented town, Saint-Aubin, written for this lesson.

*Saint-Aubin, une ville sans voitures ?*

*Depuis le 1er mars, les voitures ne peuvent plus entrer dans le centre de Saint-Aubin. Le débat a duré plus d'un an. La mesure, instaurée par la mairie, a suscité une vive inquiétude chez les commerçants. Rares sont ceux qui étaient d'accord au début. « Le centre, on ne le reconnaît plus ! », nous a dit une commerçante.*

*Il faut pourtant dresser un bilan honnête. Le premier mois s'est déroulé dans le calme, et les chiffres sont meilleurs que nous ne le pensions : la pollution a baissé, et les rues sont plus vivantes. Le samedi, les familles marchent dans les rues, et les enfants jouent sur la place. Même les commerçants le reconnaissent.*

*Sans doute la mairie a-t-elle commis des erreurs. Elle a peu tenu compte des personnes âgées, pour qui le bus reste difficile. Elle doit maintenant les écouter. Nul ne regrette pourtant la situation d'avant.*

*Reste la question des commerces. Le calme ne leur pose aucun problème. Ce sont les clients des villages voisins qui leur manquent : il n'existe pas de parking à l'entrée de la ville. Le samedi, ils faisaient leurs courses dans le centre ; aujourd'hui, ils vont ailleurs.*

*Telle est la difficulté : une ville plus agréable ne doit pas devenir une ville fermée. La mairie doit agir avant que les commerçants perdent patience. Aussi lui demandons-nous d'élaborer un plan pour les parkings. Ce plan jouera un rôle clé dans l'avenir de Saint-Aubin. La ville saura-t-elle relever ce défi ?*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Saint-Aubin, a town without cars? Since 1 March, cars can no longer enter the centre of Saint-Aubin. The debate lasted more than a year. The measure, brought in by the town hall, aroused deep concern among the shopkeepers. Few of them agreed at first. "You can't recognize the centre any more!", one shopkeeper told us (spoken). We must nevertheless take stock honestly. The first month went off calmly, and the figures are better than we thought: pollution has fallen, and the streets are livelier. On Saturdays, families walk in the streets, and children play in the square. Even the shopkeepers admit it. No doubt the town hall made mistakes. It took little account of older people, for whom the bus is still difficult. It must now listen to them. Yet nobody misses the situation before. There remains the question of the shops. The quiet causes them no problem. What they miss are the customers from the neighbouring villages: there's no car park at the entrance to the town. On Saturdays, they used to do their shopping in the centre; today, they go elsewhere. Such is the difficulty: a more pleasant town mustn't become a closed town. The town hall must act before the shopkeepers lose patience. We therefore ask it to draw up a plan for car parks. This plan will play a key role in the future of Saint-Aubin. Will the town rise to this challenge?)

## How the text uses the unit

- **Precise verbs and collocations** (not counted as effects): *instaurée*, *a suscité une vive inquiétude*, *dresser un bilan*, *s'est déroulé*, *il n'existe pas de parking*, *élaborer un plan*, *jouera un rôle clé*, *relever ce défi*.
- **Fronting**: *rares sont ceux qui* (paragraph 1), *reste la question des commerces* (paragraph 4), *telle est la difficulté* (paragraph 5).
- **Inversion**: *sans doute la mairie a-t-elle commis* (paragraph 3), *aussi lui demandons-nous* (paragraph 5). The incise *nous a dit une commerçante* and the closing question *la ville saura-t-elle…* are ordinary formal word order, not counted as effects.
- **Literary negation**: *nul ne regrette* (paragraph 3).
- **The ne explétif**: *meilleurs que nous ne le pensions* (paragraph 2).
- **Adverbs and prepositions**: *même les commerçants*, and *manquer à* with the clients as the subject.
- **Dislocation** appears only once, inside a quotation, because it's spoken French.

Notice what the text doesn't do. Each paragraph has one or two effects, never more, and the plainest sentences (*la pollution a baissé*) carry the facts. That restraint is *la sobriété*.

## Check your work

- Did you replace only the general verbs that hide information?
- Is each collocation right (*dresser un bilan* rather than *faire le bilan* in formal writing)?
- After *rares sont ceux qui* or *nombreux sont ceux qui*, is the verb plural?
- After *aussi* with inversion, did you keep a noun subject first and add a pronoun (*aussi la mairie doit-elle*)?
- Is there a *pas* after the *ne explétif* by mistake?
- Are there two plain sentences left, so that the effects stand out?

<Callout type="warning" title="Aussi la mairie doit-elle, not aussi doit la mairie">
  With a noun subject, learners often swap the noun and the verb after *aussi*. Keep the noun first and add a pronoun after the verb: **aussi la mairie doit-elle agir**. With a pronoun subject, simply invert: **aussi devons-nous agir**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read it aloud as a listener">
  When you've polished a text, read it aloud once, as if you were hearing it on the radio. Wherever you run out of breath or lose the thread, an effect is in the way: remove it or split the sentence. Good formal French still sounds natural when read aloud.
</Callout>
