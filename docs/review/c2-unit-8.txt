## Pastiche, parody, plagiarism

<VocabTable group="kinds" />

Their real test is what the writer does with the model's text: write new sentences in its style, or copy it.

- **Un pastiche** imitates a style: its words, its syntax, its rhythm. It's written *à la manière de* (in the style of) an author or a genre, in sentences of its own. It can be a game or a way to *rendre hommage à* (pay tribute to) a writer.
- **Une parodie** imitates in order to mock: it *parodie* (parodies) its model by exaggerating its features until they become funny.
- **Le plagiat** (plagiarism) is something else: it copies another's text and presents it as one's own, without saying so. To *plagier* is dishonest, whether the copy is long or short.

The difference from plagiarism is the text, not the label: a pastiche or a parody writes new sentences in a style, while plagiarism takes another's. Naming the model is still good practice.

Two famous books show how far the game can go. In *Pastiches et mélanges* (1919), Proust included pastiches of other writers. In *Exercices de style* (1947), Queneau told one tiny incident again and again, each time in a different style: *un exercice de style* (a stylistic exercise) taken to its limit.

<Example fr="Un pastiche imite le style d'un auteur ; une parodie s'en moque ; le plagiat copie son texte." en="A pastiche imitates an author's style; a parody mocks it; plagiarism copies their text." />

## Finding the markers of a style

<VocabTable group="markers" />

To imitate a style, first list its *marques de style* (stylistic markers): what makes a text recognizable before you know who wrote it. Look at four things:

- **Vocabulary**: the words a style likes (*il appartient à*, *a-t-on appris*).
- **Syntax**: long or short sentences, impersonal turns, inversions.
- **Rhythm**: the length of the paragraphs, *la concision* (brevity), repetitions.
- **Tense and voice**: the passé simple of a tale, the passé composé and the conditionnel de prudence of a wire, the passive of a notice.

Each style also has its *tics d'écriture* (mannerisms), such as a formula that comes back. A pastiche reproduces them with a light hand. A parody *force le trait* (exaggerates them) until the reader laughs.

<Example fr="La dépêche aime la concision : une phrase, un fait." en="The wire likes brevity: one sentence, one fact." />

<Example fr="Chaque genre a ses tics d'écriture : la dépêche commence par le fait, l'avis par une formule impersonnelle." en="Each genre has its mannerisms: the wire begins with the fact, the notice with an impersonal formula." />

<Example fr="Cette parodie force le trait : chaque phrase de l'avis commence par « Il est rappelé que »." en="This parody overdoes it: every sentence of the notice begins with 'Il est rappelé que'." />

## The fable à la manière de La Fontaine

La Fontaine, who died in 1695, is on the safe list, so his fables can be quoted. His *Le Lièvre et la Tortue* opens with its moral, before the story:

<Example fr="« Rien ne sert de courir ; il faut partir à point » (La Fontaine, Fables, « Le Lièvre et la Tortue »)" en="&quot;There's no point running; you have to set off at the right time.&quot; (our translation)" />

A prose pastiche of a fable keeps a few of his *traits de style* (features of style): animals that act like people, written with a capital letter (*le Chat*); a short story with a sudden turn; a moment of dialogue; the passé simple for the events; and a moral in one line, here at the end. The pastiche in this lesson is in prose, so it makes no claim about verse or metre.

## The dépêche and the notice

The other two pastiches imitate genres rather than an author. The dépêche gives the fact first, then the place and the day, and *cite* (quotes) its source in an inverted incise (*a-t-on appris auprès de la mairie*); it uses the conditionnel de prudence for what it hasn't checked, and ends with a formula such as *aucun blessé n'est à déplorer*. The notice speaks impersonally (*il est porté à la connaissance de*), uses the passive, and ends with a reminder of everyone's duties.

## Imitating honestly

<VocabTable group="ethics" />

Labelling is good practice: a pastiche *signale* (points out) what it imitates. As a rule of thumb:

- **Label it**: *à la manière de…*, *pastiche écrit pour…*
- **Don't copy sentences**: imitate the markers, write your own sentences. A copied line must be quoted, in quotation marks, with its source.
- ***Citez vos sources*** (name your sources) whenever you borrow a fact or a phrase.
- **Keep the facts** when you tell the same event in several styles: only the style changes.

<Example fr="Une imitation honnête signale son modèle et cite ses sources." en="An honest imitation points out its model and names its sources." />

## The model: one event, three styles

The texts below tell the same invented event, an afternoon at the primary school of Valbrune. The first three are original pastiches, written for the lesson; the fourth is a short parody of the third.

*À la manière d'une fable de La Fontaine (en prose). Un Chat, monté sur le toit de l'école, ne savait plus descendre. Tout l'après-midi, les Enfants criaient, les Parents criaient, et le Maire criait plus fort que tous. « Descends ! » lui criait-on. « Pourquoi descendre, pensa le Chat, puisque tout le monde crie en bas ? » Et, effrayé par tant de bruit, il montait toujours plus haut. Survint une vieille Dame, qui ne dit rien. Elle posa une assiette de lait au pied du mur, et s'assit. Le Chat la regarda longtemps, puis il descendit. Rien ne sert de crier ; il faut savoir se taire.*

*À la manière d'une dépêche. VALBRUNE, 14 mai (agence inventée). Un chat est resté bloqué, mardi après-midi, sur le toit de l'école primaire de Valbrune, a-t-on appris auprès de la mairie. Selon des témoins, de nombreux parents et le maire se trouvaient devant le bâtiment. L'animal serait redescendu en fin d'après-midi, après qu'une habitante a déposé une assiette de lait au pied du mur. L'incident a duré tout l'après-midi, selon les mêmes témoins. Aucun blessé n'est à déplorer.*

*À la manière d'un avis administratif. Mairie de Valbrune. Avis aux parents d'élèves. Objet : incident du mardi 14 mai. Il est porté à la connaissance des familles qu'un chat s'est trouvé bloqué sur le toit de l'école primaire au cours de l'après-midi du mardi 14 mai. L'animal a regagné le sol en fin d'après-midi, après qu'une habitante a déposé de la nourriture au pied du bâtiment. Il est rappelé qu'il appartient à chacun de ne pas se rassembler devant l'établissement en cas d'incident. Les services de la mairie remercient les familles de leur compréhension.*

*À la manière d'une parodie de l'avis. Il est porté à la connaissance des familles qu'il est rappelé qu'un chat s'est trouvé, au cours de l'après-midi, en situation de présence non autorisée sur le toit de l'établissement. Il est rappelé, en outre, qu'il est rappelé qu'il appartient à chacun de ne pas se rassembler, ni crier, ni penser trop fort.*

(In the manner of a fable by La Fontaine (in prose). A Cat, who had climbed onto the school roof, no longer knew how to get down. All afternoon, the Children shouted, the Parents shouted, and the Mayor shouted louder than all of them. "Come down!" they shouted at him. "Why come down," thought the Cat, "when everyone is shouting down there?" And, frightened by so much noise, he kept climbing higher. Along came an old Lady, who said nothing. She put a plate of milk at the foot of the wall, and sat down. The Cat looked at her for a long time, then came down. There's no point shouting; you have to know how to keep quiet. / In the manner of a wire. VALBRUNE, 14 May (invented agency). A cat was stuck on Tuesday afternoon on the roof of the Valbrune primary school, the town hall said. According to witnesses, many parents and the mayor were in front of the building. The animal reportedly came back down late in the afternoon, after a local woman put a plate of milk at the foot of the wall. The incident lasted all afternoon, according to the same witnesses. No one was injured. / In the manner of an administrative notice. Valbrune town hall. Notice to parents. Subject: incident of Tuesday 14 May. Families are hereby informed that a cat was stuck on the roof of the primary school during the afternoon of Tuesday 14 May. The animal got back to the ground late in the afternoon, after a local woman put food at the foot of the building. Everyone is reminded that they must not gather in front of the school in case of an incident. The town hall's services thank families for their understanding. / In the manner of a parody of the notice. Families are hereby informed that it is recalled that a cat found itself, during the afternoon, in a situation of unauthorized presence on the roof of the school. It is recalled, moreover, that it is recalled that everyone must not gather, nor shout, nor think too loudly.)

## How the pastiches work

- **The same facts** in all three: a cat stuck on the school roof, in the afternoon, a crowd with the mayor, a woman who puts down food (a plate of milk), the cat coming down. The fable leaves out the date and the notice says *de la nourriture* rather than *du lait*, but none adds a fact.
- **The fable**: capitals for the characters, a moment of direct speech (*« Descends ! »*, then the Cat's thought), the passé simple for the events (*survint*, *posa*, *descendit*), a repetition (*criaient… criaient… criait*), a sudden turn and a moral that echoes La Fontaine's line without copying it.
- **The dépêche**: the fact and the place first, the source in an inverted incise, *selon des témoins*, the conditionnel de prudence (*serait redescendu*) and the closing formula.
- **The notice**: *objet*, an impersonal opening, the passive (*s'est trouvé bloqué*), no named person, and a reminder of duties (*il appartient à chacun de*).
- **The parody** forces the trait of the notice: *il est rappelé que* doubled, a heavy noun phrase (*en situation de présence non autorisée*), and a final list that turns absurd (*ni de penser trop fort*). It adds no fact; it only makes the style laugh at itself.
- **The labels**: each text says what it imitates, so none of them could pass for the original.

<Callout type="warning" title="A pastiche isn't a copy">
  When they write a pastiche, learners sometimes reuse whole lines of the model with one word changed: *Rien ne sert de courir ; il faut partir à point* becomes *Rien ne sert de courir ; il faut partir tôt*. That's a near copy, not an imitation. Write your own sentence with the same markers, **Rien ne sert de crier ; il faut savoir se taire**, and quote the original only in quotation marks, with its source.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write one fact in three styles">
  Take one ordinary event from your week and write it three times, in three short paragraphs: as a tale, as a news wire and as an official notice. Then underline, in each one, the two markers that make it recognizable.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to choose between idioms that look alike, use an idiom at the right strength and in the right register, recognize literary and negative idioms, and deal with idioms that don't translate.

## What you already know

You know everyday idioms such as *avoir du pain sur la planche* from <LessonLink id="b2-idioms-everyday" text="the lesson on everyday idioms" />, and *mettre de l'eau dans son vin* and *ne pas y aller par quatre chemins* from <LessonLink id="c1-advanced-idioms" text="the lesson on advanced idioms" />. You know *détourner une expression* from <LessonLink id="c2-culture-wordplay" text="the lesson on humour and wordplay" />. This lesson adds idioms that weren't taught before, chosen for the precision they demand.

**The register labels** follow the usual dictionary categories: familiar, everyday (unmarked) and formal. Where dictionaries differ, the label is marked "to verify" in docs/review-needed.md.

## Pairs that are easy to confuse

<VocabTable group="pairs" />

*Une expression figée* (a set phrase) doesn't change its words, but some set phrases look so alike that learners swap them.

- **Tenir à cœur / prendre à cœur.** With *tenir à cœur*, the thing that matters is the subject: *ce projet me tient à cœur* (this project matters to me). With *prendre à cœur*, the person is the subject: *elle prend son travail à cœur* (she takes her work seriously).
- **Faire long feu / ne pas faire long feu.** In its traditional sense, *faire long feu* means to fail, to come to nothing; it's formal and rare. The everyday form is negative: *ne pas faire long feu* (not to last long). Dictionaries also record *faire long feu* in the sense "not to last", so in your own writing, use the negative form for that meaning, which no reader can misread.
- **De but en blanc / à brûle-pourpoint.** Both mean abruptly, without any lead-in: *il m'a demandé de but en blanc si j'étais d'accord*. *À brûle-pourpoint* sounds a little more literary.
- **Jeter de l'huile sur le feu** (to make a conflict worse) is the opposite of *mettre de l'eau dans son vin*, which you know.

<Example fr="Ce projet me tient à cœur, et toute l'équipe le prend à cœur." en="This project matters a great deal to me, and the whole team takes it seriously." />

<Example fr="Au lieu de mettre de l'eau dans son vin, il a jeté de l'huile sur le feu." en="Instead of toning down his demands, he made the conflict worse." />

<Example fr="La nouvelle émission n'a pas fait long feu : elle s'est arrêtée au bout d'un mois." en="The new programme didn't last long: it stopped after a month." />

## The strength of an idiom

<VocabTable group="strength" />

An idiom has a strength, and choosing the right one is part of precision.

- **Surprise**: *s'étonner* is mild; *tomber des nues* (to be utterly astonished) is the strongest.
- **Joy**: *être content* is neutral; *être aux anges* (to be overjoyed) is strong.
- **Minimizing**: *il n'y a pas de quoi fouetter un chat* (familiar) says that something is no big deal.
- **Anger**: *prendre la mouche* (familiar) is to take offence over nothing; *monter sur ses grands chevaux* is to react with angry pride, often to defend one's dignity.

As a rule of thumb, a strong idiom used for a small event sounds ironic. *Il est tombé des nues* about a late bus suggests the speaker finds his surprise excessive.

<Example fr="Quand elle a appris la nouvelle, elle est tombée des nues." en="When she heard the news, she was utterly astonished." />

<Example fr="Quand on a critiqué son travail, il est monté sur ses grands chevaux." en="When his work was criticized, he reacted with angry pride." />

<Example fr="Il a pris la mouche pour une simple remarque, alors qu'il n'y avait pas de quoi fouetter un chat." en="He took offence at a simple remark, when it was no big deal." />

## Literary and old-fashioned idioms

<VocabTable group="literary" />

Some idioms belong to written, formal or slightly old-fashioned French. You'll meet them in novels and essays, and in the press when a writer wants a literary tone.

- **Avoir maille à partir avec** (to be in dispute with): *il a eu maille à partir avec ses voisins*.
- **Faire florès** (to be very successful): *ce mot a fait florès dans les journaux*.
- **Battre la campagne** (to ramble, to let one's mind wander): *l'orateur bat la campagne*.
- **Sur-le-champ** (immediately) is more common, and fits any careful text.

<Example fr="Le maire a eu maille à partir avec plusieurs associations." en="The mayor has been in dispute with several associations." />

<Example fr="Cette formule a fait florès, puis tout le monde l'a oubliée." en="This phrase was very successful, then everyone forgot it." />

## Idioms in the negative

<VocabTable group="negative" />

Some idioms are almost always used in the negative, and the positive form sounds odd:

- **Ne pas être sorti de l'auberge** (familiar): to be far from the end of one's troubles.
- **Ne pas avoir froid aux yeux** (familiar): to be fearless.
- **Ne pas faire long feu**, as above.

Keep the negation when you use them; *il a froid aux yeux* isn't the expected form.

<Example fr="Avec ce nouveau retard, nous ne sommes pas sortis de l'auberge." en="With this new delay, we're far from the end of our troubles." />

## Idioms and English

<VocabTable group="untranslatable" />

An idiom's image may or may not survive in English, and the four cases call for different care:

- **A twin**: *avoir plus d'une corde à son arc* (to have more than one resource) has a close British twin, *to have more than one string to one's bow*. The image survives, so a literal translation works in British English.
- **A near twin**: *couper l'herbe sous le pied* (to get in ahead of someone) is close to *to cut the ground from under someone's feet*, with ground for grass. Translate by the English idiom or by the meaning.
- **A false twin**: translated word for word, *mettre la puce à l'oreille* (to make someone suspicious) gives *to put a flea in someone's ear*, but the English *a flea in one's ear* is a sharp rebuke. Never translate it literally.
- **No twin**: *tirer son épingle du jeu* (to get out of a bad situation without loss) has no English idiom with the same image.

Where there's no twin, translate the meaning in plain words, as in this lesson's glosses. When you translate into French, don't look for an idiom at all costs: a plain sentence is better than an idiom at the wrong strength or in the wrong register.

<Example fr="Ce silence m'a mis la puce à l'oreille." en="This silence made me suspicious." />

<Example fr="Avec plus d'une corde à son arc, elle a tiré son épingle du jeu." en="With more than one resource to fall back on, she got out of the situation without loss." />

## En français

Ophélie and Thierry, two editors at a local magazine, argue over a headline. They use *tu*. A few everyday words appear here and in the exercises:

<VocabTable group="other" />

- *Pour le festival, j'ai écrit : « Le festival fait long feu ».* (For the festival, I wrote: "Le festival fait long feu".)
- *Tu veux dire qu'il n'a pas duré ? Alors écris « Le festival n'a pas fait long feu ».* (You mean it didn't last? Then write "Le festival n'a pas fait long feu".)
- *Les deux ne veulent pas dire la même chose ?* (Don't the two mean the same thing?)
- *Non. Sans la négation, beaucoup de lecteurs vont comprendre qu'il a échoué.* (No. Without the negation, many readers are going to understand that it failed.)
- *Il a duré deux soirs, et il a eu du public. Il n'a pas échoué.* (It lasted two evenings, and it had an audience. It didn't fail.)
- *Donc la forme négative. Et le sous-titre ?* (So the negative form. And the subtitle?)
- *« Les organisateurs tombent des nues ».* ("The organizers are utterly astonished".)
- *C'est trop fort. Ils sont surpris, pas plus.* (That's too strong. They're surprised, nothing more.)
- *Alors : « Les organisateurs s'étonnent ».* (Then: "Les organisateurs s'étonnent".)
- *Mieux. Et ne mets pas « il n'y a pas de quoi fouetter un chat » dans l'article : c'est trop familier pour la rubrique culture.* (Better. And don't put "il n'y a pas de quoi fouetter un chat" in the article: it's too familiar for the culture section.)
- *D'accord, mais j'ai le droit à une image ?* (All right, but am I allowed one image?)
- *Une seule : « La mairie a coupé l'herbe sous le pied des organisateurs ». C'est familier, mais c'est exactement ce qui s'est passé.* (Just one: "The town hall got in ahead of the organizers". It's familiar, but it's exactly what happened.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Keep the negation in a negative idiom">
  Learners often drop the negation of an idiom that lives in the negative, or add one to an idiom that doesn't: *le festival a fait long feu* for a festival that simply didn't last. For that meaning, write **le festival n'a pas fait long feu**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Note an idiom with its strength and register">
  When you note a new idiom, write three things beside it: its plain meaning, its register (familiar, everyday, formal) and its strength on a small scale from 1 to 3. When you need it later, you'll know at once whether it fits the text and the situation.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Une expression figée ne change pas ses mots, mais certaines expressions se ressemblent beaucoup. Avec tenir à cœur, la chose qui compte est le sujet : ce projet me tient à cœur. Avec prendre à cœur, la personne est le sujet : elle prend son travail à cœur. Ne pas faire long feu veut dire ne pas durer longtemps ; sans la négation, faire long feu veut dire, dans son sens traditionnel, échouer. De but en blanc et à brûle-pourpoint veulent dire brusquement. Une expression a aussi une force : tomber des nues est plus fort que s'étonner, et être aux anges plus fort qu'être content. Prendre la mouche et il n'y a pas de quoi fouetter un chat sont familiers. Avoir maille à partir avec, faire florès et battre la campagne, qui veut dire laisser ses pensées aller où elles veulent, appartiennent à un registre soutenu. Ne pas avoir froid aux yeux et ne pas être sorti de l'auberge s'emploient à la forme négative. Enfin, beaucoup d'expressions, comme mettre la puce à l'oreille, ne se traduisent pas mot à mot : on en traduit le sens.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to break the register of a text on purpose, for humour, emphasis or irony, signal the break so that the reader knows it's intended, and tell a chosen effect from a slip.

## What you already know

You know how to move a whole text up or down a register (*monter* or *descendre d'un registre*) from <LessonLink id="c1-register-shifting" text="the lesson on register shifting" />, and *l'ironie*, *au second degré* and *les guillemets* from <LessonLink id="c1-irony-and-understatement" text="the lesson on irony and understatement" />. You know *une rupture de registre* as a fault to revise from <LessonLink id="c2-revising-your-style" text="the lesson on revising your style" />. This lesson shows the other side: the same break, made on purpose.

**The column, the town council and the people in this lesson are invented.**

## The deliberate break

<VocabTable group="break" />

A word from another register *détonne* (clashes) with the text around it. That clash creates *un décalage* (a gap), and a gap can be a fault or an effect. When a writer creates it *à dessein* (on purpose), the result is *un effet de rupture*: the reader stops for a moment on the word, and the word means more than it would elsewhere.

The real test is not the word itself, but whether the break is *voulu* (intended) and whether the reader can see that it is. A familiar word in a formal text is a slip when nothing signals it and nothing in the text calls for it. It's an effect when the context or a signal shows that the writer chose it.

<Example fr="Dans ce rapport soutenu, le mot « galérer » détonne : l'auteur l'a mis à dessein." en="In this formal report, the word 'galérer' clashes: the writer put it there on purpose." />

<Example fr="Le décalage entre le ton solennel et le sujet produit un effet de rupture." en="The gap between the solemn tone and the subject produces an effect of rupture." />

## Three effects

<VocabTable group="effects" />

As a rule of thumb, a deliberate break serves one of three effects.

- **Humour**: a familiar word in a formal text produces *un effet comique* (a comic effect), especially when it's attributed to someone: *pour parler comme mon neveu, on a « galéré »*.
- **Emphasis** (*l'insistance*, not *l'emphase*, which usually means pomposity): a short familiar quotation *met en relief* (highlights) an idea that a long formal sentence would dilute: *une habitante l'a dit plus simplement : « ça craint »*.
- **Irony**: the break can also go upwards. A *solennel* (solemn) register for a small event says the opposite of what it seems to say: *avec la solennité qui convient aux grands moments de l'histoire, l'installation de deux bancs*.

The same word can serve different effects in different texts. Name the effect you want before you write the break, and check that the rest of the paragraph supports it.

<Example fr="Ici, l'insistance passe par une citation courte et familière." en="Here, the emphasis comes from a short, familiar quotation." />

<Example fr="Le ton solennel, pour une si petite nouvelle, crée un effet comique et ironique à la fois." en="The solemn tone, for such small news, creates an effect that is comic and ironic at once." />

## Signalling the break

<VocabTable group="signals" />

A reader recognizes an intended break by its signals:

- **Typography**: the word is *entre guillemets* (in quotation marks) or *en italique* (in italics).
- **An attribution**: *pour reprendre l'expression de…* (to use the words of…), *pour parler comme…* (to put it the way … would), *comme on dit* (as they say).
- **The context**: an obviously solemn sentence about a trivial subject signals irony without any punctuation.

Quoting someone's register is the safest break: the familiar words belong to the person quoted, and your own text keeps its register. In free indirect speech, which you met at C1, a novel slips into a character's register in the same way.

<Example fr="Pour reprendre l'expression d'une habitante, le projet « craint »." en="To use the words of one resident, the project is 'bad news' (familiar, quoted)." />

<Example fr="Le mot est en italique : c'est, comme on dit, un mot à la mode." en="The word is in italics: it is, as they say, a fashionable word." />

## When a break is a mistake

The break is a mistake when:

- **Nothing signals it**, and the word looks like the writer's own choice in the wrong register: *nous vous remercions de bosser sur ce dossier*.
- **Nothing in the text calls for it**: no humour, no emphasis, no irony, only a word that came too fast.
- **The reader can't take it as a joke**: an official letter, a complaint, a report for a client.

As you saw in the lesson on revising your style, the remedy for an involuntary break is to replace the word. The remedy for an intended one that readers might misread is to add a signal.

## The model: a column with three breaks

The column below is original, written for the lesson. Its three deliberate breaks are explained after it.

*Chronique du lundi. Le conseil municipal s'est réuni jeudi pour examiner le projet d'aménagement de la place du Marché. Le rapport présenté aux élus, long de quatre-vingts pages, conclut que le projet est « susceptible de générer des difficultés de circulation ». La séance a duré quatre heures. Autant dire, pour parler comme mon neveu, qu'on a « galéré ».*

*Le rapport étudie tour à tour la circulation, le stationnement, le bruit et le coût des travaux. Il propose de réduire le nombre de places de parking, de rendre la place aux piétons et d'y planter des arbres. Certains élus ont défendu ces propositions ; d'autres ont demandé une nouvelle étude avant toute décision. Le débat, toujours courtois, a surtout porté sur la date des travaux.*

*Les habitants présents dans la salle n'ont pas tous suivi les quatre-vingts pages. Une habitante l'a dit plus simplement que le rapport, et en deux mots : « Ça craint. » On ne saurait mieux résumer l'inquiétude des riverains, qui craignent de ne plus pouvoir se garer près de chez eux.*

*Il faut pourtant reconnaître au conseil une qualité : la patience. Chacun a pu parler, chaque question a reçu une réponse, et personne n'a quitté la salle avant la fin. Peu de réunions publiques peuvent en dire autant. On peut trouver la séance longue ; on ne peut pas lui reprocher son sérieux.*

*Au terme de ces quatre heures, une décision a tout de même été prise, après un long silence et sans aucune opposition. On nous annonce donc, avec la solennité qui convient aux grands moments de l'histoire, l'installation de deux bancs. Le reste du projet sera examiné lors d'une prochaine séance, dont la date n'est pas encore fixée.*

*Deux bancs, donc. C'est peu, mais c'est un début : on pourra au moins s'asseoir pour attendre la suite. Les riverains, eux, attendront la prochaine séance. Nous aussi, avec intérêt.*

(Monday column. The town council met on Thursday to examine the plan for redeveloping place du Marché. The report presented to the councillors, eighty pages long, concludes that the plan is "likely to generate traffic difficulties". The meeting lasted four hours. You might as well say, to put it the way my nephew would, that we "had a hard time of it" (familiar, quoted). The report examines in turn traffic, parking, noise and the cost of the works. It proposes to reduce the number of parking spaces, to give the square back to pedestrians and to plant trees there. Some councillors defended these proposals; others asked for a new study before any decision. The debate, always courteous, was mainly about the date of the works. The residents in the room didn't all follow the eighty pages. One resident put it more simply than the report, in two words: "That's bad news." (familiar, quoted). The local residents' worry, that they won't be able to park near their homes any more, couldn't be better summed up. Yet one quality must be granted to the council: patience. Everyone was able to speak, every question got an answer, and nobody left the room before the end. Few public meetings can say as much. You can find the meeting long; you can't fault it for lack of seriousness. At the end of these four hours, a decision was taken all the same, after a long silence and without any opposition. So we are told, with the solemnity befitting the great moments of history, of the installation of two benches. The rest of the plan will be examined at a later meeting, whose date hasn't yet been set. Two benches, then. It's not much, but it's a start: at least we'll be able to sit down while we wait for what comes next. The residents will wait for the next meeting. So will we, with interest.)

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

## How the column works

- **The base register** is formal: *examiner le projet d'aménagement*, *au terme de*, *on ne saurait mieux résumer*.
- **First break, humour**: *galéré*, a familiar verb, signalled twice, by *pour parler comme mon neveu* and by quotation marks. The writer lends the word to someone else and smiles at the long meeting.
- **Second break, emphasis** (*l'insistance*): *« Ça craint »*, a resident's two familiar words, quoted, against the report's formal phrase in quotation marks too. The short quotation highlights the residents' worry better than the eighty pages.
- **Third break, irony**: the break goes upwards. *Avec la solennité qui convient aux grands moments de l'histoire* is far too solemn for two benches; the context alone signals the irony.
- **The ending** returns to a plain, everyday register (*c'est peu, mais c'est un début*) to close with a smile.

<Callout type="warning" title="Signal the break, or it reads as a slip">
  Learners who discover deliberate breaks sometimes drop a familiar word into a formal text and expect the reader to see the joke: *le projet craint*. Without a signal, it reads as a slip. Attribute it or mark it: **le projet, pour reprendre l'expression d'une habitante, « craint »**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Allow yourself one break per text">
  In a formal text of your own, allow yourself one deliberate break, not more, and decide its effect before you write it. Then read the paragraph aloud: if the break makes you smile or stop where you wanted, keep it; if it just sounds careless, remove it.
</Callout>

## One event, five ways

The texts below tell one invented event: the two benches decided by the town council, in the column of the lesson on register breaks, have been installed on place du Marché. The same facts appear in each:

- **The event**: this Monday, two benches were installed on place du Marché.
- **The decision**: the town council had decided it, after a four-hour meeting.
- **What's still to come**: the rest of the project will be examined at another meeting.

Each text keeps these three facts and changes only its register or its style. The familiar text is labelled as such; the fable and the dépêche are original pastiches, written for the lesson.

## Range across the level

Range isn't only a matter of this unit. Each unit of C2 added to the palette: Unit 1 the fine points of form, Unit 2 the exact word, Unit 3 the registers of law, medicine, science and administration, Unit 4 the reading of what a text implies, Unit 5 the long forms of writing, Unit 6 the voices of mediation and negotiation, and Unit 7 the history and the literatures of the language. This unit asks you to choose among all of them, for one reader at a time.

A wide palette doesn't mean using every register in one text. It means knowing which one the situation calls for, and being able to write it without effort.

## Checking a text for its register

When a text is written, check it in three passes:

- **The words**: is there a word from another register, and is it signalled if it's meant?
- **The sentences**: do their length and structure suit the register (short and direct for a message, complete and connected for an official text)?
- **The facts**: if the text is a new version of another one, does it keep every fact and add none?

## Choosing the register

As a rule of thumb, choose by the reader and the situation, then keep the choice for the whole text:

- **Familiar**, for a friend, in a message: short sentences, *on* for *nous*, familiar words such as *galérer*.
- **Everyday**, for the general public: complete sentences, the passive where the agent doesn't matter, no image that needs explaining.
- **Formal**, for an official text: impersonal turns (*c'est avec… que*), nouns rather than verbs, no familiar word unless it's quoted and signalled.

A style adds its own markers on top of a register: the capitals and the moral of a fable, the inverted incise and the closing formula of a dépêche.

## How the five texts work

- **The same three facts** in each: the two benches installed this Monday on place du Marché, the council's decision after four hours, and the rest of the plan still to be examined.
- **The familiar message**: *tu sais quoi ?*, *ils ont mis*, *on a bien galéré*, an exclamation.
- **The everyday text**: the passive (*ont été installés*), *au terme de*, no image.
- **The formal release**: *c'est avec satisfaction que*, nouns (*l'installation*, *un examen*), *conformément à*, *faire l'objet de*.
- **The fable**: capitals, the passé simple (*accoucha*, *posa*, *s'assirent*), a moral that plays on a well-known contrast between talking and acting.
- **The dépêche**: the fact first, *a-t-on appris auprès de*, and a final detail on what isn't known.

The dépêche's last detail (*dont la date n'a pas été précisée*), the familiar *on a bien galéré* and the closing question, and the release's invitation to residents add comments, not facts: none of the five texts adds an event.

<Callout type="warning" title="A register is a choice for the whole text">
  When they write in a register they don't use often, learners slip back halfway through: *C'est avec satisfaction que la municipalité annonce l'installation des bancs, et franchement, c'est top.* Choose the register at the start and keep it: **C'est avec satisfaction que la municipalité annonce l'installation de deux bancs.** If you want a break, signal it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the facts first, then the registers">
  Before you write a text in several registers or styles, list its facts in three or four short lines, in neutral French. Then write each version from that list, not from the previous version. You'll change the words without losing or adding a fact.
</Callout>

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words name where you are now. *La maîtrise* (mastery) of a language is the ability to use it with precision, range and ease. It isn't the same as speaking like *un locuteur natif* (a native speaker): a native speaker doesn't necessarily master every register, and an advanced *apprenant* (learner) may write with more care. And mastery isn't an end: every level, C2 included, leaves room for *un perfectionnement* (further refinement).

<Example fr="La maîtrise d'une langue ne veut pas dire parler comme un locuteur natif." en="Mastering a language doesn't mean speaking like a native speaker." />

## What you can now do

At the end of C2, you can do each of the following, with the unit that taught it in mind:

- **Write with exact form**, including the agreements and concordances that careful writers check twice.
- **Choose the exact word**, and avoid a paronym, a false friend or an anglicism a reader would notice.
- **Read and restate** legal, medical, scientific and administrative texts without losing a condition.
- **Read between the lines**: presuppositions, connotations and fallacies, in the press and in literature.
- **Write long texts** (a contraction, an essay, a translation) and revise them sentence by sentence.
- **Mediate and negotiate**: relay a text faithfully, reach an agreement, argue aloud and ask precise questions.
- **Place French in its history**, from the Strasbourg Oaths to the literatures written in French today.
- **Move between registers and styles** on purpose, and imitate a style honestly.

## How to keep improving

C2 isn't a finishing line. As a rule of thumb, three habits keep a high level alive:

- **Read widely**, in genres you don't usually read: a legal text one week, a poem the next.
- **Write regularly**, and revise with the checklists of Unit 5.
- **Notice**: keep a list of the words, idioms and turns of phrase that surprise you, with their register.

## The model: Mon rapport à la langue française

The text below is invented, written for the lesson: a learner who has reached C2 looks back. It isn't by a real person.

*Mon rapport à la langue française (texte inventé). Le français est entré dans ma vie par la petite porte : une chanson entendue à la radio, dont je ne comprenais pas un mot. J'ai voulu savoir ce qu'elle disait, et je n'ai plus arrêté. J'avais un dictionnaire, beaucoup de patience, et aucune idée du chemin qui m'attendait.*

*Les premières années, la langue m'a résisté de toutes les façons possibles. Je confondais les registres, j'accordais mal les participes, et je traduisais mot à mot des expressions qui ne se traduisent pas. Je croyais aussi les mots qui ressemblent à l'anglais, sans penser aux faux amis. Un jour, j'ai écrit à une administration une lettre que je croyais polie ; elle était surtout trop familière. J'ai appris, ce jour-là, que la politesse française a ses formules.*

*Puis la langue est devenue une compagne. J'ai lu La Fontaine, dont la morale vient parfois en premier, et j'ai découvert que l'humour français aime jouer sur les sons. J'ai écrit des contractions, des essais, et même un petit pastiche, signalé comme tel. J'ai aussi appris à servir d'intermédiaire, pour expliquer à une voisine un avis de la mairie sans rien oublier.*

*Aujourd'hui, je ne parle pas comme un locuteur natif, et ce n'est plus mon but. Mon accent reste le mien. Ce que je cherche, c'est la justesse : le mot exact, le ton qui convient, la nuance qui change tout. Je relis mes textes en me demandant, comme on me l'a appris, si chaque phrase dit ce que je veux dire, et rien de plus. Je sais aussi changer de registre quand je le veux, et le signaler.*

*La maîtrise d'une langue n'est jamais finie. Un perfectionnement m'attend encore, à l'oral surtout. Mais j'ai gardé de mes débuts ce qui compte le plus : la curiosité, et le plaisir d'entendre une phrase française qui sonne juste.*

(My relationship with the French language (an invented text). French came into my life in a modest way: a song heard on the radio, of which I didn't understand a word. I wanted to know what it said, and I never stopped. I had a dictionary, a lot of patience, and no idea of the road that lay ahead of me. In the first years, the language resisted me in every possible way. I mixed up registers, made participles agree wrongly, and translated word for word expressions that don't translate. I also trusted words that look like English, without thinking of false friends. One day, I wrote a letter to an administration that I thought was polite; above all, it was too familiar. I learned that day that French politeness has its formulas. Then the language became a companion. I read La Fontaine, whose moral sometimes comes first, and I discovered that French humour likes to play on sounds. I wrote contractions, essays, and even a short pastiche, labelled as such. I also learned to act as a go-between, to explain a notice from the town hall to a neighbour without leaving anything out. Today, I don't speak like a native speaker, and that's no longer my aim. My accent remains my own. What I'm looking for is accuracy: the exact word, the right tone, the nuance that changes everything. I reread my texts asking myself, as I was taught, whether each sentence says what I mean, and nothing more. I also know how to change register when I want to, and to signal it. Mastering a language is never finished. Further refinement still awaits me, in speaking above all. But I've kept from my beginnings what matters most: curiosity, and the pleasure of hearing a French sentence that rings true.)

## How the closing text works

- **One register throughout**: everyday written French, in the first person, with no familiar word and no deliberate break.
- **What it draws on**: the registers and the formulas of an official letter, mediation (relaying a notice without leaving anything out), idioms that don't translate, La Fontaine's moral placed first, wordplay on sounds, the contraction, the essay and a labelled pastiche.
- **A plan in five steps**: a first contact, the resistance, the companionship, today, and what remains to do. The writing task follows the same plan.
- **No exam, no level claim**: the text speaks of a relationship with a language, not of a test.

<Callout type="warning" title="Mastery isn't a native-speaker imitation">
  At the end of a long course, learners sometimes judge themselves only against native speakers: *je ne parlerai jamais comme un Français, donc je n'ai pas réussi*. Mastery is precision, range and ease in your own voice. Say it as the closing text does: **je ne parle pas comme un locuteur natif, et ce n'est plus mon but**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Reread one review a month">
  Once you've finished the course, choose one unit review a month and reread it, then do its exercises again without looking at the lesson. The items you get wrong show you where your perfectionnement should go next.
</Callout>
