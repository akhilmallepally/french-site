By the end of this lesson you'll be able to write participle clauses that have their own subject, use the fixed participles of formal French, and avoid a participle that refers to the wrong person.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-participles-and-gerund" text="the lesson on the present participle and the gérondif" />, you learned the participe présent, *ayant* or *étant* + past participle, *étant donné*, and the rule that an *-ant* form at the start of a sentence refers to the subject of the main verb. You know *une fois que* from <LessonLink id="b2-futur-anterieur" text="the lesson on the futur antérieur" />, and *vu que* from <LessonLink id="b2-logical-connectors" text="the lesson on logical connectors" />. This lesson adds clauses with their own subject.

## The absolute participle clause

<VocabTable group="forms" />

A participle clause can have its own subject, different from the subject of the main verb. French grammar calls it *la proposition participiale*. The noun comes first, then the participle, and the clause is separated from the rest by a comma.

<Example fr="La réunion terminée, chacun est rentré chez soi." en="The meeting over, everyone went home." />
<Example fr="Le contrat signé, les travaux ont commencé." en="Once the contract was signed, the work began." />

<Example fr="Dans cette phrase, la proposition participiale a son propre sujet." en="In this sentence, the participle clause has its own subject." />

The past participle agrees with its own subject, as with *être*: *la réunion terminée*, *les contrats signés*. The clause usually gives a time or a cause: the first event is over, then the second one happens.

With a present participle, the clause gives a cause or a circumstance. For an earlier action, use *ayant* or *étant* + past participle.

<Example fr="Le train ayant du retard, nous avons manqué la correspondance." en="As the train was late, we missed our connection." />
<Example fr="Les participants ayant lu le rapport, la discussion a été courte." en="As the participants had read the report, the discussion was short." />

## Une fois + noun + participle

*Une fois* + noun + participle is the most flexible form. It means "once (something is done)", and it works in any tense.

<Example fr="Une fois le rapport terminé, envoyez-le au directeur." en="Once the report is finished, send it to the director." />
<Example fr="Une fois la séance levée, les participants sont partis." en="Once the meeting was closed, the participants left." />

In everyday French, use *une fois que* + a conjugated verb: *une fois que le rapport est terminé, envoyez-le*.

## Fixed participles: vu, étant donné, compte tenu de, excepté

<VocabTable group="fixed" />

A few participles work like prepositions. Before the noun, they don't agree.

- **Vu** + noun means "given": *vu la situation*, *vu l'heure*.
- **Étant donné** + noun means the same, in a slightly more formal register: *étant donné les circonstances*.
- **Compte tenu de** + noun means "taking into account". It's the only one of the three that takes *de*.
- **Excepté** + noun means "except", like *sauf*: *tous excepté le président*.

<Example fr="Vu la situation, nous reportons la réunion." en="Given the situation, we're postponing the meeting." />
<Example fr="Compte tenu de la hausse des prix, le budget a été revu." en="In view of the rise in prices, the budget has been revised." />
<Example fr="Tous les participants ont signé, excepté le président." en="All the participants signed, except the president." />

## Same subject or own subject: the test

This is the key point, and it extends the B2 rule. A participle clause with no noun of its own refers to the subject of the main verb. The test is simple: ask "who did the action of the participle?", then check that the answer is the subject of the main verb.

<Example fr="Arrivé à la gare, il a vu que le train était parti." en="When he got to the station, he saw that the train had left." />

Here *arrivé* and *il* have the same subject: correct. *Arrivé à la gare, le train était parti* would mean that the train got to the station. If the subjects differ, give the participle its own noun, or use a conjugated verb with *quand* or *comme*.

<Example fr="Les participants arrivés, la séance a été ouverte." en="When the participants had arrived, the meeting was opened." />

## Register

Absolute participle clauses belong to written French: reports, minutes, history and literature. In literary narrative, you'll often meet them with the passé simple: *la séance levée, chacun quitta la salle*. In speech, use *quand*, *comme*, *une fois que* or *après* + past infinitive.

| Formal writing | Everyday French |
|---|---|
| *La réunion terminée, chacun est parti.* | *Quand la réunion s'est terminée, tout le monde est parti.* |
| *Le train ayant du retard, nous avons manqué la correspondance.* | *Comme le train avait du retard, on a raté la correspondance.* |
| *Vu l'heure, nous allons nous arrêter.* | *Vu l'heure, on va s'arrêter.* |

The last row shows that *vu* is also common in speech.

## Words for minutes

<VocabTable group="words" />

Participle clauses are typical of *le procès-verbal* (the minutes of a meeting). The president *ouvre la séance* and later *lève la séance*; the participants *approuvent* the budget or *le calendrier*.

<Example fr="La séance ouverte, le président a présenté l'ordre du jour." en="With the meeting open, the president presented the agenda." />
<Example fr="Le calendrier approuvé, chacun a reçu une copie du contrat." en="Once the schedule had been approved, everyone received a copy of the contract." />

## The whole list

- Absolute clause: noun + participle, own subject: *la réunion terminée, chacun est parti*.
- With a present participle: *le train ayant du retard, …*
- *Une fois* + noun + participle: *une fois le rapport terminé*.
- Fixed participles, invariable before the noun: *vu*, *étant donné*, *excepté*; and *compte tenu de*.
- The test: who did the action of the participle? If it isn't the subject of the main verb, give the participle its own noun.
- Register: formal writing; in speech, *quand*, *comme*, *une fois que*.

## En français

Nadia is writing the minutes of this morning's meeting. Her colleague Julien reads over her shoulder.

- *Tu as fini le procès-verbal de la séance ?* (Have you finished the minutes of the meeting?)
- *Presque. J'ai écrit : « La séance ouverte, le président a présenté l'ordre du jour. »* (Nearly. I wrote: "With the meeting open, the president presented the agenda.")
- *C'est très écrit ! À l'oral, on dirait « quand la séance a commencé ».* (That's very written! In speech, we'd say "quand la séance a commencé". (spoken))
- *Oui, mais c'est un procès-verbal, pas une conversation.* (Yes, but it's minutes, not a conversation.)
- *D'accord. Et pour le budget ?* (OK. And for the budget?)
- *« Compte tenu de la hausse des prix, le budget a été revu. »* ("In view of the rise in prices, the budget was revised.")
- *Et la question du calendrier ?* (And the question of the schedule?)
- *« Vu l'heure, elle a été reportée à la prochaine séance. »* ("Given the time, it was postponed to the next meeting.")
- *Attention à la phrase suivante : « Ayant lu le rapport, la réunion a commencé. »* (Careful with the next sentence: "Having read the report, the meeting began.")
- *Ah oui, ce n'est pas la réunion qui a lu le rapport ! « Les participants ayant lu le rapport, la discussion a été courte. »* (Oh yes, it wasn't the meeting that read the report! "As the participants had read the report, the discussion was short.")
- *C'est juste. Et la fin ?* (That's right. And the end?)
- *« Tous les points traités, le président a levé la séance. »* ("All the items dealt with, the president closed the meeting.")
- *Parfait. Une fois le texte relu, envoie-le à toute l'équipe.* (Perfect. Once the text has been read over, send it to the whole team.)
- *Je l'envoie dès que j'ai fini.* (I'll send it as soon as I've finished.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Arrivé à la gare, il a vu…, not arrivé à la gare, le train…">
  Learners often open with a past participle and then change the subject. A participle with no noun of its own refers to the subject of the main verb: write **arrivé à la gare, il a vu que le train était parti**, or give it its own subject: **les participants arrivés, la séance a été ouverte**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Close each step of the minutes">
  When you write minutes or a report, start each new paragraph with a participle clause that closes the previous step: *Le budget approuvé, …*, *Ce point traité, …*. The reader sees at once that one item is finished and the next one begins.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La proposition participiale a son propre sujet, différent du sujet du verbe principal : la réunion terminée, chacun est rentré chez soi. Le nom vient d'abord, puis le participe, qui s'accorde avec ce nom. Avec ayant ou étant, la proposition donne une cause : le train ayant du retard, nous avons manqué la correspondance. Une fois suivi d'un nom et d'un participe veut dire après la fin d'une action : une fois le rapport terminé, envoyez-le. Vu, étant donné et excepté ne s'accordent pas devant le nom, et compte tenu prend de : compte tenu de la hausse des prix. Un participe sans nom a le même sujet que le verbe principal. Si ce n'est pas la même personne, il faut donner au participe son propre sujet. Ces formes sont typiques de l'écrit. À l'oral, on dit plutôt quand, comme ou une fois que.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to build long formal sentences with *dont* and *duquel*, choose between a relative clause with or without commas, and use apposition and detached adjectives.

## What you already know

You know *dont* for verbs with *de* and for "whose" from <LessonLink id="b1-relative-dont" text="the lesson on dont" />, and *au cours duquel* and *la raison pour laquelle* from <LessonLink id="b2-relative-lequel" text="the lesson on lequel, auquel and duquel" />, where you also learned that *dont* can't follow a preposition of several words. *Ce dont* and *ce à quoi* come from <LessonLink id="b2-relative-prepositions" text="the lesson on relatives after prepositions" />. This lesson uses them in the long sentences of academic writing.

## Dont + noun: the word order

<VocabTable group="dont" />

When *dont* means "whose" or "of which", the noun it depends on comes later in the clause, in the normal order: subject, verb, object. The noun takes *le*, *la* or *les*, not a possessive, because *dont* already says whose it is.

<Example fr="C'est un écrivain dont on connaît surtout les romans." en="He's a writer whose novels are best known." />
<Example fr="C'est une autrice dont j'admire l'écriture." en="She's an author whose writing I admire." />

*Dont j'admire son écriture* is a common mistake: the possessive repeats what *dont* already says.

In a long sentence, keep the subject of the relative clause close to *dont*, and don't let the clause grow longer than the main sentence. If it does, split the sentence in two.

## A preposition + noun + duquel

<VocabTable group="duquel" />

You know that *dont* can't follow *près de* or *au cours de*. The same is true of the formal prepositions of academic writing: *à l'issue de* (at the end of), *en raison de* (because of), *à la suite de* (following). Use the preposition + *duquel*, *de laquelle*, *desquels* or *desquelles*.

<Example fr="La conférence, à l'issue de laquelle un débat a eu lieu, était longue." en="The lecture, at the end of which a debate took place, was long." />
<Example fr="Le retard, en raison duquel nous avons manqué le train, était de vingt minutes." en="The delay, because of which we missed the train, was twenty minutes." />
<Example fr="La réunion, à la suite de laquelle le projet a été lancé, a duré deux heures." en="The meeting, following which the project was launched, lasted two hours." />

The same applies when *dont* would depend on a noun that follows a preposition: *l'homme à la fille duquel j'ai parlé* (the man whose daughter I spoke to). This form is very formal. In most texts, a simpler sentence is clearer: *j'ai parlé à la fille de cet homme*.

## Commas change the meaning

<VocabTable group="commas" />

French distinguishes two kinds of relative clause, and the commas show which one you mean.

- **Une relative déterminative**, with no commas, says which people or things you mean. Without it, the sentence would mean something else.
- **Une relative explicative**, between commas, adds information about all of them. You could remove it and the main sentence would still be true.

<Example fr="Les étudiants qui avaient travaillé ont réussi." en="The students who had worked passed (only those)." />
<Example fr="Les étudiants, qui avaient travaillé, ont réussi." en="The students, who had all worked, passed." />

Check the meaning before you add commas: the first sentence is only about the students who had worked; the second is about all the students.

## Apposition and detached adjectives

<VocabTable group="apposition" />

*L'apposition* places a noun next to another noun, between commas, to add information about it. It often replaces a relative clause with *qui est*.

<Example fr="Victor Hugo, l'auteur des Misérables, a passé près de vingt ans en exil." en="Victor Hugo, the author of Les Misérables, spent nearly twenty years in exile." />

The article in an apposition is optional: *Victor Hugo, auteur des Misérables, …* is also correct and a little more formal.

An adjective or a participle can be detached in the same way, at the start or at the end of the sentence. A detached adjective at the start refers to the subject of the main verb and agrees with it, just like the participle clauses in <LessonLink id="c1-participle-clauses" text="the lesson on participle clauses" />.

<Example fr="Inquiète, elle a appelé son frère." en="Worried, she called her brother." />
<Example fr="Il est arrivé à la soirée, surpris de voir tant de monde." en="He arrived at the party, surprised to see so many people." />

*Inquiet, sa mère l'a appelé* would mean that the mother was worried, with the wrong agreement. If the adjective describes someone else, put it next to that person or use a clause.

## Commas in formal prose

The same commas frame every detached element: an apposition, a relative explicative, a detached adjective, a participle clause. As a rule of thumb, if you can remove the element and keep a correct sentence, put it between commas; if the sentence needs it to say who or what, don't.

<Example fr="Le rapport, qui comporte trois parties, a été approuvé." en="The report, which consists of three parts, was approved." />

## The whole list

- *Dont* + subject + verb + *le / la / les* + noun: *dont j'admire l'écriture*.
- After a preposition of several words: *à l'issue duquel*, *en raison de laquelle*, never *dont*.
- No commas: the relative defines; between commas: it adds information.
- Apposition: a noun between commas, with or without the article.
- Detached adjective: same subject as the main verb, same agreement.

## En français

Inès and Tom, two students, are writing a presentation about Victor Hugo.

- *Tu as écrit l'introduction ?* (Have you written the introduction?)
- *Oui : « Victor Hugo, dont on connaît surtout Les Misérables, a aussi écrit des poèmes. »* (Yes: "Victor Hugo, whose best-known work is Les Misérables, also wrote poems.")
- *Bien. Dans ma partie, j'ai écrit : « Hugo, dont on connaît surtout ses romans… ».* (Good. In my part, I wrote: "Hugo, dont on connaît surtout ses romans…".)
- *Attention, pas de « ses » après « dont ». On dit « dont on connaît surtout les romans ».* (Careful, no "ses" after "dont". We say "dont on connaît surtout les romans".)
- *D'accord. Ensuite, je parle de son exil.* (OK. Then I talk about his exile.)
- *Écris : « Hugo, opposant au Second Empire, a passé près de vingt ans en exil. »* (Write: "Hugo, an opponent of the Second Empire, spent nearly twenty years in exile.")
- *C'est une apposition ! Il faut des virgules ?* (That's an apposition! Do we need commas?)
- *Oui, avant et après. Sans elles, la phrase est difficile à lire.* (Yes, before and after. Without them, the sentence is hard to read.)
- *Et pour la fin : « L'exil, au cours duquel il a beaucoup écrit, a duré jusqu'en 1870. »* (And for the end: "The exile, during which he wrote a lot, lasted until 1870.")
- *Très bien. Tu peux aussi commencer une phrase par un adjectif.* (Very good. You can also start a sentence with an adjective.)
- *Comme « Fatigué, Hugo est rentré en France » ?* (Like "Tired, Hugo returned to France"?)
- *Oui, si c'est vrai ! Je ne sais pas s'il était fatigué.* (Yes, if it's true! I don't know if he was tired.)
- *Alors j'écris plutôt « Âgé de soixante-huit ans, Hugo est rentré en France ».* (Then I'll write "At the age of sixty-eight, Hugo returned to France" instead.)
- *Parfait, c'est vrai et c'est bien écrit.* (Perfect, it's true and it's well written.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Dont j'admire l'écriture, not dont j'admire son écriture">
  Learners often add a possessive after *dont*, because English says "whose writing". *Dont* already gives the owner, so the noun takes an article: **c'est une autrice dont j'admire l'écriture**, **un écrivain dont on connaît les romans**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Remove it and reread">
  Before you put commas around a relative clause, cover it and reread the sentence. If the sentence still says what you mean, the clause is explicative and goes between commas. If it now says something else (*les étudiants ont réussi*, all of them), keep the clause without commas.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Dans une phrase longue, dont est suivi de l'ordre normal : sujet, verbe, puis le nom avec le, la ou les. On dit dont j'admire l'écriture, sans son, sa ou ses, car dont montre déjà à qui est la chose. Après une préposition de plusieurs mots, comme à l'issue de ou en raison de, on emploie duquel ou de laquelle, et jamais dont : la conférence, à l'issue de laquelle un débat a eu lieu. Les virgules changent le sens. Une relative déterminative, sans virgules, dit de qui on parle. Une relative explicative, entre virgules, ajoute une information sur tous. L'apposition place un nom à côté d'un autre nom, entre virgules : Victor Hugo, l'auteur des Misérables, a passé près de vingt ans en exil. Un adjectif détaché au début de la phrase a le même sujet que le verbe principal : inquiète, elle a appelé son frère.

## What you already know

You know the main connectors of argument from <LessonLink id="b2-logical-connectors" text="the lesson on logical connectors" /> (*en outre*, *or*, *par conséquent*, *d'où*, *en somme*) and *en revanche* from <LessonLink id="b2-concession-opposition" text="the lesson on concession and opposition" />. The conditionnel for a fact you can't confirm comes from <LessonLink id="b2-conditionnel-passe" text="the lesson on the conditionnel passé" />, and *a priori* and *dans une certaine mesure* from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing your opinion" />. This lesson adds the connectors and hedges that academic texts rely on.

## Connectors of academic reasoning

<VocabTable group="logic" />

Academic French links each step of an argument explicitly. Four connectors are typical of it.

**Dès lors** draws a consequence, like *par conséquent*, but it stresses that the consequence follows necessarily from what was just said. **Dès lors que** + indicative introduces a condition already met: "since, given that".

<Example fr="Les données sont limitées. Dès lors, il est difficile de conclure." en="The data are limited. Consequently, it's difficult to draw a conclusion." />
<Example fr="Dès lors que les données sont fiables, nous pouvons tirer une conclusion." en="Since the data are reliable, we can draw a conclusion." />

**En l'occurrence** names the particular case you're talking about: "in this case, namely". It doesn't mean "by chance".

<Example fr="Certaines villes ont réduit la circulation, en l'occurrence Lyon et Nantes." en="Some cities have reduced traffic, namely Lyon and Nantes." />

**A fortiori** (written without an accent) says that a conclusion is even more true in a second case.

<Example fr="Ce texte est difficile pour les adultes, et a fortiori pour les enfants." en="This text is difficult for adults, and all the more so for children." />

**À cet égard** links a sentence to the point just made: "in this respect, on this point".

<Example fr="La sécurité est notre priorité. À cet égard, de nouvelles règles seront instaurées." en="Safety is our priority. In this respect, new rules will be brought in." />

## Two B2 connectors at C1

Two connectors you know need care in academic writing.

**Or** introduces a new fact that changes the reasoning, often the second step of a three-step argument: a rule, then a fact, then the conclusion. As a connector, it never means "or", and it's followed by a comma.

<Example fr="Tout candidat doit avoir un diplôme. Or, Paul n'en a pas. Dès lors, sa candidature est refusée." en="Every candidate must have a degree. But Paul doesn't have one. Consequently, his application is turned down." />

**D'ailleurs** adds a supporting point, often one that the reader didn't expect, while **en outre** simply adds one more point of the same kind. In an essay, use *en outre* for a list and *d'ailleurs* for an aside that strengthens the argument.

## En revanche or par contre: a register point

<VocabTable group="register" />

*Par contre* and *en revanche* both mark a contrast. *Par contre* is very common in speech and in everyday writing, and it's correct French. In formal and academic writing, however, many teachers and style guides prefer *en revanche*. As a rule of thumb, write *en revanche* in an essay, a report or a formal letter, and use *par contre* freely when you speak.

<Example fr="Le prix est bas. Par contre, la qualité est faible." en="The price is low. On the other hand, the quality is poor." />
<Example fr="Le prix est bas. En revanche, la qualité est faible." en="The price is low. On the other hand, the quality is poor. (formal)" />

## Academic hedging

<VocabTable group="hedging" />

A careful writer doesn't state more than the evidence shows. B2 gave you the conditionnel for unconfirmed facts and *a priori* or *il semble que* for caution. Three formal tools complete the set.

**À en croire** + a source keeps a distance from what the source says: "if we're to believe". It's often followed by the conditionnel.

<Example fr="À en croire la presse, la ministre partirait à la fin du mois." en="If the press is to be believed, the minister is going to leave at the end of the month." />

**Selon toute vraisemblance** says that something is very probable, but not certain: "in all likelihood".

<Example fr="Selon toute vraisemblance, la réunion sera reportée." en="In all likelihood, the meeting will be postponed." />

**Sous réserve de** + noun makes a statement depend on a condition: "subject to".

<Example fr="Le projet sera lancé sous réserve de l'accord du conseil." en="The project will be launched subject to the council's approval." />

Compare the three levels of confidence: *le projet coûtera dix millions* (certain), *selon toute vraisemblance, le projet coûtera dix millions* (very probable), *à en croire le rapport, le projet coûterait dix millions* (reported, not confirmed).

## The whole list

| Connector or hedge | Use |
|---|---|
| *dès lors* / *dès lors que* | a necessary consequence / since, given that |
| *en l'occurrence* | naming the case in question |
| *a fortiori* | even more true in a second case |
| *à cet égard* | in this respect |
| *or* | a new fact that changes the reasoning |
| *en revanche* / *par contre* | contrast: formal / everyday |
| *à en croire* + source | reported, with distance |
| *selon toute vraisemblance* | very probable |
| *sous réserve de* + noun | subject to a condition |

## En français

Sarah, a student, shows a chapter of her research project to her supervisor, Madame Martin.

- *Madame, j'ai fini la partie sur les résultats. Vous pouvez la lire ?* (Madame Martin, I've finished the section on the results. Could you read it?)
- *Volontiers. Vous écrivez : « Les jeunes lisent moins. » C'est un peu direct.* (Gladly. You write: "Young people read less." It's a little blunt.)
- *Mais les données le montrent !* (But the data show it!)
- *Elles le suggèrent. Écrivez plutôt : « Selon toute vraisemblance, les jeunes lisent moins. »* (They suggest it. Write instead: "In all likelihood, young people read less.")
- *Et quand je parle de l'enquête du ministère ?* (And when I mention the ministry's survey?)
- *« À en croire cette enquête, le temps de lecture aurait baissé. » Le conditionnel montre que vous ne l'affirmez pas.* ("If this survey is to be believed, reading time has fallen." The conditionnel shows that you're not stating it as fact.)
- *D'accord. Et ici, j'ai mis « par contre ».* (OK. And here, I put "par contre".)
- *C'est correct, mais dans un texte académique, « en revanche » est plus soigné.* (It's correct, but in an academic text, "en revanche" is more careful.)
- *Et pour donner un exemple précis ?* (And to give a precise example?)
- *« Certains lecteurs, en l'occurrence les adolescents, lisent surtout en ligne. »* ("Some readers, namely teenagers, read mostly online.")
- *Et pour ma conclusion ?* (And for my conclusion?)
- *« L'échantillon est limité. Dès lors, ces résultats restent à confirmer. »* ("The sample is limited. Consequently, these results remain to be confirmed.")
- *Ce n'est pas trop prudent ?* (Isn't that too cautious?)
- *Non, c'est honnête. À cet égard, votre travail est déjà très bon.* (No, it's honest. In this respect, your work is already very good.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Dès lors, not dès lors que, before a comma">
  Learners often write *dès lors que* where they mean "consequently". *Dès lors que* needs a clause: **dès lors que les données sont fiables, nous pouvons conclure**. To start a sentence with a consequence, write **dès lors,** followed by the main clause.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Grade your claims">
  Before you hand in an academic text, mark each claim with a number from 1 to 3: 1 for what you're sure of, 2 for what is very probable, 3 for what a source says. Then check that the wording matches: plain verbs for 1, *selon toute vraisemblance* for 2, *à en croire* and the conditionnel for 3.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le texte académique relie chaque étape du raisonnement. Dès lors annonce une conséquence nécessaire, et dès lors que, suivi de l'indicatif, veut dire puisque. En l'occurrence donne le cas précis dont on parle : certaines villes, en l'occurrence Lyon et Nantes. A fortiori montre qu'une conclusion est encore plus vraie dans un second cas, et à cet égard relie une phrase au point précédent. Or présente un fait nouveau qui change le raisonnement. Par contre est correct et très courant à l'oral, mais à l'écrit soutenu, on préfère en revanche. Pour rester prudent, on dit à en croire la presse, souvent avec le conditionnel, selon toute vraisemblance pour ce qui est très probable, et sous réserve de pour une condition : le projet sera lancé sous réserve de l'accord du conseil.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to grant a point and imagine a case with the formal tools of argument, choose the right mood after each one, and recognize the literary inverted hypothesis.

## What you already know

You know *même si* and *quand même* from <LessonLink id="b2-concession-opposition" text="the lesson on concession and opposition" />, *bien que* + subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b2-subjunctive-conjunctions" text="the lesson on conjunctions with the subjunctive" />, and *quoique* from <LessonLink id="b2-relative-prepositions" text="the lesson on relatives after prepositions" />. *Au cas où* + conditionnel comes from <LessonLink id="b2-mixed-conditionals" text="the lesson on mixed si-clauses" />, and *fût-il* from <LessonLink id="c1-imperfect-subjunctive" text="the lesson on the imperfect subjunctive" />. This lesson adds the formal conjunctions of argument, each with its own mood.

## Quand bien même: even if, in theory

<VocabTable group="concession" />

*Quand bien même* means "even if", for a case you only imagine. Unlike *même si*, it takes the conditionnel, and the main clause is usually in the conditionnel too.

<Example fr="Quand bien même il pleuvrait, nous partirions." en="Even if it rained, we would leave. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Même s'il pleuvait, nous partirions." en="Even if it rained, we would leave." />

Compare the moods: *quand bien même il pleuvrait* (conditionnel), *même s'il pleuvait* (imparfait). In the past, use the conditionnel passé: *quand bien même il aurait plu, nous serions partis*.

*Quand bien même* sounds stronger than *même si*: the speaker grants the worst case and keeps the conclusion. It's typical of essays and debates.

## Encore que: a reservation added afterwards

*Encore que* + subjunctive adds a reservation after the main point: "although, mind you". It often comes at the end of a sentence, and the verb can be left out.

<Example fr="Le film est bon, encore qu'il soit un peu long." en="The film is good, although it's a bit long. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Le film est bon, encore qu'un peu long." en="The film is good, if a bit long. (formal)" />

*Bien que* can come before or after the main clause: *bien qu'il soit long, le film est bon* or *le film est bon, bien qu'il soit long*. *Encore que* almost always comes after it: the main point first, then the reservation, as an afterthought.

## Three ways to set a condition

<VocabTable group="hypothesis" />

Three conjunctions set a condition, and all three take the subjunctive. They differ in how the speaker sees the condition.

- **Pour peu que**: the smallest condition is enough. *Pour peu qu'il fasse beau, la plage sera pleine* (if the weather is even a little fine, the beach will be full).
- **À supposer que**: you assume something for the sake of argument. *À supposer que le projet soit accepté, qui le financera ?*
- **Si tant est que**: you doubt the condition will be met. *Il réussira, si tant est qu'il fasse des efforts.*

<Example fr="Pour peu qu'il fasse beau, la plage sera pleine." en="If the weather is at all fine, the beach will be full." />
<Example fr="À supposer que le projet soit accepté, qui le financera ?" en="Assuming the project is accepted, who will fund it?" />
<Example fr="Il réussira, si tant est qu'il fasse des efforts." en="He'll pass, assuming he makes an effort (which isn't certain)." />

In these examples, the subjunctive after the conjunction differs from the indicative: *qu'il fasse* (not *fait*), *soit* (not *est*). The main clause is in the futur or the present, as with *si* + present.

Remember that *si* itself never takes the subjunctive or, when it means "if", the conditionnel: *si le projet est accepté*. Only these conjunctions with *que* take the subjunctive.

## The inverted hypothesis: literary

<VocabTable group="inverted" />

In literary and very formal French, a hypothesis can be expressed with no conjunction at all, by inverting the verb and the subject pronoun at the start of the sentence. The meaning is "even if".

- With the conditionnel: **Serait-il malade, il viendrait.** (Even if he were ill, he would come.)
- With the imparfait du subjonctif, for recognition only: **Fût-il malade, il viendrait.**

<Example fr="Serait-il ministre, il devrait respecter la loi." en="Even if he were a minister, he would have to respect the law. (literary)" />

Both are literary. In your own writing, prefer *même si* or *quand bien même*. You'll meet the inverted forms in classic texts and in speeches.

## Choosing the right tool

All of these tools belong to careful writing and formal speech. In conversation, *même si* and *si* with the indicative cover most of the same ground, and *quand bien même* or *si tant est que* can sound bookish among friends. Keep them for essays, reports and debates, where they show exactly how far you grant a point.


| You want to say | Formal tool | Mood |
|---|---|---|
| even if (imagined) | *quand bien même* | conditionnel |
| although (afterthought) | *encore que* | subjunctive |
| if only, as long as | *pour peu que* | subjunctive |
| supposing that | *à supposer que* | subjunctive |
| assuming (doubtfully) | *si tant est que* | subjunctive |
| in case | *au cas où* | conditionnel |
| even if (literary) | *serait-il…*, *fût-il…* | conditionnel, imparfait du subjonctif |

## Words for a project

<VocabTable group="words" />

These tools are useful whenever you discuss a plan and its risks: how to *financer* (fund) it, whether *une subvention* (a grant) will come, and what happens if it doesn't.

<Example fr="La ville pourrait financer le festival, à supposer que la région lui donne une subvention." en="The town could fund the festival, assuming the region gives it a grant." />

## En français

Malik and Clara run a small theatre association. They're planning a summer festival.

- *Le festival aura lieu en juillet, à supposer que la mairie nous donne une subvention.* (The festival will take place in July, assuming the town hall gives us a grant.)
- *Et si elle refuse ?* (And if it refuses?)
- *Quand bien même elle refuserait, nous trouverions l'argent ailleurs.* (Even if it refused, we would find the money elsewhere.)
- *Tu es optimiste ! Il faudrait des entreprises pour financer le projet.* (You're optimistic! We'd need companies to fund the project.)
- *Pour peu qu'on contacte les bonnes entreprises, ce sera possible.* (As long as we contact the right companies, it'll be possible. (spoken))
- *Si tant est qu'elles aient du budget cette année…* (Assuming they have any budget this year…)
- *Certaines en ont, encore que ce soit plus difficile qu'avant.* (Some do, although it's harder than before.)
- *Et le public ? Il viendra ?* (And the audience? Will they come?)
- *Pour peu qu'il fasse beau, la place sera pleine.* (If the weather's at all fine, the square will be full.)
- *Et au cas où il pleuvrait ?* (And in case it rains?)
- *On jouerait à l'intérieur, à la mairie.* (We'd play indoors, at the town hall. (spoken))
- *Tu penses vraiment à tout !* (You really do think of everything!)
- *Il le faut. Même si c'est difficile, ce festival doit avoir lieu.* (I have to. Even if it's difficult, this festival must take place.)
- *Alors, au travail !* (Then let's get to work!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Quand bien même il pleuvrait, not il pleuve or il pleut">
  Learners often give *quand bien même* the subjunctive, like *bien que*, or the indicative, like *même si*. It takes the conditionnel: **quand bien même il pleuvrait, nous partirions**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Pick the tool by how sure you are">
  When you write an argument, ask how likely your condition is. Likely and minimal: *pour peu que*. Neutral, for the sake of argument: *à supposer que*. Doubtful: *si tant est que*. Imagined and conceded: *quand bien même*. One question gives you the conjunction and its mood.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Pour faire une concession ou une hypothèse à l'écrit soutenu, on a plusieurs outils, chacun avec son mode. Quand bien même veut dire même si, pour un cas qu'on imagine, et il prend le conditionnel : quand bien même il pleuvrait, nous partirions. Encore que ajoute une réserve après l'idée principale, avec le subjonctif : le film est bon, encore qu'il soit un peu long. Pour peu que, à supposer que et si tant est que posent une condition et prennent le subjonctif. Pour peu que montre qu'une petite condition suffit, à supposer que fait une hypothèse pour la discussion, et si tant est que montre un doute. Au cas où prend le conditionnel. Enfin, la langue littéraire peut mettre le verbe avant le sujet, sans conjonction : serait-il malade, il viendrait. Fût-il malade a le même sens, avec l'imparfait du subjonctif.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use the abstract nouns of academic argument to judge a claim, measure the scale of a problem and describe a method, without making your writing heavy.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-nominalisation" text="the lesson on nominalisation" />, you turned verbs and adjectives into nouns (*l'augmentation*, *la fiabilité*, *la mise en place*) and learned *le style nominal*. You know *le constat* from <LessonLink id="b2-formal-report" text="the lesson on the formal report" /> and *un enjeu* from <LessonLink id="b2-review-science" text="the review of science and research" />. This lesson adds the nouns that academic texts use to discuss ideas rather than facts.

## Why abstract nouns

Academic French often talks about ideas as things: not "this argument is relevant" but "the relevance of this argument". A noun can become the subject of a sentence, take adjectives and be discussed: *la pertinence de cet argument est discutable*. This is why essays, reports and syntheses use so many of them, and why a C1 reader expects them in any text that weighs ideas against each other.

The nouns below come in families. Learn each with its adjective or verb, so that you can move between the two styles.

## Judging an argument

<VocabTable group="judge" />

These nouns help you evaluate a claim, a decision or a study. *La pertinence* is whether it's relevant; *la légitimité*, whether it's justified or acceptable; *la cohérence*, whether its parts fit together; *la rigueur*, whether it's careful and exact. *Le bien-fondé* is the validity of a claim or decision, and it usually follows *douter de* or *reconnaître*. *L'efficacité* is whether something works.

<Example fr="Je doute de la pertinence de cet argument." en="I doubt whether this argument is relevant." />
<Example fr="Personne ne remet plus en question la légitimité de cette demande." en="Nobody questions the legitimacy of this request any more." />
<Example fr="L'étude manque de rigueur : l'échantillon est trop petit." en="The study lacks rigour: the sample is too small." />
<Example fr="Nul ne doute du bien-fondé de cette décision." en="Nobody doubts the merits of this decision. (formal)" />

## Scale and impact

<VocabTable group="scale" />

*La portée* is how far something reaches: the impact of a decision or the scope of a study. *L'ampleur* is the size of a problem or a phenomenon. *L'essor* is a rise, a boom. *L'apport* is what something contributes.

<Example fr="Personne ne mesure encore la portée de cette décision." en="Nobody yet grasps the impact of this decision." />
<Example fr="Il faut mesurer l'ampleur du problème." en="We must measure the scale of the problem." />
<Example fr="L'essor de la lecture en ligne a changé le marché du livre." en="The rise of online reading has changed the book market." />
<Example fr="L'apport de cette étude est important." en="This study's contribution is significant." />

## Method

<VocabTable group="method" />

These nouns describe how a study or an essay proceeds. *La démarche* is the approach. *Le cadre* is the framework or context. *La prise en compte*, *la remise en question* and *la mise en évidence* are nouns built on the verbs *prendre en compte*, *remettre en question* and *mettre en évidence*. *La faisabilité* is whether a plan can be carried out. *Prendre du recul* means to step back and see a question from a distance.

<Example fr="La démarche de l'auteur est originale." en="The author's approach is original." />
<Example fr="Dans le cadre de ce projet, nous avons interrogé dix salariés." en="As part of this project, we interviewed ten employees." />
<Example fr="La prise en compte des besoins des habitants est nécessaire." en="Taking residents' needs into account is necessary." />
<Example fr="Avant de donner votre avis, il faut prendre du recul." en="Before giving your opinion, you need to step back." />

## From verbs to nouns, and back

Nominal style lets you pack an idea into a noun phrase. Compare:

<Example fr="Nous n'avions pas compris que le problème était si grand." en="We hadn't understood that the problem was so big." />
<Example fr="Nous n'avions pas compris l'ampleur du problème." en="We hadn't understood the scale of the problem." />

The second sentence is shorter and more formal. But a text made only of nouns is hard to read. As a rule of thumb, use one abstract noun per sentence, keep a real verb in every sentence, and turn a noun back into a verb when a sentence has more than two *de*.

<Example fr="La prise en compte de la remise en question de la démarche est nécessaire." en="Taking into account the questioning of the approach is necessary. (too heavy)" />
<Example fr="Il faut tenir compte des critiques de notre démarche." en="We need to take account of the criticism of our approach." />

## En français

Léo and Emma, two students, are preparing a presentation on the four-day week.

- *Pour l'exposé sur la semaine de quatre jours, par quoi on commence ?* (For the presentation on the four-day week, where do we start? (spoken))
- *Par le constat : beaucoup d'entreprises s'y intéressent.* (With the findings: a lot of companies are interested in it.)
- *D'accord. Ensuite, on parle de l'ampleur du changement ?* (OK. Then we talk about the scale of the change? (spoken))
- *Oui, et des enjeux : la santé des salariés, le temps libre…* (Yes, and what's at stake: employees' health, free time…)
- *Il faut aussi présenter notre démarche.* (We also need to present our approach.)
- *On a lu trois études et on a interrogé dix salariés.* (We read three studies and interviewed ten employees. (spoken))
- *Avec dix personnes, la portée de nos résultats est limitée.* (With ten people, the scope of our results is limited.)
- *C'est vrai. Il faut le dire, pour être honnêtes.* (True. We have to say so, to be honest.)
- *Et la légitimité de la mesure ? Certains ne sont pas d'accord.* (And the legitimacy of the measure? Some people disagree.)
- *On peut parler de la pertinence de leurs arguments.* (We can talk about how relevant their arguments are. (spoken))
- *Attention, pas trop de noms abstraits : le texte devient lourd.* (Careful, not too many abstract nouns: the text gets heavy.)
- *Tu as raison. Prenons un peu de recul : notre message principal, c'est quoi ?* (You're right. Let's step back: what's our main message? (spoken))
- *Que la semaine de quatre jours est possible, mais pas partout.* (That the four-day week is possible, but not everywhere.)
- *Parfait. Commençons par ça.* (Perfect. Let's start with that.)

<Callout type="warning" title="La démarche, l'enjeu: check the gender">
  Learners often guess the gender of abstract nouns from English. Learn it with the article: **la démarche**, **la portée**, **l'ampleur** (feminine), but **l'enjeu**, **l'essor**, **l'apport**, **le cadre** (masculine). A wrong gender shows at once in *quel est l'enjeu* or *la démarche est originale*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a verb in every sentence">
  When you revise an academic text, underline the conjugated verb of each sentence. If it's only *être* or *avoir* and the sentence holds three abstract nouns, turn one noun back into a verb. Your text stays formal, but the reader can follow it.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le texte académique parle souvent des idées comme de choses, avec des noms abstraits. Quand on donne son avis sur un argument, on parle de sa pertinence, de sa légitimité, de sa cohérence, de sa rigueur, de son bien-fondé ou de son efficacité. Pour mesurer un problème, on parle de sa portée, de son ampleur, de son essor ou de l'apport d'une étude. Pour présenter une méthode, on parle de la démarche, du cadre, de la prise en compte, de la remise en question, de la mise en évidence ou de la faisabilité d'un projet. Prendre du recul veut dire regarder une question de loin. Il faut apprendre chaque nom avec son article et son adjectif ou son verbe. Attention : trop de noms rendent le texte lourd, et il faut garder un vrai verbe dans chaque phrase.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read two or three documents on the same subject and combine them into one structured, neutral text.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-summarizing-an-article" text="the lesson on summarising an article" />, you learned to stay *neutre* and *objectif*, to *reformuler* rather than copy, and to start with what an article *porte sur*. You know the connectors of comparison *en revanche* and *tandis que* from <LessonLink id="b2-concession-opposition" text="the lesson on concession and opposition" />, and the hedges of <LessonLink id="c1-advanced-connectors" text="the lesson on advanced connectors" />. A synthesis uses all of them, with one new difficulty: several documents at once.

## What a synthèse is

<VocabTable group="method" />

*Une synthèse de documents* is a classic French written exercise in schools, universities and the workplace. You read two or three documents on one subject and write a single text that presents their ideas together. Four rules define it.

- **It's neutral.** You don't give your opinion, and you don't judge the documents.
- **It contains nothing else.** No outside facts and no examples of your own: only what the documents say.
- **It doesn't quote.** You reformulate, and you say which document an idea comes from.
- **It's organized by ideas, not by document.** Each part *confronte* the documents on one question: where they *s'accordent*, where they *divergent*, how they *se complètent*.

The last rule is the hardest. A text that summarizes the first document, then the second, is two summaries, not a synthesis.

## Step 1: compare before you write

Read each document once for its main idea, then a second time with a pencil. Make a small grid: one line per question the documents raise, one column per document. In each box, note what the document says, in a few words of your own. Some boxes stay empty: that's information too.

The grid shows you *les points communs* and *les divergences*. Those become your plan.

## Step 2: build the plan

As a rule of thumb, a synthesis has an introduction, two or three parts and a short conclusion.

- **The introduction** presents the common subject and the documents in one or two sentences: *les deux documents portent sur…*. It can announce the plan.
- **Each part** answers one question from your grid, with the views of all the documents on it.
- **The conclusion** sums up the main point of agreement or disagreement. It doesn't add an opinion.

## Step 3: write neutrally

To say who thinks what, use the reporting verbs you know (*affirmer*, *souligner*, *estimer*) with a neutral subject: *le premier document*, *l'autre article*, *selon la maire*. To compare, use *de même*, *en revanche*, *tandis que*, *l'un… l'autre*. To show how sure a source is, keep its hedges: if a document says *selon toute vraisemblance*, don't turn it into a fact.

<Example fr="L'un des documents insiste sur les avantages, tandis que l'autre souligne les difficultés." en="One of the documents stresses the advantages, while the other highlights the difficulties." />
<Example fr="De même, les deux auteurs évoquent la demande du public." en="Likewise, both writers mention public demand." />

Avoid every word that judges: *heureusement*, *malheureusement*, *à juste titre*, *il est évident que*. And avoid "I": a synthesis never says *je pense*.

## Useful formulas

A synthesis has its own set phrases. They're neutral, and they keep the reader oriented.

- **To open**: *les deux documents portent sur…*, *ces textes traitent de…*.
- **To present agreement**: *les documents s'accordent sur…*, *les deux auteurs soulignent…*, *de même…*.
- **To present a difference**: *en revanche…*, *tandis que…*, *les documents divergent sur…*.
- **To show that they complement each other**: *l'un… l'autre…*, *les documents se complètent*.
- **To conclude**: *au total…*, *en somme…*.

<Example fr="Ces textes traitent du même sujet, mais ils divergent sur les solutions." en="These texts deal with the same subject, but they differ on the solutions." />
<Example fr="Le titre d'une synthèse reste neutre : il donne le sujet, pas la conclusion." en="The title of a synthesis stays neutral: it names the subject, not the conclusion." />
<Example fr="En somme, les documents se complètent plus qu'ils ne s'opposent." en="In short, the documents complement each other more than they contradict each other." />

Two more habits help. First, give each document a short, neutral name the first time you mention it (*l'article*, *la tribune*), and keep it. Second, never write that a document "is right" or "is wrong": write what it says, and let the reader judge. If your teacher asks for a title, choose a neutral one that names the subject, not your conclusion.

<VocabTable group="topic" />

These two short documents are original texts written for this lesson. The town and the people in them are invented.

*Document 1 : un article de presse locale*

*Depuis le mois de septembre, la bibliothèque de Saint-Aubin ouvre le dimanche après-midi. Selon la maire, c'est un grand succès : les familles viennent nombreuses, et beaucoup d'usagers qui travaillent pendant la semaine découvrent enfin les lieux. *Document 2 : la tribune d'un syndicat*

*L'ouverture des bibliothèques le dimanche répond à une vraie demande, nous le reconnaissons. Mais elle ne doit pas se faire au détriment du personnel. Le travail du dimanche doit rester volontaire et être mieux payé. Sans moyens supplémentaires, les équipes s'épuisent, et la qualité du service finit par baisser. <VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Document 1, a local newspaper article: Since September, the Saint-Aubin library has been open on Sunday afternoons. According to the mayor, it's a great success: families come in large numbers, and many users who work during the week are at last discovering the place. The town has taken on two students to help the team, and the Sunday opening hours are from two to six. The librarians agree that the atmosphere is pleasant, but some find the days long. Document 2, an opinion piece by a union: Opening libraries on Sundays meets a real demand; we recognize that. But it mustn't be done at the expense of the staff. Sunday work must remain voluntary and be better paid. Without extra resources, teams wear themselves out, and the quality of the service ends up falling. Our union therefore asks that every opening be preceded by a discussion with the staff.)

## A model synthesis

*Les deux documents portent sur l'ouverture des bibliothèques le dimanche. L'un est un article de presse locale, l'autre la tribune d'un syndicat.*

*Les deux textes s'accordent d'abord sur un point : cette ouverture répond à une demande du public. Le premier document rapporte que, selon la maire, c'est un succès, en particulier pour les familles et les usagers qui travaillent en semaine.*

*En revanche, ils ne traitent pas de la même façon la question des conditions de travail. L'article évoque seulement les journées longues de certains bibliothécaires, tandis que la tribune demande que le travail du dimanche reste volontaire et soit mieux payé.*

*Au total, les documents se complètent : l'un montre l'intérêt du public, l'autre les conditions qui permettraient de maintenir ce service.*

(Both documents are about opening libraries on Sundays. One is a local newspaper article, the other a union opinion piece. The two texts first agree on one point: this opening meets public demand. The first document reports that, according to the mayor, it's a success, especially for families and for users who work during the week. On the other hand, they treat the question of working conditions differently. The article only mentions the long days of some librarians, while the opinion piece asks that Sunday work remain voluntary and be better paid. All in all, the documents complement each other: one shows public interest, the other the conditions that would make it possible to keep this service going.)

## How the model works

- **The introduction** names the subject and the two documents, with no opinion.
- **The plan follows ideas**: first the point of agreement (the demand), then a question the documents treat differently (working conditions). Each paragraph speaks of both documents.
- **There's no quotation.** The mayor's words are reported with their source: *le premier document rapporte que, selon la maire, c'est un succès*.
- **Every view has its source**: *le premier document*, *selon la maire*, *l'article*, *la tribune*.
- **The connectors carry the comparison**: *d'abord*, *en revanche*, *tandis que*, *l'un… l'autre*.
- **The conclusion** shows how the documents fit together, without taking sides.

Here the synthesis is nearly as long as the two documents, because they're very short. With documents of a page or more, as a rule of thumb, aim for a quarter to a third of their total length.

## En français

Camille is writing her first synthesis. She asks her friend Hugo, who studies law, for advice.

- *J'ai lu les deux documents. Je commence par résumer le premier ?* (I've read the two documents. Shall I start by summarizing the first one?)
- *Non, sinon tu fais deux résumés. Il faut organiser par idées.* (No, otherwise you'll write two summaries. You need to organize it by ideas.)
- *Comment je trouve les idées ?* (How do I find the ideas?)
- *Fais un tableau : les questions d'un côté, les documents de l'autre.* (Make a grid: the questions on one side, the documents on the other.)
- *D'accord. Les deux parlent de la demande du public.* (OK. Both talk about public demand.)
- *Voilà un point commun. Et la différence ?* (That's a point in common. And the difference?)
- *Le syndicat parle surtout du personnel.* (The union talks mainly about the staff.)
- *Alors ça, c'est ta deuxième partie.* (Then that's your second part.)
- *Je peux dire que je suis d'accord avec le syndicat ?* (Can I say that I agree with the union?)
- *Surtout pas ! Une synthèse reste neutre.* (Definitely not! A synthesis stays neutral.)
- *Et je peux citer la maire ?* (And can I quote the mayor?)
- *Non, tu reformules : « selon la maire, c'est un succès ».* (No, you reformulate: "according to the mayor, it's a success".)

<Callout type="warning" title="Organize by ideas, not by document">
  The most common mistake is to summarize document 1, then document 2. Each part of a synthesis compares the documents on one question: **les deux documents s'accordent sur…**, **en revanche, ils divergent sur…**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the grid before the text">
  Spend the first part of your time on the grid of questions and documents, not on writing. When every box is filled, the plan is already there, and the writing goes much faster.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to write a C1 essay: an open problématique, a plan that suits the subject, clear transitions, nuanced arguments and an ouverture.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-argumentative-essay" text="the lesson on the argumentative essay" />, you learned the structure of *une dissertation*: *l'amorce*, *la problématique*, the announced plan, the dialectical plan (*la thèse*, *l'antithèse*, *la synthèse*), *une transition*, *en guise de conclusion* and *une ouverture*. You can grant a point with *quand bien même* and *encore que* from <LessonLink id="c1-concession-and-hypothesis" text="the lesson on concession and hypothesis" />. This lesson takes each of those steps to C1 level.

## An open problématique

<VocabTable group="question" />

At B2, the subject often asked for a yes or no, and the problématique was an indirect question with *on peut se demander si*. At C1, as a rule of thumb, the problématique is an open question that makes room for a nuanced answer. Two formulas are typical: **dans quelle mesure** (to what extent) and **en quoi** (in what way).

<Example fr="Dans quelle mesure les écrans transforment-ils notre façon de lire ?" en="To what extent are screens changing the way we read?" />
<Example fr="En quoi la lecture sur écran est-elle différente ?" en="In what way is reading on screen different?" />

In a direct question, keep the formal inversion of written French: with a noun subject, add a pronoun after the verb (*dans quelle mesure les écrans changent-ils… ?*). In an indirect question, after *on peut se demander*, there's no inversion and no question mark: *on peut se demander dans quelle mesure les écrans changent notre façon de lire*. A good problématique doesn't contain the answer: *pourquoi les écrans sont-ils dangereux ?* has already decided.

## Three kinds of plan

<VocabTable group="plans" />

The body of the essay, *le développement*, follows a plan announced at the end of the introduction. You know the dialectical plan. Two others are common, and the subject decides which one fits.

- **Un plan dialectique** (thesis, antithesis, synthesis) suits a question that opens a debate. The third part doesn't just choose a side: it *dépasse l'opposition*, for example by showing that the two views apply in different cases.
- **Un plan analytique** (the facts, their causes, their consequences or the solutions) suits a subject about a problem or a phenomenon.
- **Un plan thématique** (one aspect per part: economic, social, cultural) suits a subject that asks you to describe or assess something from several angles.

<Example fr="Le développement comporte deux ou trois parties." en="The body has two or three parts." />
<Example fr="Pour un sujet sur un problème, un plan analytique convient souvent mieux qu'un plan thématique." en="For a subject about a problem, an analytical plan often works better than a thematic one." />

As a rule of thumb, ask what the subject asks you to do: debate, explain or explore. Whatever the plan, two or three parts of similar length read better than one long part and one short one, and each part usually holds two or three arguments with an example each. In each case, every part should answer the problématique, not just describe the subject.

## Transitions

A transition closes one part and opens the next in one or two sentences. At C1, it often grants what the last part showed and then turns: concessive **si** + the indicative, then **il n'en reste pas moins que** (the fact remains that).

<VocabTable group="links" />

<Example fr="Si les écrans facilitent l'accès aux textes, il n'en reste pas moins qu'ils changent notre attention." en="While screens make texts easier to reach, the fact remains that they change our attention." />

This *si* doesn't express a condition: it means "while, although", and it takes the indicative like the *si* of a condition. Other transitions use the tools of earlier lessons: *reste à savoir si…*, *reste la question de…*, or a question.

## Nuance in the arguments

An argument at C1 rarely stands alone: it's supported by an example, then nuanced. Three tools help.

- **Concession**: *certes… mais*, *quand bien même* + conditionnel, *encore que* + subjunctive.
- **Hedging**: *selon toute vraisemblance*, *à en croire* + source, from <LessonLink id="c1-advanced-connectors" text="the lesson on advanced connectors" />.
- **Abstract nouns**: *la portée*, *les enjeux*, *la pertinence*, from <LessonLink id="c1-abstract-nouns" text="the lesson on abstract nouns" />.

<Example fr="Quand bien même l'attention changerait, la lecture elle-même ne disparaîtrait pas." en="Even if attention were to change, reading itself wouldn't disappear." />

## The conclusion and the ouverture

The conclusion answers the problématique in two or three sentences, with no new argument and no new example. The ouverture then widens the subject with a related question. It shouldn't change the subject completely, and it shouldn't repeat the answer.

<Example fr="On peut se demander si l'école doit apprendre aux enfants à lire sur écran." en="One may ask whether schools should teach children to read on screen." />

## A model essay

<VocabTable group="topic" />

This is an original model essay written for this lesson. It uses a dialectical plan in three short parts.

*Sujet : Les écrans changent-ils notre façon de lire ?*

*Aujourd'hui, on lit partout : dans le métro, sur son téléphone, sur un ordinateur au travail. Les écrans font désormais partie de la vie quotidienne, et la lecture ne peut pas y échapper. On peut dès lors se demander dans quelle mesure les écrans transforment notre façon de lire. Nous verrons d'abord qu'ils rendent les textes plus accessibles, puis qu'ils peuvent modifier notre attention, avant de montrer que les deux formes de lecture peuvent se compléter.*

*Dans un premier temps, force est de constater que les écrans ont rendu la lecture plus accessible. Un article, un roman ou une étude sont disponibles en quelques secondes, souvent sans frais. De même, les personnes qui voient mal peuvent agrandir le texte. Les bibliothèques en ligne permettent aussi de lire des textes rares. Les écrans permettent ainsi d'élargir le public des lecteurs.*

*Si les écrans facilitent l'accès aux textes, il n'en reste pas moins qu'ils changent la manière dont nous lisons. Dans un deuxième temps, en effet, la lecture sur écran est souvent plus rapide. Le lecteur passe d'un texte à l'autre, et des messages l'interrompent. Selon toute vraisemblance, cette lecture rapide convient surtout aux textes courts, encore que certains lecteurs lisent longtemps sur une tablette.*

*Dans un troisième temps, il convient de dépasser cette opposition. Quand bien même l'attention changerait, la lecture elle-même ne disparaîtrait pas. Le papier et l'écran ne s'excluent pas : on lit un article sur son téléphone et un roman sur papier. Pour un texte long et difficile, beaucoup de lecteurs préfèrent encore le papier ; pour une information rapide, l'écran suffit. Chaque support a ses usages, et un bon lecteur sait choisir le sien.*

*En guise de conclusion, les écrans transforment notre façon de lire, mais sans remplacer le livre : ils ajoutent une lecture plus rapide à une lecture plus lente. On peut se demander si l'école doit apprendre aux enfants à lire sur écran.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Subject: Are screens changing the way we read? Today, we read everywhere: on the underground, on our phones, on a computer at work. Screens are now part of everyday life, and reading can't escape them. One may therefore ask to what extent screens are transforming the way we read. We will first see that they make texts more accessible, then that they can change our attention, before showing that the two forms of reading can complement each other. Firstly, it must be said that screens have made reading more accessible. An article, a novel or a study is available in a few seconds, often free of charge. Likewise, people with poor eyesight can enlarge the text. Online libraries also make it possible to read rare texts. Screens thus make it possible to widen the reading public. While screens make texts easier to reach, the fact remains that they change the way we read. Secondly, reading on screen is indeed often faster. The reader moves from one text to another, and messages interrupt them. In all likelihood, this fast reading suits short texts above all, although some readers read for a long time on a tablet. Thirdly, we should go beyond this opposition. Even if attention were to change, reading itself wouldn't disappear. Paper and screen don't exclude each other: we read an article on our phone and a novel on paper. By way of conclusion, screens are transforming the way we read, but without replacing the book: they add a faster kind of reading to a slower one. One may ask whether schools should teach children to read on screen.)

## How the essay works

- **The introduction** turns the yes/no subject into an open problématique in an indirect question, with no inversion (*on peut dès lors se demander dans quelle mesure les écrans transforment notre façon de lire*). It starts with an amorce (*on lit partout*) and ends with the plan in the futur simple (*nous verrons*).
- **The parts** follow a dialectical plan, announced with *dans un premier temps*, *dans un deuxième temps* and *dans un troisième temps*.
- **The transition** between the first and second parts uses *si… il n'en reste pas moins que*.
- **The arguments are nuanced** with *selon toute vraisemblance* and *encore que* (second part).
- **The third part** goes beyond the opposition instead of choosing a side, and opens with a concession: *quand bien même l'attention changerait*.
- **The conclusion** answers the problématique, then opens with a related question.

## En français

Julie and Lucas are preparing an essay for their university course.

- *Le sujet, c'est : « Les écrans changent-ils notre façon de lire ? »* (The subject is: "Are screens changing the way we read?")
- *On répond oui ou non ?* (Do we answer yes or no?)
- *Ni l'un ni l'autre. On fait une problématique ouverte : « dans quelle mesure… ».* (Neither. We write an open problématique: "to what extent…".)
- *D'accord. Et quel plan ? Dialectique ?* (OK. And which plan? Dialectical?)
- *Oui, le sujet ouvre un débat. Mais la troisième partie doit dépasser l'opposition.* (Yes, the subject opens a debate. But the third part has to go beyond the opposition.)
- *Comment on passe d'une partie à l'autre ?* (How do we move from one part to the next? (spoken))
- *Avec une transition : « si…, il n'en reste pas moins que… ».* (With a transition: "while…, the fact remains that…".)
- *Et pour nuancer un argument ?* (And to nuance an argument?)
- *« Quand bien même », ou « encore que ». On les a vus la semaine dernière.* ("Quand bien même", or "encore que". We saw them last week. (spoken))
- *Et la conclusion ?* (And the conclusion?)
- *Une réponse courte, puis une ouverture : une question proche du sujet.* (A short answer, then an ouverture: a question close to the subject.)
- *Parfait. On commence par l'introduction ?* (Perfect. Shall we start with the introduction? (spoken))

<Callout type="warning" title="Dans quelle mesure les écrans changent-ils…, not les écrans changent…">
  Learners often drop the inversion after *dans quelle mesure* or *en quoi* in a direct question, as they would in speech. In a written direct question, keep it: **dans quelle mesure les écrans changent-ils notre façon de lire ?** In an indirect question, don't invert: **on peut se demander dans quelle mesure les écrans changent notre façon de lire**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the transitions first">
  Once your plan is ready, write the two transitions before anything else, each in one sentence with *si…, il n'en reste pas moins que…*. If you can't write them, the parts don't follow from each other yet: fix the plan before you write the body.
</Callout>

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These five words are useful whenever you work on several documents or plan an essay. *Un corpus* is the set of documents you study together. *Mettre en perspective* is to set an idea against a wider context. *Un fil conducteur* is the thread that links every part of a text. *La norme* is what is usual or standard, and *généraliser* is to extend something to everyone. You know *la problématique* from <LessonLink id="b2-argumentative-essay" text="the lesson on the argumentative essay" />.

<Example fr="Le corpus comporte trois documents de nature différente." en="The corpus contains three documents of different kinds." />
<Example fr="Une bonne conclusion met le sujet en perspective." en="A good conclusion puts the subject into perspective." />
<Example fr="Le fil conducteur de la dissertation est clair." en="The essay's common thread is clear." />

## Two tasks, one method

The synthesis and the essay look different: one is neutral, the other argues. But they share a method, and the tools of this unit serve both.

1. **Read and sort.** For a synthesis, fill a grid of questions and documents. For an essay, list arguments for and against, with an example for each.
2. **Find the fil conducteur.** In a synthesis, it's the common subject and the main agreement or disagreement. In an essay, it's the problématique.
3. **Plan by ideas.** Each part answers one question. Announce the plan in the introduction.
4. **Write with precise tools.** Use connectors to show each step (*dès lors*, *en revanche*, *tandis que*), hedges to match the evidence (*selon toute vraisemblance*), and abstract nouns to name ideas (*la faisabilité*, *l'ampleur*).
5. **Reread for register and length.** A participle clause or a *dont* clause tightens a sentence; three in a row make it hard to read.

The difference lies in the voice. In a synthesis, every view belongs to a document: *le premier document souligne…*. In an essay, you build your own answer, but you still nuance it: *quand bien même…*, *encore que…*.

## A model synthesis paragraph and essay introduction

The corpus below is invented: a newspaper article, a study and an opinion piece by a business owner, all about the four-day week. The two texts are original models written for this lesson.

*Synthèse (extrait)*

*Les trois documents du corpus portent sur la semaine de quatre jours. Ils s'accordent d'abord sur un constat : la demande des salariés est forte.*

*Sur les effets de la mesure, en revanche, les documents ne sont pas du même avis. L'article de presse souligne ses bons effets sur la santé, car les salariés se disent moins fatigués ; il rapporte aussi que, les premiers essais terminés, beaucoup d'entreprises ont choisi de continuer. L'étude, qui porte sur cent entreprises, met en évidence des résultats plus mitigés : selon toute vraisemblance, écrivent ses auteurs, l'efficacité de la mesure dépend du secteur. Quant à la tribune d'un chef d'entreprise, elle émet des réserves sur sa faisabilité dans les petites entreprises.*

*Au total, les documents se complètent : l'un montre l'intérêt de la mesure, les deux autres en précisent les limites.*

*Dissertation (introduction)*

*Sujet : Faut-il généraliser la semaine de quatre jours ?*

*Depuis quelques années, de nombreuses entreprises font l'essai de la semaine de quatre jours, et un débat s'est ouvert. Ce débat, dont les enjeux dépassent le monde du travail, porte autant sur la santé des salariés que sur l'économie. Certains y voient un progrès social, d'autres un risque pour l'économie. Quand bien même la mesure serait populaire, la généraliser poserait des questions difficiles. On peut dès lors se demander dans quelle mesure la semaine de quatre jours peut devenir la norme. Nous verrons d'abord qu'elle répond à de vraies attentes, puis que sa faisabilité dépend du secteur, avant de montrer qu'il faut la mettre en perspective avec d'autres façons d'organiser le travail.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Synthesis, extract: The three documents in the corpus are about the four-day week. They first agree on one finding: employees' demand is strong. On the effects of the measure, on the other hand, the documents don't share the same view. The press article stresses its good effects on health, as employees say they feel less tired; it also reports that, with the first trials over, many companies chose to continue. The study, which covers a hundred companies, brings out more mixed results: in all likelihood, its authors write, the measure's effectiveness depends on the sector. As for the opinion piece by a business owner, it expresses reservations about its feasibility in small companies. All in all, the documents complement each other: one shows the interest of the measure, the other two set out its limits. Essay, introduction. Subject: Should the four-day week be made general? For some years, many companies have been trying out the four-day week, and a debate has opened. This debate, whose stakes go beyond the world of work, concerns employees' health as much as the economy. Some see it as social progress, others as a risk to the economy. Even if the measure were popular, making it general would raise difficult questions. One may therefore ask to what extent the four-day week can become the norm. We will first see that it meets real expectations, then that its feasibility depends on the sector, before showing that it must be put into perspective with other ways of organizing work.)

## How the texts use the unit

- **Participle clause**: *les premiers essais terminés* (synthesis, paragraph 2).
- **Formal relative**: *ce débat, dont les enjeux dépassent le monde du travail* (introduction).
- **Connectors**: *d'abord*, *en revanche*, *quant à* and *au total* (synthesis), *dès lors* (introduction).
- **Hedging**: *selon toute vraisemblance*, attributed to the study's authors (synthesis).
- **Concession and hypothesis**: *quand bien même la mesure serait populaire* (introduction).
- **Abstract nouns**: *le constat*, *l'efficacité*, *la faisabilité* (synthesis), *les enjeux* (introduction).
- **Synthesis method**: three paragraphs organized by idea (the shared finding, the effects, the conclusion); in the second, each document's view keeps its source (*l'article de presse*, *l'étude*, *la tribune*); nothing is quoted.
- **Essay method**: an amorce, an open problématique in an indirect question (*on peut dès lors se demander dans quelle mesure la semaine de quatre jours peut devenir la norme*, with no inversion), and a three-part plan in the futur simple, ending with *mettre en perspective*.

## Your turn

Write the introduction of an essay on this subject, in five or six sentences.

*Sujet : Le numérique rend-il les études plus faciles ?*

(Subject: Is digital technology making studying easier?)

Start with an amorce, then turn the subject into an open problématique with *dans quelle mesure* or *en quoi*. Announce a dialectical or analytical plan in the futur simple. Use at least one *dont* clause, one connector from this unit and one concession with *quand bien même* or *encore que*. Keep one fil conducteur: every sentence should lead to the problématique.

## Check your work

- Does your problématique ask an open question, without the answer in it?
- In a direct question after *dans quelle mesure* or *en quoi*, did you keep the inversion (*dans quelle mesure le numérique rend-il… ?*)? In an indirect one, after *on peut se demander*, did you keep the normal order (*on peut se demander dans quelle mesure le numérique rend…*)?
- After *dont*, did you use *le*, *la* or *les*, not a possessive?
- After *quand bien même*, is the verb in the conditionnel?
- Is your plan announced in the futur simple, with one idea per part?
- Did you avoid *par contre* and *je pense* in formal writing?

<Callout type="warning" title="Dans quelle mesure la semaine… peut-elle, not peut la semaine">
  In a direct question after *dans quelle mesure* with a noun subject, learners often put the verb before the noun. Keep the noun first and add a pronoun after the verb: **dans quelle mesure la semaine de quatre jours peut-elle devenir la norme ?** In an indirect question, there's no inversion: **on peut se demander dans quelle mesure la semaine de quatre jours peut devenir la norme**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="One sentence for the fil conducteur">
  Before you write a synthesis or an essay, write one sentence that states your fil conducteur, and stick it above your notes. Each time you start a paragraph, check that it serves that sentence. If it doesn't, it belongs in another text.
</Callout>
