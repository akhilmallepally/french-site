By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize the main figures of style in a speech or an essay, name them in French, and use a few of them yourself.

## What you already know

You know rhetorical questions such as *n'est-il pas vrai que* and the appeals *avouez que* and *convenez que* from <LessonLink id="b2-persuasion-and-hedging" text="the lesson on persuasion and hedging" />, and *l'antithèse* as a part of the essay from <LessonLink id="b2-argumentative-essay" text="the lesson on the argumentative essay" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-review-style" text="the review of style" />, you used *un effet de style* with restraint. This lesson gives you the names of the devices themselves.

## Why name the devices

<VocabTable group="terms" />

*Une figure de style* is a way of saying something that gives it more force than the plain version. French schools teach these devices by name, and essays, commentaries and debates use the names freely. Knowing them helps you in two ways: you notice *le procédé* when a speaker uses it on you, and you can choose one when you want a sentence to *marquer les esprits*.

<Example fr="Ce slogan est percutant : il marque les esprits." en="This slogan is punchy: it sticks in people's minds." />

## Repetition and rhythm

<VocabTable group="repeat" />

*L'anaphore* repeats the same words at the start of several sentences or clauses. It builds *le rythme* and makes a list sound like a march. *La gradation* lists words or ideas in increasing (or decreasing) strength.

<Example fr="Nous voulons une ville propre. Nous voulons une ville sûre. Nous voulons une ville pour tous." en="We want a clean town. We want a safe town. We want a town for everyone." />
<Example fr="Il faut agir aujourd'hui, demain, toujours." en="We must act today, tomorrow, always." />
<Example fr="C'est une erreur, une faute, presque un scandale." en="It's a mistake, a fault, almost a scandal." />

As a rule of thumb, three items are enough: a fourth repetition starts to sound mechanical, and the audience stops listening to the words and hears only the pattern.

## Opposition

<VocabTable group="oppose" />

You know *l'antithèse* as the second part of a dialectical essay; as a figure of style, it places two opposite ideas side by side in one sentence. *L'oxymore* goes further and joins two opposite words directly. *La question rhétorique* asks something that needs no answer: the answer is obvious, and the question makes the point more strongly than a statement.

<Example fr="Ils parlent beaucoup et agissent peu." en="They talk a lot and act little. (antithesis)" />
<Example fr="C'était une victoire amère." en="It was a bitter victory. (oxymoron)" />
<Example fr="Qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ?" en="Who can still believe that nothing will change? (rhetorical question)" />

## Saying more, saying less

<VocabTable group="degree" />

*La litote* says less in order to mean more, usually by denying the opposite: *ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée* means "it's a good idea". *L'hyperbole* does the reverse: it *exagère* on purpose. Both are common in everyday speech as well as in speeches.

<Example fr="Ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée." en="It's not a bad idea (it's a good one)." />
<Example fr="Je vous l'ai dit mille fois !" en="I've told you a thousand times! (hyperbole)" />

The litote is especially French: a speaker who says *ce n'était pas mal* often means it was very good. <LessonLink id="c1-irony-and-understatement" text="The lesson on irony and understatement" /> comes back to it.

## Images

<VocabTable group="image" />

*Une comparaison* links two things with a word such as *comme*: *il est rusé comme un renard*. *La métaphore* makes the link without the word: *ce débat est une guerre*. Both turn an abstract idea into an image the *auditoire* can see. A *slogan* often combines an image with rhythm.

<Example fr="Ce débat est une guerre." en="This debate is a war. (metaphor)" />
<Example fr="Le temps file comme le vent." en="Time flies like the wind. (simile)" />

## Using devices yourself

At C1, the aim is to recognize every device and to use a few well. As a rule of thumb, one or two devices per paragraph are enough in an essay, and three or four in a whole speech. Choose the device by its effect:

- **To make a list memorable**: anaphora or gradation.
- **To contrast two positions**: antithesis.
- **To involve the audience**: a rhetorical question.
- **To soften or to be ironic**: litotes.
- **To make an abstract idea concrete**: a metaphor or a comparison.

Avoid hyperbole in a formal argument: an exaggeration that a reader can check weakens your credibility. In a speech, by contrast, a single hyperbole at the right moment can wake up a tired audience.

## The whole list

| Device | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| anaphore | repeats the start | *Nous voulons… Nous voulons…* |
| gradation | increasing strength | *aujourd'hui, demain, toujours* |
| antithèse | opposite ideas side by side | *parlent beaucoup, agissent peu* |
| oxymore | opposite words joined | *une victoire amère* |
| question rhétorique | a question with an obvious answer | *Qui peut encore croire… ?* |
| litote | says less to mean more | *ce n'est pas mal* |
| hyperbole | deliberate exaggeration | *mille fois* |
| comparaison, métaphore | images | *comme un renard*, *une guerre* |

## En français

Hugo and Clara are preparing a short speech for their university debating club.

- *Notre discours est clair, mais il ne marque pas les esprits.* (Our speech is clear, but it doesn't stick in people's minds.)
- *On pourrait commencer par une question rhétorique.* (We could start with a rhetorical question. (spoken))
- *Par exemple : « Qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ? »* (For example: "Who can still believe that nothing will change?")
- *Bien. Et pour nos trois arguments, une anaphore ?* (Good. And for our three arguments, an anaphora?)
- *« Nous voulons… Nous voulons… Nous voulons… » Ça donne du rythme.* ("We want… We want… We want…" That gives it rhythm.)
- *Attention, pas plus de trois fois, sinon on ne l'écoute plus.* (Careful, no more than three times, otherwise people stop listening.)
- *Et j'ai écrit : « Ce projet coûtera des millions de millions. »* (And I wrote: "This project will cost millions and millions.")
- *C'est une hyperbole. Dans un débat, l'autre équipe va vérifier.* (That's hyperbole. In a debate, the other team will check.)
- *Tu as raison. J'écris plutôt : « Ce projet n'est pas bon marché. »* (You're right. I'll write instead: "This project isn't cheap.")
- *Une litote ! C'est plus fin, et l'auditoire comprend quand même.* (A litote! It's subtler, and the audience still understands.)
- *Et pour finir, une métaphore ?* (And to finish, a metaphor?)
- *« Ce débat est un pont entre nous », par exemple.* ("This debate is a bridge between us", for example.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée means it's a good idea">
  Learners often build a litote by negating the wrong word. To mean "good", deny the opposite: **ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée**. *Ce n'est pas une bonne idée* simply means it's a bad one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Annotate one speech">
  Find a short French speech, or a paragraph of an opinion piece, and mark every device you can name in the margin. After two or three texts, you'll start to hear the devices as you listen, and to see why a speaker chose each one.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Une figure de style donne plus de force à une idée. L'anaphore répète les mêmes mots au début de plusieurs phrases et crée un rythme : nous voulons une ville propre, nous voulons une ville sûre. La gradation présente des mots de plus en plus forts. L'antithèse place deux idées opposées côte à côte, et l'oxymore unit deux mots contraires, comme une victoire amère. La question rhétorique n'attend pas de réponse : qui peut encore croire que rien ne va changer ? La litote dit moins pour dire plus : ce n'est pas une mauvaise idée. L'hyperbole exagère pour frapper l'esprit. La comparaison et la métaphore créent des images. Dans une dissertation, il vaut mieux choisir peu de procédés, et toujours pour un effet précis, car l'auditoire remarque vite quand il y en a trop.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to use the subjunctive where it changes the tone or the meaning of a sentence: in questions, at the start of a sentence, and after verbs that take either mood.

## What you already know

You know that *je pense que* takes the indicative and *je ne pense pas que* the subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b1-expressing-doubt-certainty" text="the lesson on doubt and certainty" />, and *le seul… qui* + subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b2-subjunctive-relative-clauses" text="the lesson on the subjunctive in relative clauses" />. You know *il se peut que* + subjunctive from <LessonLink id="b2-certainty-and-probability" text="the lesson on certainty and probability" />, and *ne pensez-vous pas que* from <LessonLink id="b2-persuasion-and-hedging" text="the lesson on persuasion and hedging" />. This lesson covers the cases where both moods are possible and the choice says something.

## Mood and meaning

<VocabTable group="terms" />

French calls the indicative, the subjunctive and the conditionnel *les modes* (moods). Most triggers decide *le mode* for you: *il faut que* always takes the subjunctive. But in some places, you choose, and the choice changes the tone or the meaning. In every pair below, the two verb forms differ (*vienne* / *viendra*, *sois* / *es*), so you can see the choice.

<Example fr="Dans cette phrase, le mode change le sens." en="In this sentence, the mood changes the meaning." />

## Penser and croire in questions

<VocabTable group="questions" />

In a question with inversion, *penser que* and *croire que* can take either mood. The subjunctive shows doubt: the speaker isn't sure. The indicative is a neutral question, and it's what you'll hear most with *est-ce que*.

<Example fr="Pensez-vous qu'il vienne demain ?" en="Do you think he'll come tomorrow? (doubtful, formal)" />
<Example fr="Pensez-vous qu'il viendra demain ?" en="Do you think he'll come tomorrow? (neutral)" />
<Example fr="Est-ce que tu crois qu'elle viendra ?" en="Do you think she'll come? (everyday)" />

With a negative question, *ne pensez-vous pas que*, the speaker expects "yes", so the indicative is usual: *ne pensez-vous pas qu'il a raison ?*

## A que clause at the front

<VocabTable group="front" />

When a *que* clause comes first in the sentence, it takes the subjunctive, even if the verb that follows would normally take the indicative. The main clause takes it up with *le* or *cela*.

<Example fr="Qu'il soit fatigué, je le comprends." en="That he's tired, I understand." />
<Example fr="Qu'elle ait raison, personne ne le nie." en="That she's right, nobody denies." />

Compare the usual order: *je comprends qu'il soit fatigué*. The fronted version, typical of debates and speeches, puts the point being conceded first.

**Que… ou non** + subjunctive means "whether… or not". **Qu'il** + subjunctive, alone, gives a wish or an order about a third person: *qu'il entre !* (let him come in!).

<Example fr="Que tu le veuilles ou non, il faut partir." en="Whether you like it or not, we have to leave." />
<Example fr="Qu'elle attende, je suis en réunion." en="She'll have to wait, I'm in a meeting." />

## Verbs that change meaning with the mood

<VocabTable group="meaning" />

A few verbs take the indicative for a fact and the subjunctive for an attitude to that fact.

- **Comprendre que** + indicative: to realize. + subjunctive: to understand why, to find it normal.
- **Admettre que** + indicative: to acknowledge a fact. + subjunctive: to accept an idea or a possibility.
- **Dire que** + indicative: to report. + subjunctive: to give an order, like *dire de* + infinitive.

<Example fr="Il comprend que tu es parti." en="He realizes that you've left." />
<Example fr="Il comprend que tu sois parti." en="He understands why you left." />
<Example fr="J'admets qu'il a raison." en="I acknowledge that he's right." />
<Example fr="J'admets qu'il ait pu se tromper." en="I accept that he may have made a mistake." />
<Example fr="Il dit que tu viens." en="He says you're coming." />
<Example fr="Il dit que tu viennes." en="He says you should come." />

<VocabTable group="words" />

Accepting a fact that concerns someone else also takes the subjunctive: *accepter que*, *il est normal que*.

<Example fr="Il accepte que tu partes plus tôt." en="He accepts that you'll leave earlier." />
<Example fr="Il est normal qu'il soit déçu : il voulait ce poste." en="It's understandable that he's disappointed: he wanted that job." />

*S'attendre à ce que* (to expect) and *tenir à ce que* (to insist that) always take the subjunctive. They're the forms of *s'attendre à* and *tenir à* before a clause.

<Example fr="Je m'attends à ce que la réunion soit longue." en="I expect the meeting to be long." />
<Example fr="Je tiens à ce que tout soit prêt à l'avance." en="I insist that everything be ready in advance." />

## In a debate

These nuances are useful when you argue. Fronting with *que* concedes a point before you turn it: *qu'il y ait des risques, je l'admets ; mais…*. *Que… ou non* sets a condition aside. And the choice of mood after *comprendre* or *admettre* shows whether you merely note a fact or accept it.

<Example fr="Qu'il y ait des risques, je l'admets. Mais ils sont limités." en="That there are risks, I admit. But they're limited." />

## The whole list

| Structure | Mood | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *pensez-vous que… ?* | subjunctive or indicative | doubt, or a neutral question |
| *que* clause at the front | subjunctive | the point conceded first |
| *que… ou non* | subjunctive | whether… or not |
| *qu'il* + verb ! | subjunctive | a wish or an order |
| *comprendre que* | indicative / subjunctive | realize / understand why |
| *admettre que* | indicative / subjunctive | acknowledge / accept the idea |
| *dire que* | indicative / subjunctive | report / order |
| *s'attendre à ce que*, *tenir à ce que* | subjunctive | expect, insist |

## En français

Sophie tells her manager, Monsieur Martin, that a colleague is leaving.

- *Vous savez que Paul quitte l'équipe ?* (Do you know that Paul is leaving the team?)
- *Oui, il me l'a dit hier. Je comprends qu'il veuille partir : on lui propose un meilleur poste.* (Yes, he told me yesterday. I understand why he wants to leave: he's been offered a better job.)
- *Pensez-vous qu'il revienne un jour ?* (Do you think he'll ever come back? (doubtful))
- *Ça, je ne sais pas. Qu'il soit déçu par notre projet, je l'admets.* (That, I don't know. That he's disappointed with our project, I admit.)
- *Et l'équipe ? Elle va le prendre mal.* (And the team? They'll take it badly.)
- *Que l'équipe le veuille ou non, il faut avancer.* (Whether the team likes it or not, we have to move on.)
- *Vous allez annoncer la nouvelle ?* (Are you going to announce the news?)
- *Paul tient à ce que je l'annonce moi-même.* (Paul insists that I announce it myself.)
- *Et qui va le remplacer ?* (And who will replace him?)
- *Je m'attends à ce que la directrice choisisse quelqu'un de l'équipe.* (I expect the director to choose someone from the team.)
- *Elle m'a dit que je sois prête à aider.* (She told me to be ready to help.)
- *Qu'elle vienne vous voir, alors !* (Then let her come and see you!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Il se rend compte que tu es parti, not que tu sois parti">
  Learners who have just met *comprendre que* + subjunctive sometimes use the subjunctive after every verb of understanding. *Se rendre compte que* (to realize) states a fact and takes the indicative: **il se rend compte que tu es parti**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask: fact or attitude?">
  When a verb could take either mood, ask whether you're stating a fact or giving your attitude to it. Fact: indicative (*il comprend que tu es parti*). Attitude, acceptance, order or doubt: subjunctive (*il comprend que tu sois parti*).
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le mode change parfois le sens ou le ton de la phrase. Dans une question avec inversion, pensez-vous que peut prendre le subjonctif, qui montre un doute, ou l'indicatif, qui est neutre. Quand une proposition avec que vient au début de la phrase, elle prend le subjonctif : qu'il soit fatigué, je le comprends. Que… ou non prend aussi le subjonctif : que tu le veuilles ou non. Qu'il, suivi du subjonctif, exprime un souhait ou un ordre. Certains verbes changent de sens avec le mode : comprendre que, suivi de l'indicatif, veut dire se rendre compte, mais avec le subjonctif, il veut dire trouver normal. Admettre que et dire que fonctionnent de la même façon. S'attendre à ce que et tenir à ce que prennent toujours le subjonctif.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to take part in a formal debate in French: present an opening statement, reply to the other side, concede a point and turn it, and conclude within your time.

## What you already know

You know *un modérateur*, *le temps de parole*, *couper la parole à*, *permettez-moi de*, *réfuter*, *objecter que* and *je maintiens que* from <LessonLink id="b2-debating" text="the lesson on taking part in a debate" />. You can concede with *quand bien même* and *encore que* from <LessonLink id="c1-concession-and-hypothesis" text="the lesson on concession and hypothesis" />, and use rhetorical questions and anaphora from <LessonLink id="c1-rhetorical-devices" text="the lesson on rhetorical devices" />. A B2 debate is a discussion; a formal debate has rules, roles and a clock.

## The format

<VocabTable group="format" />

A formal debate starts from *une motion*, a statement such as *les musées nationaux doivent être gratuits*. One team *défend la motion*; the other *s'oppose à la motion*. A moderator gives the floor, and each *intervenant* has a fixed *temps imparti*.

As a rule of thumb, the debate has three rounds:

1. **La déclaration d'ouverture**: each side presents its position and its two or three main arguments.
2. **La réplique**: each side answers the other's arguments, not just repeats its own.
3. **La conclusion**: each side sums up why its position is stronger, with no new argument.

<Example fr="Notre équipe défend la motion : les musées nationaux doivent être gratuits." en="Our team speaks for the motion: national museums should be free." />
<Example fr="Il vous reste une minute sur le temps imparti." en="You have one minute left of your allotted time." />

## Conceding without losing

<VocabTable group="concede" />

In a formal debate, a good concession makes you more convincing, not less: you show that you've heard the other side. The pattern has two steps. First, grant the point: *je vous l'accorde*, *je vous rejoins sur ce point*, or *certes*. Then turn: *il n'en demeure pas moins que* (more formal than *il n'en reste pas moins que*), *mais*, *cependant*.

<Example fr="La gratuité a un coût, je vous l'accorde. Il n'en demeure pas moins qu'elle attire un nouveau public." en="Free entry has a cost, I grant you. The fact remains that it attracts a new public." />

The strongest move is to *retourner l'argument*: you take the other side's point and show that it supports yours.

<Example fr="Vous dites que les musées manquent d'argent. Justement : plus de visiteurs, c'est plus de dons et plus de soutien public." en="You say museums are short of money. Exactly: more visitors means more donations and more public support." />

Use *concéder* to describe the move: *l'équipe adverse a concédé un point important*.

## Replying, not repeating

In the réplique, quote or summarize the other side's argument in one sentence, then answer it. As a rule of thumb, answer the strongest argument of the other side, not the weakest: the jury notices the difference.

<Example fr="Vous affirmez que la gratuité profite surtout aux visiteurs déjà habitués. Or, la fréquentation des jeunes a nettement augmenté." en="You claim that free entry mainly benefits regular visitors. But attendance among young people has clearly increased." />

*Or*, from the lesson on advanced connectors, introduces the fact that changes the reasoning.

## Keeping to time and to the rules

The moderator keeps order with the formulas you know from B2 (*laissez-le finir*, *revenons-en à la motion*). As a speaker, announce your plan in one sentence, keep one idea per minute, and finish before the moderator stops you. A conclusion cut off in the middle loses the debate.

## Formulas for each round

Each round has its own set phrases. Learn one or two for each, so that you never lose time looking for words.

- **To open**: *notre équipe défend la motion pour trois raisons*; *nous nous opposons à la motion, et voici pourquoi*.
- **To reply**: *vous affirmez que… or…*; *l'équipe adverse oublie que…*; *vous dites que… justement…*.
- **To concede and turn**: *je vous l'accorde… il n'en demeure pas moins que…*; *je vous rejoins sur ce point, mais…*.
- **To conclude**: *en définitive*, *pour toutes ces raisons*, *nous vous invitons à voter pour la motion*.

<Example fr="Pour toutes ces raisons, nous vous invitons à voter contre la motion." en="For all these reasons, we invite you to vote against the motion." />

In the conclusion, don't add a new argument: if you do, the other side has no time to answer it, and many juries count it against you. Repeat your strongest point in one sentence, and end with a clear request to the audience or the jury.

## An extract from a debate

<VocabTable group="topic" />

The debate below is an original text written for this lesson. The speakers are invented, and each side is given its strongest arguments.

*Motion : « Les musées nationaux doivent être gratuits. »*

- **La modératrice :** *Je donne la parole à l'équipe qui défend la motion. Vous avez trois minutes.*
- **Léa :** *Merci. Un musée, c'est un bien commun. Les collections appartiennent à tous ; pourquoi faudrait-il payer pour les voir ? La gratuité ouvre les portes à ceux qui n'entrent jamais : les jeunes, les familles modestes, les habitants qui n'y sont jamais allés.*
- **La modératrice :** *Merci. La parole est à l'équipe qui s'oppose à la motion.*
- **Karim :** *La culture doit être accessible, je vous l'accorde. Il n'en demeure pas moins qu'un musée a un coût : l'entretien des bâtiments, la conservation des collections, le personnel. Si l'entrée est gratuite, qui paiera ? Le contribuable, y compris celui qui ne va jamais au musée.*
- **La modératrice :** *Réplique de l'équipe qui défend la motion.*
- **Léa :** *Vous dites que les musées manquent d'argent. Justement : plus de visiteurs, c'est plus de dons et plus de soutien public. Or, là où l'entrée est devenue gratuite pour les jeunes, leur fréquentation a nettement augmenté.*
- **Karim :** *Je vous rejoins sur ce point : la gratuité pour les jeunes est une bonne mesure. Mais pour tous, elle profite surtout aux visiteurs qui venaient déjà. Une gratuité ciblée coûte moins et aide mieux.*
- **La modératrice :** *Il vous reste une minute chacun pour conclure.*
- **Léa :** *Pour toutes ces raisons, nous vous invitons à voter pour la motion : un musée gratuit est un musée ouvert à tous.*
- **Karim :** *Nous partageons le même but, mais pas le même moyen. Une gratuité ciblée protège les musées et aide ceux qui en ont besoin. Nous vous invitons donc à voter contre la motion.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Motion: "National museums should be free." The moderator: I give the floor to the team for the motion. You have three minutes. Léa: Thank you. A museum is a common good. The collections belong to everyone; why should people have to pay to see them? Free entry opens the doors to those who never go in: young people, families on low incomes, residents who have never been. The moderator: Thank you. The floor goes to the team against the motion. Karim: Culture must be accessible, I grant you. The fact remains that a museum has a cost: the upkeep of the buildings, the conservation of the collections, the staff. If entry is free, who will pay? The taxpayer, including the one who never goes to museums. The moderator: Rebuttal from the team for the motion. Léa: You say museums are short of money. Exactly: more visitors means more donations and more public support. And where entry has become free for young people, their attendance has clearly increased. Karim: I agree with you on that point: free entry for young people is a good measure. But for everyone, it mainly benefits visitors who already came. Targeted free entry costs less and helps better. The moderator: You each have one minute left to conclude. Léa: For all these reasons, we invite you to vote for the motion: a free museum is a museum open to all. Karim: We share the same goal, but not the same means. Targeted free entry protects museums and helps those who need it. We therefore invite you to vote against the motion.)

## How the debate works

- **The format**: the moderator gives the floor, sets the time (*trois minutes*, *une minute*) and announces each round.
- **The openings**: Léa uses a rhetorical question (*pourquoi faudrait-il payer… ?*) and a list of three groups; Karim lists three costs.
- **The concessions**: Karim grants a point (*je vous l'accorde*, *je vous rejoins sur ce point*) and turns it (*il n'en demeure pas moins que*, *mais*).
- **The réplique**: Léa turns Karim's argument round (*vous dites que… justement*) and adds a fact with *or*.
- **The conclusions**: each side repeats its strongest point and asks for the vote (*nous vous invitons à voter pour / contre la motion*), with no new argument.
- **The outcome**: the debate moves from two opposed positions to a narrower disagreement: free entry for everyone, or only for some groups.

## En français

After the debate, Léa and her teammate Hugo talk about how it went.

- *Alors, comment tu nous trouves ?* (So, how do you think we did?)
- *Ta réplique était très forte. Tu as retourné son argument.* (Your rebuttal was very strong. You turned his argument round.)
- *Oui, mais Karim a été très fort : il a concédé un point pour mieux revenir.* (Yes, but Karim was very good: he conceded a point to come back stronger.)
- *« Je vous rejoins sur ce point »… Le jury a aimé.* ("I agree with you on that point"… The jury liked it.)
- *Et ma conclusion ? J'ai dépassé le temps imparti ?* (And my conclusion? Did I go over the allotted time?)
- *De dix secondes. La modératrice t'a laissée finir.* (By ten seconds. The moderator let you finish.)
- *La prochaine fois, je prépare une conclusion plus courte.* (Next time, I'll prepare a shorter conclusion.)
- *Et réponds à son argument le plus fort, celui du contribuable.* (And answer his strongest argument, the one about the taxpayer.)
- *Tu as raison, je l'ai un peu évité.* (You're right, I avoided it a bit.)
- *Pourtant, tu avais la réponse : les dons et le soutien public.* (And yet you had the answer: donations and public support.)
- *Je vous l'accorde, Monsieur le Président !* (I grant you that, Mr President! (joking))
- *Très drôle. On s'entraîne demain ?* (Very funny. Shall we practise tomorrow? (spoken))

<Callout type="warning" title="Je vous l'accorde, not je vous accorde">
  Learners often drop the pronoun in this formula. *Accorder* needs its object: **je vous l'accorde**, where *l'* stands for the point you grant.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write their best argument first">
  Before a debate, write the other side's strongest argument in one sentence, then your answer to it. If you can't answer it, prepare a concession instead (*je vous l'accorde…, il n'en demeure pas moins que…*). You'll never be caught without a reply.
</Callout>

## What you already know

You presented data with *un graphique* and *une courbe* in <LessonLink id="b2-presenting-data" text="the lesson on presenting data" />, and took the floor with *prendre la parole* in <LessonLink id="b2-meetings-and-calls" text="the lesson on meetings and professional calls" />. You know *prononcer un discours* and *je vous remercie de votre attention* from earlier lessons, and *l'anaphore*, *la gradation* and *la question rhétorique* from <LessonLink id="c1-rhetorical-devices" text="the lesson on rhetorical devices" />. This lesson puts them into the shape of a speech.

## Addressing the audience

<VocabTable group="address" />

A French speech starts by addressing the audience. *Mesdames et messieurs* is the standard formal opening; for a closer group, *chers collègues* or *chers amis*. If there are official guests, name them first by title: *Madame la Maire, mesdames et messieurs*. *Une allocution* is a short formal speech, for example at an inauguration or a ceremony.

After the address comes *l'accroche* (the hook): a question, a short story, a striking fact. Its job is to *interpeller* the audience, to speak to them directly from the first sentence.

<Example fr="Mesdames et messieurs, combien d'entre vous ont lu un livre ce mois-ci ?" en="Ladies and gentlemen, how many of you have read a book this month?" />
<Example fr="L'intervenante a commencé par une accroche qui a interpellé toute la salle." en="The speaker began with a hook that spoke directly to the whole room." />

## Signposting

<VocabTable group="signpost" />

A listener can't go back a page. Signposts tell them where they are. Announce the plan at the start (*je commencerai par…, puis…, enfin…*), mark each move (*en premier lieu*, *j'en viens maintenant à*), and announce the end (*avant de conclure*, *permettez-moi de conclure*).

<Example fr="Je commencerai par l'histoire du projet, puis j'en viendrai à ses objectifs." en="I'll begin with the history of the project, then I'll come to its aims." />
<Example fr="J'en viens maintenant à la question du financement." en="I now come to the question of funding." />
<Example fr="Permettez-moi de conclure par une remarque personnelle." en="Allow me to conclude with a personal remark." />

As a rule of thumb, a speech needs more signposts than an essay: what a reader sees in the paragraphs, a listener needs to hear.

## Devices in a speech

The devices from the last lessons work best when they're heard. An anaphora in three sentences gives rhythm; a rhetorical question wakes the audience up; a gradation builds to the key point. Keep them for the moments that matter: the hook, the turning point and the end. A speech with a device in every sentence sounds like a parody.

## Delivery

<VocabTable group="deliver" />

How you say it matters as much as what you say. Speak more slowly than in conversation. *Marquer une pause* before an important sentence, and after it, so that it lands. Let *la voix* rise and fall: a flat voice loses the audience. Look at the room, not at your notes. If you use *le micro*, hold it at the same distance throughout.

Almost every speaker has *le trac* (stage fright). A practical answer is to learn the first two sentences by heart: once they're out, the rest follows.

<Example fr="Avant son allocution, elle avait le trac, mais sa voix est restée calme." en="Before her speech, she had stage fright, but her voice stayed calm." />

## Presentations at work

A presentation at work follows the same rules, with slides. Address the room with *chers collègues*, announce the plan, and signpost each slide. Don't read the slides aloud: the audience reads faster than you speak. One idea per slide, a short title, few words. Check *le micro* and the screen before you start.

<Example fr="Chers collègues, je commencerai par les chiffres de l'année, puis j'en viendrai aux projets." en="Dear colleagues, I'll begin with the year's figures, then I'll come to the projects." />
<Example fr="Avant de commencer, vérifiez que le micro fonctionne." en="Before you start, check that the microphone works." />

As a rule of thumb, plan one minute per slide, and keep the last slide for your message, not for "questions?".

## Ending well

The last sentence is the one the audience remembers. Don't end with *voilà* or *c'est tout*. Come back to your hook, sum up your message in one sentence, then thank the audience: *je vous remercie de votre attention*.

## An inauguration speech

The speech below is an original text written for this lesson. The town, the library and the speaker are invented.

*Allocution de la directrice pour l'ouverture de la médiathèque de Saint-Aubin*

*Madame la Maire, mesdames et messieurs, chers amis,*

*Combien d'entre vous ont lu un livre ce mois-ci ? Levez la main… Je vois beaucoup de mains, et c'est une bonne nouvelle, car ce soir, nous ouvrons un lieu fait pour vous.*

*Je commencerai par l'histoire de ce projet, puis j'en viendrai à ce que nous voulons en faire.*

*En premier lieu, je voudrais remercier toutes les personnes qui ont rendu ce projet possible : les architectes, les ouvriers, les bibliothécaires, et les habitants qui ont donné leur avis pendant trois ans. Je pense aussi à mon équipe, qui travaille depuis des mois pour ouvrir à temps.*

*Ce bâtiment a été pensé pour tous : il est calme, plein de lumière, et il accueillera aussi des concerts.*

*J'en viens maintenant à notre projet. Nous voulons une médiathèque ouverte. Nous voulons une médiathèque vivante. Nous voulons une médiathèque pour tous : pour les enfants qui découvrent leurs premiers livres, pour les étudiants qui préparent leurs examens, pour les personnes âgées qui cherchent un lieu de rencontre. La lecture publique n'est pas un luxe : c'est un droit.*

*Avant de conclure, un mot sur les horaires. La médiathèque sera ouverte six jours sur sept, et le dimanche après-midi dès le mois de septembre.*

*Permettez-moi de conclure par une question. Le mois prochain, combien d'entre vous auront lu un livre de plus ? J'espère que la réponse sera : tous.*

*Je vous remercie de votre attention, et à très bientôt à la médiathèque !*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Speech by the director at the opening of the Saint-Aubin library. Madam Mayor, ladies and gentlemen, dear friends, How many of you have read a book this month? Raise your hand… I can see a lot of hands, and that's good news, because tonight we are opening a place made for you. I'll begin with the history of this project, then I'll come to what we want to make of it. First of all, I would like to thank everyone who made this project possible: the architects, the builders, the librarians, and the residents who gave their views for three years. I'm also thinking of my team, who have been working for months to open on time. This building was designed for everyone: it is quiet, full of light, and it will also host concerts. I now come to our project. We want an open library. We want a lively library. We want a library for everyone: for children discovering their first books, for students preparing their exams, for older people looking for a meeting place. Public libraries aren't a luxury: they're a right. Before I conclude, a word about opening hours. The library will be open six days a week, and on Sunday afternoons from September. Allow me to conclude with a question. Next month, how many of you will have read one more book? I hope the answer will be: all of you. Thank you for your attention, and see you very soon at the library!)

## How the speech works

- **Address**: *Madame la Maire* first, then *mesdames et messieurs, chers amis*.
- **Hook**: a question that interpellates the audience, with a gesture (*levez la main*).
- **Plan**: announced in one sentence (*je commencerai par…, puis j'en viendrai à…*).
- **Signposts**: *en premier lieu*, *j'en viens maintenant à*, *avant de conclure*, *permettez-moi de conclure*.
- **Devices**: an anaphora (*nous voulons…*, three times), a list in three, and an antithesis (*n'est pas un luxe : c'est un droit*).
- **Ending**: the hook comes back as a question, then the thanks.

## En français

The director, Madame Morel, rehearses the speech with her colleague Julien.

- *Alors, tu peux m'écouter ? J'ai un peu le trac.* (So, can you listen to me? I'm a bit nervous.)
- *Bien sûr. Commence quand tu veux.* (Of course. Start whenever you like.)
- *« Madame la Maire, mesdames et messieurs, chers amis… »* ("Madam Mayor, ladies and gentlemen, dear friends…")
- *Très bien. Et ton accroche ?* (Very good. And your hook?)
- *Une question au public : « Combien d'entre vous ont lu un livre ce mois-ci ? »* (A question to the audience: "How many of you have read a book this month?")
- *Parfait. Marque une pause après, laisse-les répondre.* (Perfect. Pause afterwards, let them answer.)
- *Ensuite, j'annonce le plan, puis je remercie tout le monde.* (Then I announce the plan, then I thank everyone.)
- *Ta voix descend à la fin des phrases. Parle un peu plus fort.* (Your voice drops at the end of the sentences. Speak a bit louder.)
- *D'accord. Et l'anaphore, « nous voulons », ce n'est pas trop ?* (OK. And the anaphora, "nous voulons", isn't it too much?)
- *Non, trois fois, c'est juste. Mais regarde la salle, pas tes notes.* (No, three times is right. But look at the room, not at your notes.)
- *Et pour finir, je reviens à ma question.* (And to finish, I come back to my question.)
- *C'est la meilleure idée du discours. Ils vont s'en souvenir.* (That's the best idea in the speech. They'll remember it.)

<Callout type="warning" title="J'en viens à la question, not j'en viens de la question">
  Learners often use *de* after *venir*, as in *je viens de Lyon*. To move to a new point in a speech, the construction is **en venir à**: **j'en viens maintenant à la question du budget**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn the first two sentences by heart">
  Before any speech, learn your address and your hook by heart, word for word. Nerves are strongest in the first thirty seconds; once those sentences are out, the rest of the speech, read from short notes, follows much more easily.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize irony and understatement in spoken and written French, understand what speakers really mean, and decide when not to use irony yourself.

## What you already know

You know *la litote* and *l'hyperbole* from <LessonLink id="c1-rhetorical-devices" text="the lesson on rhetorical devices" />, and spoken features such as *quoi* and dropped *ne* from <LessonLink id="b2-colloquial-french" text="the lesson on spoken, everyday French" />. You also know *se moquer de* from <LessonLink id="b1-relative-dont" text="the lesson on dont" />. This lesson is about meaning that isn't in the words.

## Irony

<VocabTable group="irony" />

*L'ironie* says one thing and means another, usually the opposite, to criticize or to amuse. Its simplest form is *l'antiphrase*: praise that is really blame.

<Example fr="Cette phrase est une antiphrase : elle dit le contraire de ce que pense la personne qui parle." en="This sentence is an antiphrasis: it says the opposite of what the speaker thinks." /> A sentence *au second degré* isn't meant literally; a listener who *prend au premier degré* misses the point, and may even be *vexé*.

<Example fr="Quel voyage agréable !" en="What a pleasant journey! (ironic, after a three-hour delay)" />
<Example fr="Bravo, quelle organisation !" en="Well done, what organization! (ironic, after a disaster)" />
<Example fr="Il a pris ma remarque au premier degré et il s'est vexé." en="He took my remark literally and was offended." />

*Pince-sans-rire* describes someone who makes jokes with a straight face, or that style of humour. It's admired in French conversation, and it's also the hardest to read for a learner.

## Understatement in everyday French

<VocabTable group="less" />

French conversation uses understatement constantly, and many phrases are fixed. They work like the litote: they say less, and mean more.

- **Ce n'est pas mal** means "it's good", often "it's very good".
- **Pas fameux** and **ce n'est pas terrible** mean "not great", often "bad". *Terrible* here is a false friend: *pas terrible* doesn't mean "not awful".
- **Il ne fait pas chaud** means "it's cold".
- **Je ne dis pas non** means "yes, please".

<Example fr="Ton gâteau ? Ce n'est pas mal du tout !" en="Your cake? It's really good!" />
<Example fr="Le film n'était pas terrible." en="The film wasn't great." />
<Example fr="Un café ? Je ne dis pas non." en="A coffee? I wouldn't say no." />

The same words can be sincere or ironic: *ce n'est pas mal* about a burnt cake is ironic. Only the context decides.

<Example fr="Ce n'est pas terrible, ce film." en="This film isn't great. (spoken)" />

## Reading the signals

<VocabTable group="signals" />

In speech, *le ton* (the tone) carries the irony: a flat voice, a pause, a look. In writing, readers rely on other signals:

- **A mismatch with the facts**: praise for something that obviously went wrong.
- **Exaggeration**: *le meilleur voyage de ma vie* after a breakdown.
- **Set words**: *évidemment*, *comme d'habitude*, *quelle chance*.
- **Typography**: *les guillemets* around a word (*une « petite » panne*) or *un émoji* at the end of a message.

<Example fr="Les guillemets montrent souvent l'ironie à l'écrit." en="Quotation marks often show irony in writing." />
<Example fr="Un émoji à la fin du message montre que c'est une blague." en="An emoji at the end of the message shows it's a joke." />

<Example fr="Le train a encore eu une « petite » panne de trois heures, comme d'habitude." en="The train had another \"little\" three-hour breakdown, as usual. (ironic)" />

## Why irony fails in writing

Without the voice, many readers take irony *au premier degré*. In an email to a colleague, *bravo pour ta ponctualité* after a late report can be read as sincere, or as an attack. As a rule of thumb, avoid irony in professional writing and with people you don't know well, and say the criticism plainly and politely. Keep irony for friends, and for texts where the context makes it obvious: a humorous column, a sketch, a blog post.

<Example fr="Le rapport est arrivé avec deux jours de retard." en="The report arrived two days late. (plain and safe)" />

## Answering irony

When someone is ironic with you, you have three choices. You can show that you understood by answering in the same tone: *ah oui, vraiment inoubliable !*. You can answer the real meaning: *je suis désolé pour le retard*. Or, if you're not sure, you can ask: *tu es sérieux ?*, *c'est au second degré ?*. Asking is always safer than taking a remark *au premier degré* and being hurt by it.

<Example fr="Tu es sérieux, ou c'est au second degré ?" en="Are you serious, or is that tongue in cheek?" />

In France, irony and understatement are part of everyday humour among friends, between colleagues who know each other, and in the media. They aren't a sign of hostility. As a rule of thumb, a learner should recognize all of them and use the fixed understatements (*ce n'est pas mal*, *je ne dis pas non*) freely, but keep real irony for people who know you well.

## A humorous review

This restaurant review is an original text written for this lesson, in the style of a humorous blog. The restaurant is invented. Most of the text is ironic: the translation marks it.

*Sur notre blog : Le Petit Gourmet, une soirée inoubliable*

*Samedi soir, nous avons enfin testé Le Petit Gourmet, le restaurant dont tout le quartier parle. Quelle soirée !*

*D'abord, l'accueil. Nous avions réservé pour vingt heures ; on nous a installés à vingt et une heures, ce qui nous a laissé tout le temps d'admirer la porte d'entrée. Une attention délicate, vraiment.*

*Ensuite, la carte. Le chef propose une « cuisine créative ». En effet, notre poisson était créatif : froid à l'extérieur, encore plus froid à l'intérieur. Le serveur, très pince-sans-rire, nous a expliqué que c'était voulu. Nous l'avons remercié, évidemment. Il est revenu trois fois pour nous demander si tout allait bien. Tout allait très bien, merci : nous attendions toujours le plat suivant.*

*Le cadre, lui aussi, mérite quelques mots. La musique était si forte que nous avons pu suivre les conversations de toutes les tables voisines. Quel plaisir de partager la vie des autres !*

*Le pain n'était pas fameux, mais il était au moins frais. Nous avons beaucoup ri, surtout en attendant le plat suivant.*

*Le dessert, lui, n'était pas mal du tout : une tarte aux pommes simple et chaude. Le chef sait donc cuisiner quand il veut.*

*L'addition ? Il ne fait pas chaud dans ce restaurant, mais les prix, eux, sont brûlants.*

*Bref, une soirée inoubliable, que nous recommandons à tous ceux qui ont beaucoup de patience. Nous y retournerons… peut-être, quand nous aurons oublié le poisson. Et si vous aimez attendre, je ne dis pas non : réservez pour nous aussi.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(On our blog: Le Petit Gourmet, an unforgettable evening (ironic). On Saturday evening, we finally tried Le Petit Gourmet, the restaurant the whole neighbourhood is talking about. What an evening! (ironic) First, the welcome. We had booked for eight o'clock; we were seated at nine, which gave us plenty of time to admire the front door. A thoughtful touch, really. (ironic) Next, the menu. The chef offers "creative cooking". Indeed, our fish was creative: cold on the outside, even colder on the inside. (ironic) The waiter, very deadpan, explained that it was intentional. We thanked him, of course. (ironic) He came back three times to ask if everything was all right. Everything was fine, thank you: we were still waiting for the next course. (ironic) The setting, too, deserves a few words. The music was so loud that we could follow the conversations at all the neighbouring tables. What a pleasure to share other people's lives! (ironic) The bread wasn't great, but at least it was fresh. (sincere) We laughed a lot, especially while waiting for the next course. The dessert, on the other hand, was really good: a simple, warm apple tart. (sincere) So the chef can cook when he wants to. The bill? It's not warm in this restaurant, but the prices are scorching. Anyway, an unforgettable evening, which we recommend to everyone with a great deal of patience. (ironic) We'll go back… perhaps, once we've forgotten the fish. And if you like waiting, I wouldn't say no: book for us too.)

## How the review works

- **Antiphrasis**: the title and the first lines praise a bad evening (*une soirée inoubliable*, *quelle soirée !*).
- **Mismatch with the facts**: *une attention délicate* for an hour's wait, *tout allait très bien* while the food didn't come, *quel plaisir* for loud music.
- **Quotation marks**: *une « cuisine créative »*.
- **A deadpan character**: *le serveur, très pince-sans-rire*.
- **Set words**: *évidemment*.
- **Understatement used sincerely**: *n'était pas fameux* for the bread, and *n'était pas mal du tout* for the one good dish.
- **Understatement used ironically**: *il ne fait pas chaud* (the room is cold) and *je ne dis pas non* at the end.

## En français

Emma shows the review to her friend Marc, who isn't French.

- *Tu as lu la critique du Petit Gourmet ? Elle est géniale.* (Have you read the review of Le Petit Gourmet? It's brilliant.)
- *Oui, j'ai même réservé : ils disent que c'est une soirée inoubliable !* (Yes, I've even booked: they say it's an unforgettable evening!)
- *Marc… tu l'as prise au premier degré !* (Marc… you took it literally!)
- *Comment ça ?* (What do you mean?)
- *C'est de l'ironie. Ils ont attendu une heure, et le poisson était froid.* (It's irony. They waited an hour, and the fish was cold.)
- *Mais ils écrivent « une attention délicate » !* (But they write "a thoughtful touch"!)
- *Justement. C'est le contraire de ce qu'ils pensent.* (Exactly. It's the opposite of what they think.)
- *Et « le dessert n'était pas mal », c'est ironique aussi ?* (And "the dessert wasn't bad", is that ironic too?)
- *Non, là, c'est sincère : ça veut dire que c'était bon.* (No, that one's sincere: it means it was good.)
- *Le français, ce n'est pas simple !* (French isn't simple! (understatement))
- *Tu vois, toi aussi tu fais des litotes. Tu annules ta réservation ?* (You see, you're using litotes too. Are you cancelling your booking?)
- *Je ne dis pas non.* (I wouldn't say no.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Pas terrible means not great">
  Learners often read *pas terrible* as "not terrible", that is, fine. It means the opposite: **le film n'était pas terrible** is a criticism. To praise, say **ce n'était pas mal** or **c'était très bien**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Check the facts against the words">
  When you suspect irony, compare the words with the facts around them. If the words praise and the facts are bad, it's irony; if they match, it's sincere. In the review above, *pas mal* matches a good dessert, so it's sincere.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to describe the main French traditions of satire and humour, recognize the kinds of wordplay French loves, and explain why French humour is hard to translate.

## What you already know

You know *la presse satirique*, *un dessin de presse* and *la caricature* from <LessonLink id="b2-culture-french-press" text="the lesson on the French press" />, *un humoriste* from <LessonLink id="b2-arts-and-literature" text="the lesson on arts and literature" />, and *se moquer de* from <LessonLink id="b1-relative-dont" text="the lesson on dont" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-irony-and-understatement" text="the lesson on irony and understatement" />, you learned to read irony. This lesson places it in a long tradition.

## A long tradition

<VocabTable group="tradition" />

France has a long tradition of *la satire*: humour that *tourne en ridicule* the powerful, the pretentious and the hypocritical. *Le rire* is seen as a way to criticize without giving a lecture.

**Les fables.** Jean de La Fontaine (1621–1695) published his *Fables* from 1668. In short stories in verse, animals behave like people, and each fable ends with *une morale*. In *Le Loup et l'Agneau*, a wolf finds a pretext to eat a lamb; the moral is famous.

<Example fr="« La raison du plus fort est toujours la meilleure. » (La Fontaine)" en="\"The argument of the strongest is always the best.\" (our translation)" />

Behind the animals, readers recognize the court and the powerful of the time.

**La comédie.** Molière (1622–1673) wrote comedies that laugh at *l'hypocrisie*, false learning and social pretensions: the religious hypocrite in *Tartuffe*, the doctors who don't cure anybody, the snobs who imitate the nobility.

<Example fr="Molière tourne en ridicule l'hypocrisie de son temps." en="Molière holds up the hypocrisy of his time to ridicule." />

**Le conte philosophique.** In the eighteenth century, Voltaire used the *conte philosophique* to mock war, fanaticism and blind optimism. *Candide* (1759) is the best-known example: a naive young man discovers that the world is far from "the best of all possible worlds".

## Satire today

<VocabTable group="modern" />

**La presse satirique.** You met it at B2. *Le Canard enchaîné*, a satirical weekly founded in 1915, combines investigation and humour. Satirical papers rely on *le dessin de presse*, where a single cartoon comments on the news.

**Le sketch et le stand-up.** Comedians write *sketches*, short comic scenes, for the stage, the radio and television. Since the early 2000s, *le stand-up*, imported from English-speaking countries, has become very popular, especially among young audiences. Many comedians use *l'autodérision*: they laugh at themselves before they laugh at others.

<Example fr="Cet humoriste pratique beaucoup l'autodérision." en="This comedian uses a lot of self-mockery." />

As at B2, this lesson describes the tradition; it doesn't judge any particular paper or artist.

## Playing with words

<VocabTable group="words" />

French humour loves *les jeux de mots*. *Un calembour* plays on two meanings of the same sound: many French words sound alike, which makes puns easy. *Une contrepèterie* swaps sounds between two words to create a second meaning, often cheeky; the reader has to find it. *Un sous-entendu* suggests something without saying it.

<Example fr="Ce calembour repose sur deux mots qui se prononcent de la même façon." en="This pun relies on two words that are pronounced the same way." />
<Example fr="Le sous-entendu est clair, même si personne ne le dit." en="The implication is clear, even though nobody says it." />

## Why humour is hard to translate

Puns and spoonerisms depend on the sounds of one language: they're often *intraduisibles*. Satire depends on references a foreigner may not share: a politician's habits, an advertisement, a regional accent. Irony depends on tone. When you watch French comedy, expect to miss some jokes at first; it's a sign of how much of humour is culture, not language.

<Example fr="Ce jeu de mots est intraduisible : il n'existe qu'en français." en="This play on words is untranslatable: it only exists in French." />

## Fables in everyday French

La Fontaine's morals are still part of everyday French. People quote them, or half-quote them, without always knowing where they come from. *La raison du plus fort* is used to criticize any abuse of power; other fables gave French its images of the clever fox and the proud crow.

<Example fr="C'est encore la raison du plus fort : la grande entreprise gagne toujours." en="It's the law of the strongest again: the big company always wins." />

Knowing the best-known fables helps you catch these references in the news and in conversation, where a single word can call up a whole story.

## Humour and register

French humour moves easily between registers. A satirical paper can use very formal French to describe something absurd, and the contrast itself is funny. A sketch can put the language of an official letter into the mouth of a child. When you read satire, ask which register the writer is imitating, and why: the joke is often there.

<Example fr="Le journal décrit une simple panne de café avec le ton d'une crise d'État." en="The paper describes a simple coffee-machine breakdown in the tone of a national crisis." />

As a learner, enjoy satire as a reader, and be careful as a writer: satire that targets a group of people, rather than the powerful, quickly stops being funny for the people concerned.

## A short article

The article below is an original text written for this lesson.

*Rire du pouvoir : une tradition française*

*En France, on aime rire des puissants. Cette tradition est vieille. Il y a plus de trois cents ans, La Fontaine faisait parler des animaux : derrière le loup, le lion ou le renard, ses lecteurs reconnaissaient la cour et ses mœurs. On lit encore ses fables à l'école aujourd'hui. À la même époque, Molière tournait en ridicule l'hypocrisie et les médecins de son temps, et le roi lui-même assistait à ses comédies. Le public s'amusait, mais il réfléchissait aussi.*

*Au siècle suivant, Voltaire choisit le conte philosophique. Sous une forme légère, il critique la guerre, le fanatisme et ceux qui pensent que tout va pour le mieux.*

*Aujourd'hui, la tradition continue sous d'autres formes. La presse satirique commente l'actualité avec des dessins et des jeux de mots. Chaque semaine, les lecteurs retrouvent ainsi des personnages qu'ils connaissent bien, toujours un peu ridicules. À la radio et à la télévision, les humoristes imitent les responsables politiques et se moquent des petites habitudes de chacun. Sur scène, le stand-up a trouvé un jeune public, qui apprécie l'autodérision autant que la critique.*

*Ce rire a ses règles. Il vise plutôt les puissants que les faibles, et il suppose un public complice, qui comprend les sous-entendus. C'est pourquoi l'humour voyage mal : un calembour ou une contrepèterie reste souvent intraduisible, et une allusion à l'actualité peut tomber à plat dans un autre pays.*

*Rire du pouvoir n'est pas toujours facile. Mais en France, on pense souvent que la liberté de rire fait partie de la liberté d'expression.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Laughing at power: a French tradition. In France, people like to laugh at the powerful. This tradition is old. More than three hundred years ago, La Fontaine made animals speak: behind the wolf, the lion or the fox, his readers recognized the court and its customs. His fables are still read at school today. At the same time, Molière made fun of hypocrisy and the doctors of his time, and the king himself attended his comedies. The audience enjoyed itself, but it also thought. In the following century, Voltaire chose the philosophical tale. In a light form, he criticizes war, fanaticism and those who think that all is for the best. Today, the tradition continues in other forms. The satirical press comments on the news with cartoons and wordplay. Every week, readers find characters they know well, always a little ridiculous. On radio and television, comedians imitate politicians and make fun of everyone's little habits. On stage, stand-up has found a young audience, which enjoys self-mockery as much as criticism. This laughter has its rules. It targets the powerful rather than the weak, and it assumes an audience in on the joke, who understands the innuendo. That's why humour travels badly: a pun or a spoonerism often remains untranslatable, and a reference to the news can fall flat in another country. Laughing at power isn't always easy. But in France, people often think that the freedom to laugh is part of freedom of expression.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une morale, not un moral">
  Learners often confuse the two nouns. The lesson at the end of a fable is feminine: **la morale de la fable**. *Le moral* (masculine) is your mood: *avoir le moral*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Start with La Fontaine">
  If you want to understand French humour from its roots, read two or three of La Fontaine's *Fables* in a modern edition with notes. They're short, they're quoted constantly in everyday French, and each one shows how a story can criticize without naming anyone.
</Callout>

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words describe the speaker and the speech. *Un orateur* or *une oratrice* is someone who speaks in public, and a good one knows how to *captiver* an audience. *Un plaidoyer* is a speech that defends a cause. *Un argument d'autorité* relies on an expert or an official source rather than on reasoning: it's useful, but a careful listener checks the source. You know *convaincant* from <LessonLink id="b1-review-opinions" text="the review of opinions" />.

## Building a persuasive speech

A short persuasive speech combines the tools of the whole unit. As a rule of thumb, follow this plan:

1. **Address and hook**: *mesdames et messieurs*, then a rhetorical question that interpellates the audience.
2. **The plan**, in one sentence, with signposts: *je commencerai par…, puis j'en viendrai à…*.
3. **Your main argument**, supported by a fact or an argument from authority.
4. **The strategic concession**: grant the other side's best point, then turn it. A fronted *que* clause works well here: *que…, je l'admets. Il n'en demeure pas moins que…*.
5. **Your proposal**, made memorable with one device, such as an anaphora.
6. **The ending**: *permettez-moi de conclure*, a final question or contrast, and the thanks.

Choose three devices at most, and place them at the hook, the proposal and the end. Between them, keep the sentences plain and factual: the devices stand out only if the rest of the speech is sober, and plain facts make your claims easier to believe. A concession placed before your proposal makes the proposal easier to accept: the audience has seen that you listened.

## A model speech

This speech is an original text written for this lesson. The town, the market and the speaker are invented.

*Discours de Madame Leroy, au nom des commerçants du marché, lors d'une réunion publique*

*Mesdames et messieurs, chers amis,*

*Qui d'entre vous n'a jamais fait ses courses au marché du samedi ? Je vois que personne ne lève la main.*

*Je commencerai par ce que le marché nous apporte, puis j'en viendrai aux critiques que nous avons entendues.*

*Ce marché existe depuis plus de cent ans, et il fait partie de l'histoire de notre ville. Le marché, c'est d'abord un lieu de rencontre. On y vient pour acheter, mais aussi pour parler et pour retrouver ses voisins. Une étude de la mairie elle-même montre que le samedi, le centre-ville accueille deux fois plus de visiteurs que les autres jours. Le samedi, la place est pleine de monde, et les commerçants du centre vendent plus ce jour-là.*

*Que le marché pose des problèmes de circulation, je l'admets. Que certains habitants soient gênés par le bruit, je le comprends. Nous avons entendu ces critiques, et elles sont légitimes. Il n'en demeure pas moins que le déplacer loin du centre serait une erreur. Ce ne serait pas une petite perte : ce serait la fin d'une tradition.*

*J'en viens maintenant à nos solutions. Nous proposons de commencer plus tard, à huit heures, et de libérer la place à treize heures. Nous proposons de nettoyer la place nous-mêmes. Nous proposons d'en parler tous ensemble, avant toute décision.*

*Alors, permettez-moi de conclure par une question simple : voulons-nous un centre-ville vivant, ou un centre-ville vide ?*

*Je vous remercie de votre attention.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Speech by Madame Leroy, on behalf of the market traders, at a public meeting. Ladies and gentlemen, dear friends, Which of you has never done their shopping at the Saturday market? I see that nobody raises a hand. I'll begin with what the market brings us, then I'll come to the criticisms we have heard. This market has existed for more than a hundred years, and it is part of our town's history. The market is first of all a meeting place. People come to buy, but also to talk and to see their neighbours. A study by the town hall itself shows that on Saturdays the town centre receives twice as many visitors as on other days. On Saturdays, the square is full of people, and the shops in the centre sell more that day. That the market causes traffic problems, I admit. That some residents are bothered by the noise, I understand. We have heard these criticisms, and they are legitimate. The fact remains that moving it away from the centre would be a mistake. It would be no small loss: it would be the end of a tradition. I now come to our solutions. We propose to start later, at eight, and to clear the square at one. We propose to clean the square ourselves. We propose to talk about it all together, before any decision. So allow me to conclude with a simple question: do we want a lively town centre, or an empty one? Thank you for your attention.)

## How the speech uses the unit

- **Speech structure**: address (*mesdames et messieurs, chers amis*), a hook, the plan with signposts (*je commencerai par…, puis j'en viendrai à…*, *j'en viens maintenant à*), *permettez-moi de conclure*, the thanks.
- **Three devices**: a rhetorical question at the hook and at the end (*qui d'entre vous n'a jamais… ?*, *voulons-nous un centre-ville vivant, ou un centre-ville vide ?*), an anaphora for the proposal (*nous proposons…*, three times), and a litote (*ce ne serait pas une petite perte*).
- **An argument from authority**: *une étude de la mairie elle-même montre que…*.
- **One strategic concession**, before the proposal: two fronted *que* clauses with the subjunctive (*que le marché pose…, je l'admets*; *que certains habitants soient gênés…, je le comprends*), then the turn with *il n'en demeure pas moins que*.

## Your turn

Write a short persuasive speech of eight to ten sentences for a cause you care about: a park, a school project, a local festival. Follow the six-step plan. Use exactly three devices, one strategic concession with a fronted *que* clause and *il n'en demeure pas moins que*, and one argument from authority. Then read it aloud, marking your pauses, and check that someone hearing it once could repeat your main proposal.

## Check your work

- Does the speech open with an address and a hook, and announce its plan?
- Is the verb in the fronted *que* clause in the subjunctive, with a form that differs from the indicative where possible (*soient*, *ait*, *fasse*)?
- Does the concession come before your proposal, and does it turn with *il n'en demeure pas moins que* or *mais*?
- Did you use no more than three devices, each at a key moment?
- Does the end come back to the hook, and thank the audience?

<Callout type="warning" title="Que le marché pose des problèmes, je l'admets: don't forget le">
  Learners who front a *que* clause often leave the main verb without its object. The clause must be taken up by a pronoun: **que le marché pose des problèmes, je l'admets**, **qu'ils soient gênés, je le comprends**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Record yourself once">
  Record your speech on your phone and listen to it the next day, as a member of the audience. You'll hear at once where a pause is missing, where a device sounds forced, and whether the ending really lands.
</Callout>
