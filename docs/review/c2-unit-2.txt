By the end of this lesson you'll be able to tell apart nine pairs of French words that look or sound alike, and to choose the right one in a formal text.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-false-friends" text="the lesson on false friends" />, you learned French words that look like English words but mean something else. In <LessonLink id="c1-precise-verbs" text="the lesson on precise verbs" />, you replaced general verbs with exact ones. You also know *la conjoncture* (the economic situation) from <LessonLink id="b2-economy-and-business" text="the lesson on the economy and business" />. This lesson deals with a different trap: two French words that look alike and are confused by French writers too.

## What a paronym is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

The term *un paronyme* (a paronym) is a word that looks or sounds almost like another, with a different meaning. Paronyms aren't a problem of translation but of precision: a native writer can *confondre* (confuse) *éminent* and *imminent* in a hurry, and a reader notices at once. The only reliable method is to attach each word to the things it usually goes with.

<Example fr="Même de bons journalistes confondent parfois éminent et imminent." en="Even good journalists sometimes confuse éminent and imminent." />

For each pair below, learn the two words together, each with one typical partner.

## Éminent or imminent? Prodige or prodigue?

<VocabTable group="people" />

The word *éminent* describes a person who stands out: *un éminent chercheur*, *une éminente juriste*. *Imminent* describes an event that is about to happen: *un départ imminent*, *une décision imminente*.

<Example fr="Une éminente spécialiste du climat présentera ses travaux demain." en="An eminent climate specialist will present her work tomorrow." />

<Example fr="Selon le directeur, une décision est imminente." en="According to the director, a decision is imminent." />

The term *un prodige* is a person with an exceptional gift, often a child, or something extraordinary: *un enfant prodige*. *Prodigue* is an adjective: someone who spends without counting, or who gives generously. *Être prodigue de promesses* is to make promises freely, often too freely.

<Example fr="À huit ans, cette musicienne était déjà un prodige." en="At eight, this musician was already a prodigy." />

<Example fr="Le candidat a été prodigue de promesses pendant toute la campagne." en="The candidate was lavish with promises throughout the campaign." />

## Conjecture, perpétrer and collusion

<VocabTable group="events" />

The term *une conjecture* is a guess, an idea that isn't proved: *ce ne sont que des conjectures*. *La conjoncture*, which you know, is the situation at a given moment, especially the economic one: *la conjoncture est difficile*.

<Example fr="Sans preuves, la police en est réduite aux conjectures." en="Without evidence, the police can only guess." />

The word *perpétrer* is used almost only with a crime or an attack: *perpétrer un vol*, *un attentat perpétré la nuit dernière*. *Perpétuer* means to keep something alive over time: *perpétuer une tradition*, *perpétuer un souvenir*.

<Example fr="La famille perpétue la même tradition depuis quatre générations." en="The family has kept up the same tradition for four generations." />

The term *une collision* is a physical crash, between vehicles or objects. *Une collusion* is a secret agreement between people who should be independent, for example a company and the person who controls it.

<Example fr="La collision entre le bus et la voiture n'a fait aucun blessé." en="The collision between the bus and the car caused no injuries." />

<Example fr="L'enquête doit dire s'il y a eu collusion entre l'entreprise et un élu." en="The investigation must say whether there was collusion between the company and an elected official." />

## Effraction or infraction?

<VocabTable group="law" />

The term *une effraction* is a break-in: the act of forcing a door, a lock or a window. It appears above all in *entrer par effraction* (to break in). *Une infraction* is any breach of a law or a rule, from a parking offence to a serious crime: *une infraction au code de la route* (a traffic offence).

<Example fr="Le cambrioleur est entré par effraction pendant la nuit." en="The burglar broke in during the night." />

<Example fr="Rouler trop vite en ville est une infraction." en="Driving too fast in town is an offence." />

A burglary can involve both words: *le cambrioleur a commis une infraction en entrant par effraction* (the burglar committed an offence by breaking in).

## Infecter, recouvrer and acception

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

The word *infecter* is what a germ does to a wound or a body, or a virus to a computer. *Infester* is what pests or bandits do to a place: *une cave infestée de rats*.

<Example fr="Le virus a infecté tous les ordinateurs du service." en="The virus infected all the computers in the department." />

The word *recouvrer* means to get back something you had lost: *recouvrer la santé*, *recouvrer la liberté*, *recouvrer la vue*, and, in finance, *recouvrer une somme* (to recover a sum). *Recouvrir* means to cover entirely, and it's conjugated like *couvrir*: *la neige a recouvert la route*. The two are easy to confuse in the passé composé, because *il a recouvré* and *il a recouvert* look close.

<Example fr="Après des mois à l'hôpital, elle a recouvré toutes ses forces." en="After months in hospital, she got all her strength back." />

The term *une acception* is one of the senses of a word, as in a dictionary: *dans son acception juridique*. *Une acceptation* is the act of accepting: *l'acceptation d'une offre*.

<Example fr="Dans cette acception, le mot « note » veut dire une facture." en="In this sense, the word 'note' means a bill." />

## The model: an article with five paronyms wrong

The article below is original, about an invented town. It uses ten paronyms. Five are correct, and five are wrong, as a hurried writer might get them. Find the five before you read the corrections.

*Dans la nuit de samedi, des cambrioleurs sont entrés par infraction dans la mairie de Valbrune. Selon un éminent spécialiste de la sécurité, le vol a été perpétué en moins de dix minutes. La police écarte pour l'instant toute collusion entre les cambrioleurs et le personnel, mais une arrestation semble éminente.*

*Dans la conjecture actuelle, la mairie ne peut pas payer de nouvelles portes. Le maire, qui a recouvert son calme, a déclaré que la ville perpétuerait la tradition de la fête de mai et que les caves de la mairie, infestées de rats, seraient enfin nettoyées. L'opposition, elle, reproche au maire d'être prodigue de promesses.*

(Corrected version, in translation: On Saturday night, burglars broke into the town hall of Valbrune. According to an eminent security specialist, the theft was committed in under ten minutes. For now, the police rule out any collusion between the burglars and the staff, but an arrest seems imminent. In the current economic situation, the town hall can't pay for new doors. The mayor, who has regained his composure, said that the town would keep up the tradition of the May festival and that the town hall cellars, infested with rats, would finally be cleaned. The opposition, for its part, accuses the mayor of being lavish with promises.)

## The five corrections

- In the first sentence, *par infraction* should be **par effraction**: the burglars forced their way in.
- In the second, *le vol a été perpétué* should be **perpétré**: a theft is committed, not kept alive.
- At the end of the first paragraph, *une arrestation semble éminente* should be **imminente**: the arrest is about to happen.
- At the start of the second paragraph, *dans la conjecture actuelle* should be **conjoncture**: the economic situation, not a guess.
- In the next sentence, *qui a recouvert son calme* should be **recouvré**: the mayor got his calm back.

The five correct uses are *un éminent spécialiste*, *toute collusion*, *perpétuerait la tradition*, *infestées de rats* and *prodigue de promesses*.

## En français

Lina, a trainee lawyer, asks Bastien, her supervisor, to check a report. They use *tu*.

- *Bastien, j'ai écrit « une infraction au code de la route ». C'est le bon mot ?* (Bastien, I wrote "une infraction au code de la route". Is that the right word?)
- *Oui. Une infraction, c'est le fait de ne pas respecter une règle.* (Yes. An infraction is not respecting a rule.)
- *Et pour le client qui a forcé la porte de son voisin ?* (And for the client who forced his neighbour's door?)
- *Là, c'est une effraction. Il est entré par effraction.* (That's a break-in. He broke in.)
- *Le voisin demande à recouvrer la somme qu'il a payée pour la porte.* (The neighbour is asking to recover the sum he paid for the door.)
- *Oui, recouvrer, en -er. Pas recouvrir, en -ir, qui veut dire couvrir.* (Yes, recouvrer, ending in -er. Not recouvrir, ending in -ir, which means to cover.)
- *J'ai aussi une phrase sur l'acception du mot « voisin » dans la loi.* (I also have a sentence on the sense of the word "voisin" in the law.)
- *C'est juste : une acception, c'est un sens. Une acceptation, c'est le fait d'accepter.* (That's right: an acception is a sense. An acceptance is the act of accepting.)
- *Et l'autre avocat parle d'une collision entre les deux voisins.* (And the other lawyer talks about a collision between the two neighbours.)
- *Il veut sans doute dire une collusion, un accord secret. Une collision, c'est un choc.* (He probably means a collusion, a secret agreement. A collision is a crash.)
- *Dernière chose : une décision est éminente, ou imminente ?* (Last thing: is a decision éminente, or imminente?)
- *Imminente. Éminent, c'est pour une personne remarquable.* (Imminente. Éminent is for a remarkable person.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Perpétrer un crime, not perpétuer un crime">
  Writers who know *perpétuel* often write *le crime a été perpétué*. That means the crime was kept alive over time. For a crime or an attack, the verb is *perpétrer*: **le crime a été perpétré la nuit dernière**, **un vol perpétré en plein jour**. Keep *perpétuer* for what lasts: **perpétuer une tradition**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Find the word inside the word">
  Many paronyms carry a shorter word you already know. *Recouvrir* contains *couvrir*, *acceptation* contains *accepter*, *perpétuer* goes with *perpétuel*, and *collision* goes with the idea of a shock. Before you write the longer word, say the short one in your head: if it fits the meaning, you have the right member of the pair.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un paronyme est un mot qui ressemble à un autre mot, mais qui a un autre sens. Éminent décrit une personne remarquable, alors qu'imminent décrit un événement qui va arriver très bientôt. Un prodige est une personne qui a un don très rare, et prodigue décrit quelqu'un qui dépense ou qui donne sans compter. Une conjecture est une idée qui n'est pas prouvée, alors que la conjoncture est la situation, surtout économique, à un moment donné. On perpètre un crime, mais on perpétue une tradition. Une collision est un choc entre deux voitures, alors qu'une collusion est un accord secret. Une effraction est le fait de forcer une porte ou une fenêtre, et une infraction est le fait de ne pas respecter une loi ou une règle. Un virus infecte un ordinateur, mais des rats infestent une cave. Quand on a recouvré la santé ou la liberté, on l'a retrouvée, alors que la neige a recouvert la route. Enfin, une acception est un sens d'un mot, et une acceptation est le fait d'accepter.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize the main senses of nine common words with several meanings, and to let the context tell you which one is meant.

## What you already know

You've met several words with more than one sense already. You know *une pièce* as a room, from <LessonLink id="a2-home-and-chores" text="the lesson on the home" />, as a coin, from <LessonLink id="b1-ne-que-ni-ni" text="the lesson on ne… que and ni… ni" />, and as a play (*la pièce de théâtre*), from <LessonLink id="c1-dislocation-and-fronting" text="the lesson on dislocation" />. You know *une note* as a mark, from <LessonLink id="a2-culture-french-school-system" text="the lesson on the school system" />. You know *le cadre* and *la portée* from <LessonLink id="c1-abstract-nouns" text="the lesson on abstract nouns" />, and *relever un défi* and *tenir compte de* from <LessonLink id="c1-collocations" text="the lesson on collocations" />. This lesson adds the senses a C2 reader meets in formal French.

## What polysemy is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

The term *la polysémie* (polysemy) is the fact that one word has several related senses. *Un mot polysémique* (a polysemous word) isn't two words that happen to look alike, like the paronyms of <LessonLink id="c2-paronyms" text="the last lesson" />. It's one word whose meaning has spread: from a part of a house to a part of a machine, from a mark on paper to a bill.

Each sense is an *acception*, as you saw in the last lesson. Dictionaries usually start with *le sens propre* (the literal sense) and then give *le sens figuré* (the figurative sense): *un coup* is first a blow, and then a quick action (*un coup d'œil*), an attempt (*du premier coup*) or some help (*un coup de main*).

<Example fr="Au sens propre, un coup fait mal ; au sens figuré, un coup de main fait du bien." en="In the literal sense, a blow hurts; in the figurative sense, a hand (some help) does you good." />

## Let the context choose

A polysemous word rarely leaves you in doubt, because its partners choose the sense. Three clues do most of the work.

- **The words around it.** The phrase *payer la note* is a bill; *une bonne note* is a mark; *une note de musique* is a sound.
- **The construction.** The verb in *relever une erreur* (with a direct object) means to point it out; *relever de* (with *de*) means to fall under.
- **The situation.** In a form, *une pièce* is a document; in a flat, it's a room; in a garage, it's a part.

When you read, check the partner before you choose the sense. When a sentence still allows two senses, rewrite it. *La note est trop élevée* could be about a mark or a bill; *la note de l'hôtel est trop élevée* leaves no doubt.

<Example fr="La note de l'hôtel est plus élevée que prévu." en="The hotel bill is higher than expected." />

When you write, give the reader a partner that makes the sense clear.

## Pièce and note

<VocabTable group="piece" />

In administrative French, *une pièce* is a document, and *une pièce justificative* is one that proves what you say: a bill, a certificate, a payslip. In a garage or a workshop, *une pièce détachée* is a spare part.

<Example fr="Votre demande n'est pas complète : il manque une pièce justificative." en="Your application is incomplete: a supporting document is missing." />

<Example fr="Le garage attend une pièce détachée pour réparer la voiture." en="The garage is waiting for a spare part to repair the car." />

At a hotel or in a restaurant, *la note* is the bill. In music, *une note* is a sound. The bill is the sense learners miss most often, because English uses "note" for neither.

<Example fr="Avant de partir, n'oubliez pas de payer la note." en="Before you leave, don't forget to pay the bill." />

## Coup

<VocabTable group="coup" />

The term *un coup* starts as a blow, and most of its other senses keep the idea of something quick or sudden. *Jeter un coup d'œil* is to have a quick look; *réussir du premier coup* is to succeed at the first attempt; *donner un coup de main* is to give someone a hand. You already know *ça vaut le coup* (it's worth it), from B1.

<Example fr="Elle a trouvé la solution du premier coup." en="She found the solution at the first attempt." />

The phrase *un coup de main* is informal: in a formal letter, write *une aide*.

## Matière and valeur

<VocabTable group="matiere" />

The term *la matière* is first what something is made of, and then a subject at school (*les mathématiques sont sa matière préférée*). *En matière de* introduces a field, in formal writing: *en matière de logement*, *en matière de santé*.

The term *la valeur* is what something is worth (*la valeur d'une maison*). In the plural, *les valeurs* are also the principles a person or a group believes in. *Mettre en valeur* means to show something at its best.

<Example fr="En matière de logement, la loi a beaucoup changé." en="As regards housing, the law has changed a lot. (formal)" />

<Example fr="Cette entreprise met en valeur le travail de ses employés." en="This company showcases its employees' work." />

## Titre

<VocabTable group="titre" />

You know *le titre* of a book or an article. *À juste titre* means "rightly, with good reason", and it usually follows the verb or closes the sentence. *Un titre de transport* is the formal word for a ticket or a pass, the one you see on signs in stations.

<Example fr="Les habitants se plaignent, à juste titre, du bruit." en="The residents complain, rightly, about the noise." />

## Relever, soutenir and se tenir

<VocabTable group="verbs" />

The word *relever* has many senses. With a direct object, *relever une erreur* is to notice it and point it out. With *de*, *relever de* means "to fall under, to be a matter for": *cette question relève du ministère*.

The word *soutenir* means to support a person or a project. It's the verb English speakers need when they think of "to support": *supporter*, as you saw in <LessonLink id="b2-false-friends" text="the lesson on false friends" />, means to put up with. At a university, *soutenir une thèse* is to defend a thesis in front of a jury.

The phrase *se tenir* is the formal verb for a meeting or an event that takes place: *la réunion va se tenir à Lyon*. The future works like *tenir*.

<Example fr="Plusieurs associations ont soutenu le projet de nouvelle bibliothèque." en="Several associations have supported the plan for a new library." />

<Example fr="La conférence va se tenir en mars, à la mairie." en="The conference is going to be held in March, at the town hall. (formal)" />

## The whole list

- The noun *une pièce*: a room, a coin, a play; *une pièce justificative* (a document), *une pièce détachée* (a spare part).
- The noun *la note*: a mark; *payer la note* (the bill); *une note de musique*.
- The noun *un coup*: a blow; *un coup d'œil*, *du premier coup*, *un coup de main* (informal).
- The nouns *la matière* (a subject, a material); *en matière de* (formal). *La valeur*, *les valeurs*, *mettre en valeur*.
- The phrases *à juste titre* (rightly); *un titre de transport* (a ticket, formal).
- The verbs *relever une erreur* (to point out), *relever de* (to fall under); *soutenir* (to support), *soutenir une thèse*; *se tenir* (to take place, formal).

## En français

Sam, an exchange student, is filling in a form for his university. His flatmate Chloé helps him. They use *tu*.

- *Chloé, la secrétaire veut une pièce de plus pour mon dossier. Je ne comprends pas : j'ai déjà une chambre !* (Chloé, the secretary wants one more pièce for my file. I don't understand: I already have a room!)
- *Ici, une pièce, c'est un document : une pièce justificative, comme une facture.* (Here, a pièce is a document: a supporting document, like a bill.)
- *Ah ! Elle a aussi relevé une erreur dans ma demande. Elle l'a corrigée ?* (Oh! She also relevé a mistake in my application. Did she correct it?)
- *Non, relever une erreur, c'est la remarquer et la signaler. C'est à toi de la corriger.* (No, relever a mistake is to notice it and point it out. It's up to you to correct it.)
- *D'accord. Elle m'a dit aussi que ma demande relevait du service des étudiants étrangers.* (OK. She also told me that my application relevait du international students' office.)
- *Ça veut dire que c'est ce service qui doit s'en occuper.* (That means it's that office that has to deal with it.)
- *Et hier, au restaurant, le serveur m'a apporté la note. J'ai pensé qu'il me donnait une note pour le repas !* (And yesterday, at the restaurant, the waiter brought me the note. I thought he was giving me a mark for the meal!)
- *Presque : la note, c'est ce que tu dois payer.* (Almost: the note is what you have to pay.)
- *Je comprends mieux. Tu peux me donner un coup de main pour le formulaire ?* (That's clearer. Can you give me a hand with the form?)
- *Bien sûr. Je jette un coup d'œil à tes papiers ce soir.* (Of course. I'll have a quick look at your papers this evening.)
- *Un coup d'œil, un coup de main… Personne ne va me frapper, j'espère ?* (A coup d'œil, a coup de main… Nobody's going to hit me, I hope?)
- *Mais non ! Au sens figuré, un coup, c'est souvent quelque chose de rapide.* (Of course not! In the figurative sense, a coup is often something quick.)
- *Avec la polysémie, je dois regarder chaque mot avec ses voisins.* (With polysemy, I have to look at each word with its neighbours.)
- *Exactement, et en matière de vocabulaire, c'est la meilleure méthode.* (Exactly, and as regards vocabulary, it's the best method.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Soutenir un projet, not supporter un projet">
  English speakers often write *je supporte ce projet* for "I support this project". In French, *supporter* means to put up with, so the sentence sounds as if you're only tolerating it. Use *soutenir*: **je soutiens ce projet**, **plusieurs associations soutiennent la candidature**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Give each word a sense map">
  For each polysemous word you meet, keep one page with the word in the middle and one branch per sense, each with a short sentence: *une pièce* → room, coin, play, document, part. When you meet a new sense, add a branch rather than a new page. After a few weeks, you see how the senses connect, and new ones are easier to guess.
</Callout>

## En résumé

La polysémie est le fait qu'un seul mot a plusieurs sens. Un mot polysémique a souvent un sens propre et un ou plusieurs sens figurés. Le contexte choisit le sens : les mots qui l'entourent, la construction et la situation. Dans un dossier, une pièce justificative est un document qui prouve ce qu'on dit, et dans un garage, une pièce détachée est une partie d'une machine. Au restaurant, on paie la note, mais en musique, on joue une note de musique. Un coup d'œil est un regard rapide, du premier coup veut dire dès le premier essai, et un coup de main est une aide familière. En matière de santé veut dire quand il s'agit de santé. Les valeurs sont aussi des principes, et mettre en valeur veut dire montrer sous son meilleur jour. À juste titre veut dire avec raison. On relève une erreur, une question relève d'un service, on soutient un projet ou une thèse, et une réunion se tient quelque part.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to take a French word apart into its stem, prefix and suffix, guess the meaning of a word you've never seen, and avoid forms that look regular but don't exist.

## What you already know

In <LessonLink id="b2-nominalisation" text="the lesson on nominalisation" />, you turned verbs into nouns with suffixes such as *-tion* and *-ment* (*l'arrivée*, *la fermeture*). In <LessonLink id="c1-abstract-nouns" text="the lesson on abstract nouns" />, you learned nouns such as *le changement* and *l'efficacité*. This lesson gives you the whole system, so that you can read new words and build the ones that exist.

## The parts of a word

<VocabTable group="terms" />

Most French words that aren't very short are built from parts. *Un radical* (a stem) carries the main meaning. *Un préfixe* (a prefix) comes before it and changes its meaning; *un suffixe* (a suffix) comes after it and usually changes its class, from verb to noun or from adjective to verb. Building a word this way is *la dérivation* (derivation).

<Example fr="Dans le mot « illisible », le radical est lis-, le préfixe il- et le suffixe -ible." en="In the word 'illisible', the stem is lis-, the prefix il- and the suffix -ible." />

The stem isn't always the infinitive. *Lisible* comes from *lis-* (*nous lisons*), and *faisable* from *fais-* (*nous faisons*). When you build a word, start from the *nous* form of the present if the infinitive doesn't work.

## Prefixes

<VocabTable group="prefixes" />

A prefix keeps the class of the word: a verb stays a verb, an adjective stays an adjective. These are the ones you'll meet most.

- **The prefixes dé- and dés- reverse an action**, as in *faire* / *défaire*, *obéir* / *désobéir*.
- **The prefix in- means "not"**, and it changes before some letters: *il-* before *l* (*illégal*), *ir-* before *r* (*irréaliste*), *im-* before *b*, *m* or *p* (*impossible*).
- **The prefix re- (usually ré- before a vowel) means "again"**: *refaire*, *réécrire*.
- **The prefix pré- means "before"**: *prévoir*, *la préhistoire*.
- **The prefixes sur- and sous- mean "over" and "under"**: *surestimer*, *sous-estimer*.
- **The prefixes contre-, anti- and auto- mean "against", "against" and "self"**: *un contre-argument*, *un antivirus*, *un autoportrait*.

<Example fr="Le ministre a sous-estimé le coût du projet." en="The minister underestimated the cost of the project." />

<Example fr="Cet élève a désobéi au règlement de l'école." en="This pupil disobeyed the school rules." />

## Suffixes

<VocabTable group="suffixes" />

A suffix usually changes the class of the word. The main ones work like this.

- **For nouns from verbs**, the suffixes are *-age* (*nettoyer*, *le nettoyage*), *-ment* (*changer*, *le changement*) and *-tion* (*créer*, *la création*).
- **For nouns from adjectives**, the suffix is often *-ité* (*simple*, *la simplicité*; *rapide*, *la rapidité*).
- **For movements and the people in them**, the suffixes are *-isme* and *-iste* (*le journalisme*, *un journaliste*).
- **For "that can be done"**, the suffixes are *-able* and *-ible* (*lavable*, *faisable*, *lisible*).
- **For verbs from adjectives or nouns**, the suffixes are *-iser* and *-ifier* (*moderne*, *moderniser*; *simple*, *simplifier*).

<Example fr="Le nettoyage des rues commence à six heures." en="Street cleaning starts at six." />

<Example fr="La mairie veut simplifier les démarches en ligne." en="The town hall wants to simplify the online procedures." />

**The suffix isn't predictable.** *Nettoyer* gives *le nettoyage*, but *changer* gives *le changement*, *arriver* gives *l'arrivée*, and *créer* gives *la création*. A word that looks regular may not exist: *la nettoyation* and *l'arrivement* aren't French. Before you use a word you've built, check it in a dictionary.

## Learned roots

<VocabTable group="roots" />

Some words are built from Greek or Latin roots that French shares with English. Four of them are worth knowing, because they appear in new words too.

- **The root -logie means "the study of"**: *la sociologie*, *la biologie*.
- **The root -cratie means "power"**: *la démocratie*, *la bureaucratie*.
- **The root -phobe means "who fears"**: *claustrophobe* (afraid of closed spaces).
- **The root -cide means "that kills"**: *un insecticide*.

<Example fr="La sociologie étudie la vie des groupes et des sociétés." en="Sociology studies the life of groups and societies." />

<Example fr="Cet insecticide est interdit dans les jardins publics." en="This insecticide is banned in public gardens." />

<Example fr="Mon frère est claustrophobe : il ne prend jamais l'ascenseur." en="My brother is claustrophobic: he never takes the lift." />

## Compounds, mots-valises and new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

Besides derivation, French makes new words by joining two words, as you saw with *un porte-monnaie* and *un gratte-ciel*. A more playful kind is *un mot-valise* (a blend), which joins the start of one word to the end of another: *le franglais* (*français* + *anglais*), *un courriel* (*courrier* + *électronique*).

The term *un néologisme* (a neologism) is any new word. The press creates many of them, mostly with the tools you've just seen: a prefix (*déconnecter*), a suffix (*la numérisation*), or a mot-valise. Some last, and some disappear after a few months. As a rule of thumb, read new words freely, but use them in your own formal writing only when a dictionary has them.

<Example fr="La numérisation des vieux journaux de la ville a pris trois ans." en="The digitization of the town's old newspapers took three years." />

## The model: a column with three invented words

The column below is original. Its three new words are **invented for this lesson**: you won't find them in a dictionary, and you shouldn't use them as real words.

*Notre langue manque de mots pour décrire la vie de bureau. Je propose donc trois néologismes. Le premier, la réunionocratie, veut dire une entreprise où les réunions ont pris le pouvoir : on n'y décide plus rien sans en organiser une autre.*

*Le deuxième, un notificophobe, est un employé qui a peur de ses notifications et qui coupe son téléphone dès neuf heures. Le troisième est un verbe : déréunionner, c'est annuler une réunion qui ne sert à rien, et c'est le verbe le plus agréable de la semaine.*

(Our language lacks words to describe office life. So I'm proposing three neologisms. The first, la réunionocratie, means a company where meetings have taken power: nothing is decided there any more without organizing another one. The second, un notificophobe, is an employee who is afraid of their notifications and switches off their phone from nine o'clock. The third is a verb: déréunionner is to cancel a meeting that serves no purpose, and it's the most pleasant verb of the week.)

## How the column builds its words

- **The invented word réunionocratie** is *réunion* + the root *-cratie* (power), like *démocratie*.
- **The invented word notificophobe** is *notification* + the root *-phobe* (who fears), like *claustrophobe*.
- **The invented verb déréunionner** is the prefix *dé-* (reversing an action) + *réunion* + the verb ending *-er*, like *défaire*.

Each one is clear because each part keeps its usual meaning. That's what makes a neologism work, and what lets you read one you've never seen.

## The whole list

- A word = a stem, with or without a prefix and a suffix.
- Prefixes keep the class: *dé-*, *in-* (*il-*, *ir-*, *im-*), *re-*, *pré-*, *sur-*, *sous-*, *contre-*, *anti-*, *auto-*.
- Suffixes change the class: *-age*, *-ment*, *-tion* (nouns from verbs), *-ité* (nouns from adjectives), *-isme*, *-iste*, *-able*, *-ible*, *-iser*, *-ifier*.
- The suffix isn't predictable: check every word you build.
- Learned roots: *-logie*, *-cratie*, *-phobe*, *-cide*.
- Compounds and mots-valises (*le franglais*, *un courriel*); neologisms in the press.

## En français

Nora is translating a report and asks her colleague Théo about some words. They use *tu*.

- *Théo, j'ai trouvé « numérisation » dans le rapport. C'est un vrai mot ?* (Théo, I found "numérisation" in the report. Is it a real word?)
- *Oui : numérique, plus -iser, plus -ation. C'est le fait de tout passer en numérique.* (Yes: numérique, plus -iser, plus -ation. It's putting everything into digital form.)
- *Et « irréaliste », avec deux r ?* (And "irréaliste", with two r's?)
- *Oui. In- devient ir- devant un r : in- et réaliste donnent irréaliste.* (Yes. In- becomes ir- before an r: in- and réaliste give irréaliste.)
- *J'ai voulu écrire « le changeage des horaires ». Mon logiciel le souligne en rouge.* (I wanted to write "le changeage des horaires". My software underlines it in red.)
- *Il a raison : on dit le changement. Le suffixe -age ne marche pas avec tous les verbes.* (It's right: we say le changement. The suffix -age doesn't work with every verb.)
- *Comment je peux savoir quel suffixe choisir ?* (How can I know which suffix to choose?)
- *Tu ne peux pas le deviner : il faut vérifier dans un dictionnaire.* (You can't guess it: you have to check in a dictionary.)
- *Et ce mot, « réunionite », c'est sérieux ?* (And this word, "réunionite", is it serious?)
- *C'est familier et ironique : une maladie des réunions trop nombreuses.* (It's familiar and ironic: an illness of too many meetings.)
- *Alors je ne le mets pas dans mon texte.* (Then I won't put it in my text.)
- *Mets plutôt « un trop grand nombre de réunions ». Le rapport est formel.* (Put "un trop grand nombre de réunions" instead. The report is formal.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the suffix before you use the word">
  Learners who know the pattern often build words that don't exist: *la nettoyation*, *l'arrivement*, *le changeage*. The suffix depends on the word, and you can't predict it. Check, then write: **le nettoyage**, **l'arrivée**, **le changement**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read long words from the middle">
  When you meet a long unknown word, find the stem first, in the middle, then read the prefix and the suffix around it. *Inutilisable* is *utilis-* (use), *in-* (not) and *-able* (that can be): that can't be used. With practice, most long words in a formal text stop being unknown words.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un mot est souvent formé d'un radical, d'un préfixe et d'un suffixe. La dérivation est la formation d'un mot avec un préfixe ou un suffixe. Le préfixe ne change pas la classe du mot : dé- inverse une action, in- veut dire non et devient il-, ir- ou im- devant certaines lettres, re- veut dire de nouveau, sur- et sous- veulent dire trop et pas assez. Le suffixe change souvent la classe : nettoyer donne le nettoyage, changer donne le changement, simple donne la simplicité et simplifier, et moderne donne moderniser. Les adjectifs en -able et en -ible, comme lavable ou lisible, veulent dire qu'on peut faire l'action. On ne peut pas deviner le suffixe : il faut vérifier chaque mot dans un dictionnaire. Les racines savantes, comme -logie, -cratie, -phobe et -cide aident à comprendre des mots nouveaux. Un mot-valise, comme le franglais, réunit deux mots. Enfin, un néologisme est un mot nouveau, et la presse en crée souvent.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to avoid the false friends that remain after B2, choose the uncontested word in formal writing, and recognize the anglicized uses that many French speakers accept and some criticize.

## What you already know

<LessonLink id="b2-false-friends" text="The lesson on false friends" /> taught *sensible* (sensitive), *supporter* (to put up with), *réaliser* (to carry out), *éventuellement* (possibly) and *achever* (to complete), and listed others you met earlier, such as *actuellement* (currently). You also know *respecter un délai* (to meet a deadline) from <LessonLink id="b2-futur-anterieur" text="the lesson on the futur antérieur" />. Those are recapped here, not taught again. This lesson covers the ones left: words that matter most in professional and formal French.

## Labelling, not correcting

Some false friends are simply mistakes: *une évidence* never means "proof". Others are uses that came from English and are now very common: *réaliser que* for "to realize that", or *une opportunité* for "an opportunity". *L'Académie française* (the Académie française, which publishes a dictionary and advice on usage) criticizes them, but many speakers and writers use them.

So this lesson labels these uses rather than calling them wrong. **In careful writing**, choose the uncontested word: *se rendre compte que*, *une occasion*. **In this course**, an exercise that asks for careful writing accepts only the uncontested word; an exercise with no register cue accepts both, and says so in its feedback.

## People and attitudes

<VocabTable group="people" />

The word *versatile* means fickle, quick to change: *une opinion versatile*. For "versatile" in the English sense, you know *polyvalent*. *Avoir de la chance* means to be lucky; *une chance* is luck, or a probability (*il y a peu de chances qu'il vienne*). For an opportunity, say *une occasion*.

<Example fr="L'opinion publique est versatile : elle change d'avis en quelques semaines." en="Public opinion is fickle: it changes its mind within a few weeks." />

The word *formel* usually means categorical or explicit: *un refus formel* (a flat refusal), *une interdiction formelle*. For a formal register, say *soutenu*, and for a formal occasion, *officiel*.

<Example fr="Le ministère a opposé un refus formel à notre demande." en="The ministry flatly refused our request." />

The word *conséquent* means consistent, in what you think and do: *être conséquent avec soi-même*. You know *par conséquent* (consequently). The use of *conséquent* for "large" (*un salaire conséquent*) is very common in speech and in the press, and the Académie française criticizes it. In careful writing, say *important*.

## Work and organization

<VocabTable group="work" />

The term *une opportunité* is, first, the fact of being timely or appropriate: *on discute de l'opportunité de cette réforme* (whether this reform is a good idea now). Its use for "an opportunity" is widespread, especially in business, and criticized by the Académie française. *Une occasion* says the same thing with no debate.

<Example fr="Le conseil s'interroge sur l'opportunité d'un nouveau budget." en="The council is wondering whether a new budget is appropriate now." />

The word *contrôler* means first to check or inspect: *contrôler les billets*, *contrôler une facture*. It also means to control (*contrôler la situation*), so it's only a partial false friend. The trap is the other way round: English speakers who want "to check" often forget *contrôler*.

The term *un agenda* is a diary or a planner. A meeting's agenda is *l'ordre du jour*, which you know from B2. *L'audience* is the size of an audience (*l'audience d'une émission*) or a court hearing; the people who watch are *le public*. And *un délai* is the time allowed, while a delay is *un retard*.

<Example fr="Notez la réunion dans votre agenda : l'ordre du jour suivra." en="Put the meeting in your diary: the agenda will follow." />

<Example fr="Le train a vingt minutes de retard." en="The train is twenty minutes late." />

## Facts and certainty

<VocabTable group="facts" />

The word *ignorer* means not to know, especially in formal French: *j'ignore la réponse*. *Ignorer quelqu'un* (to ignore someone) is also used, so here too the trap is that English speakers forget the first sense.

The term *une évidence* is an obvious fact: *c'est une évidence*. Proof is *une preuve*, and evidence in a trial is *des preuves*. *Définitivement* means for good: *il est parti définitivement*. You'll also hear it for "definitely", an anglicism criticized like *réaliser que*; in careful writing, say *sans aucun doute* or *certainement*.

<Example fr="La police n'a trouvé aucune preuve, même si la réponse semblait une évidence." en="The police found no evidence, even though the answer seemed obvious." />

The phrase *réaliser que* for "to realize that" is very common and criticized; you saw at B2 that careful speakers prefer *se rendre compte que*.

## The model: an email with false friends

The email below is original, between two invented companies. The first version has eight false friends, as a writer thinking in English might use them. The corrected version follows.

*Madame, j'ai réalisé ce matin que votre commande avait un délai de trois jours. C'est définitivement de notre faute, et nous voulons saisir cette opportunité pour vous présenter nos excuses. Notre fournisseur est assez versatile, mais nous avons maintenant une évidence claire du problème. Je l'ai mis à l'agenda de notre réunion de lundi, et je vous propose une réduction conséquente.*

*Madame, je me suis rendu compte ce matin que votre commande avait trois jours de retard. C'est sans aucun doute de notre faute, et nous voulons saisir cette occasion pour vous présenter nos excuses. Notre fournisseur n'est pas toujours fiable, mais nous avons maintenant une preuve claire du problème. Je l'ai mis à l'ordre du jour de notre réunion de lundi, et je vous propose une réduction importante.*

(Corrected version, in translation: Madam, I realized this morning that your order was three days late. It is definitely our fault, and we want to take this opportunity to apologize. Our supplier isn't always reliable, but we now have clear proof of the problem. I've put it on the agenda of our meeting on Monday, and I'm offering you a substantial discount.)

## How the email was corrected

- The verb *réaliser que* became **se rendre compte que**, the uncontested choice in a letter.
- The phrase *avait un délai de trois jours* became **avait trois jours de retard**: a delay is *un retard*.
- The adverb *définitivement* became **sans aucun doute**.
- The noun *opportunité* became **occasion**, in a letter of apology.
- The adjective *versatile* became **pas toujours fiable**: the writer meant unreliable, not fickle.
- The noun *évidence* became **preuve**.
- The noun *agenda* became **ordre du jour**.
- The adjective *conséquente* became **importante**, the uncontested word for "large".

## The whole list

- Versatile = fickle (versatile in English = *polyvalent*); une chance = luck (an opportunity = *une occasion*).
- Formel = categorical (formal register = *soutenu*, *officiel*).
- Conséquent = consistent; "large" is widely used, criticized: write *important*.
- Opportunité = timeliness; "opportunity" is widely used, criticized: write *occasion*.
- Contrôler = to check (and to control); ignorer = not to know (and to ignore).
- Un agenda = a diary (*l'ordre du jour* for a meeting); l'audience = ratings, a hearing (*le public* for people); un délai = time allowed (*un retard* for a delay).
- Une évidence = an obvious fact (*une preuve* for proof); définitivement = for good (*sans aucun doute* for definitely).
- Réaliser que is widely used, criticized: write *se rendre compte que* in careful French.

## En français

Clémence, a sales manager, reads the email with Antoine, who wrote the first version. They use *tu*.

- *Antoine, ton message au client est très correct, mais il y a quelques faux amis.* (Antoine, your message to the client is very proper, but there are a few false friends.)
- *Lesquels ? J'ai fait attention à l'orthographe.* (Which ones? I was careful with the spelling.)
- *« Un délai de trois jours » : tu veux dire que la commande est en retard ?* ("A délai of three days": do you mean the order is late?)
- *Oui, trois jours de retard. Un délai, ce n'est pas la même chose ?* (Yes, three days late. Isn't a délai the same thing?)
- *Non, un délai, c'est le temps qu'on a pour faire quelque chose.* (No, a délai is the time you have to do something.)
- *D'accord. Et « définitivement de notre faute » ?* (OK. And "définitivement de notre faute"?)
- *Définitivement veut dire pour toujours. Écris « sans aucun doute ».* (Définitivement means for good. Write "sans aucun doute".)
- *Et « opportunité » ? Tout le monde le dit au bureau.* (And "opportunité"? Everyone says it at the office.)
- *C'est vrai, mais l'Académie le critique. Dans une lettre d'excuses, « occasion » est plus sûr.* (That's true, but the Académie criticizes it. In a letter of apology, "occasion" is safer.)
- *J'ai aussi écrit que notre fournisseur était versatile.* (I also wrote that our supplier was versatile.)
- *Tu voulais dire qu'il change d'avis, ou qu'on ne peut pas compter sur lui ?* (Did you mean that he changes his mind, or that we can't rely on him?)
- *Qu'on ne peut pas compter sur lui.* (That we can't rely on him.)
- *Alors écris « pas toujours fiable ». Versatile parle de ses opinions.* (Then write "pas toujours fiable". Versatile is about his opinions.)
- *Merci. Je relis tout le message avant de l'envoyer.* (Thanks. I'll reread the whole message before I send it.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une preuve, not une évidence">
  English speakers writing about a case or a report often say *il n'y a aucune évidence* for "there's no evidence". In French, *une évidence* is something obvious, so the sentence says that nothing is obvious. For proof, say **il n'y a aucune preuve**, and for evidence in a trial, **des preuves**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Translate your draft back into English">
  For a formal message, read your French draft once as if you were an English reader who knows no French: translate each long word literally. Where the literal English sounds right (*définitivement*, "definitely"), stop and check, because that's exactly where a false friend hides. Where it sounds odd, the French is usually fine.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un faux ami ressemble à un mot anglais, mais il a un autre sens. Versatile veut dire qui change souvent d'avis, et pas polyvalent. Avoir de la chance, c'est profiter d'un hasard favorable, alors qu'une occasion est un moment favorable. Formel veut dire clair et sans discussion possible, comme dans un refus formel. Conséquent veut dire qui agit selon ses idées, et son emploi pour important est très courant, mais critiqué. Une opportunité est d'abord le fait qu'une chose arrive au bon moment, et son emploi pour une occasion est lui aussi critiqué par l'Académie française. Contrôler veut dire d'abord vérifier, et ignorer veut dire ne pas savoir. Un agenda est un petit livre pour noter ses rendez-vous, et l'ordre du jour est la liste des points d'une réunion. Un délai est le temps qu'on a pour faire une chose, et un retard est le fait d'arriver tard. Une évidence est une chose claire pour tout le monde, alors qu'une preuve montre qu'une chose est vraie. Enfin, définitivement veut dire pour toujours.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize common anglicisms, give the French terms recommended in their place, say how those terms are published, and describe the debate around them without taking sides.

## What you already know

You know *un courriel* from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-quebec" text="the lesson on Québec" />, and *le Journal officiel* from <LessonLink id="c1-law-and-institutions" text="the lesson on law and institutions" />. You also know two words that replaced English ones long ago and that nobody debates today: *un ordinateur* and *un logiciel*. And you know several anglicisms that are simply part of French now, such as *le week-end* and *un podcast*. This lesson is about the terms in between: recent, recommended, and used by some speakers more than others.

## What an anglicism is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

<VocabTable group="anglicisms" />

*Un anglicisme* (an anglicism) is an English word, phrase or meaning used in French: *un mail*, *le marketing*, *un hashtag*, *une newsletter*, *spoiler*, or *réaliser que* in the sense of "to realize", which you saw in <LessonLink id="c2-advanced-false-friends" text="the last lesson" />. For many of them, an official body has proposed *un équivalent officiel* (an official equivalent). To *recommander un terme* (recommend a term) is to propose it for official and professional use, not to ban the English word.

What matters at C2 is *l'emploi d'un mot* (the use of a word): who says it, in what situation, and in which country. The same text may need *courriel* in a letter to an administration and *mail* in a message to a colleague.

<Example fr="L'emploi du mot « mail » est courant au bureau, mais une lettre officielle dira « courriel »." en="Using the word 'mail' is common at the office, but an official letter will say 'courriel'." />

## Some official terms in France

<VocabTable group="words" />

Four terms show the range of what happens to a recommendation.

- **The term courriel**, for an e-mail, is official in France and very common in Québec. In France, many people still say *un mail* in everyday speech.
- **The term mot-dièse**, for a hashtag, was recommended in 2013. You'll see it in official texts; in everyday speech, many people keep *hashtag*.
- **The term mercatique**, for marketing, is official, and you'll meet it in some textbooks and official texts. In business, *le marketing* remains very common.
- **The word covoiturage** isn't a debated replacement: it's simply the usual French word for car-sharing.

<Example fr="Partagez notre message avec le mot-dièse de la semaine." en="Share our message with this week's hashtag." />

For a newsletter, French in France usually says *une lettre d'information*.

## Terms from Québec

<VocabTable group="quebec" />

In Québec, *l'Office québécois de la langue française* (the OQLF) recommends French terms, and several of them are now known across the French-speaking world. *Divulgâcher*, a blend of *divulguer* (to reveal) and *gâcher* (to spoil), means to spoil the end of a story. *La baladodiffusion* is podcasting, and *un balado* a podcast. *Une infolettre* is the usual word for a newsletter in Québec.

<Example fr="Ne me divulgâche pas la fin de la série, je n'ai pas fini !" en="Don't spoil the end of the series for me, I haven't finished! (Québec)" />

Label these terms as Québec usage when you use them. In France, they're understood by many readers, but they're not the everyday words.

## How a term becomes official

<VocabTable group="policy" />

In France, *une commission de terminologie* (a terminology commission) studies the English words that appear in a field and proposes French equivalents. The terms it adopts are published in *le Journal officiel* and listed on a public site, FranceTerme. In Québec, the OQLF publishes its recommendations in its own dictionary.

*La loi Toubon*, the law of 4 August 1994 on the use of French, requires French in some areas of public life in France, such as information for consumers and the workplace. It doesn't tell people which words to use in conversation.

<Example fr="La loi Toubon a été adoptée en 1994." en="The Toubon law was adopted in 1994." />

## Purism and usage

The debate about anglicisms is old, and the two sides can be summed up fairly.

- **Some people, often called puristes,** see official terms as a way to keep French able to name new things in its own words, and they prefer them wherever possible.
- **Others, including many linguists,** point out that borrowing is a normal part of a language's life, and that *l'usage* (usage: how people really speak and write) decides in the end which words stay.

This course doesn't take sides. An official term isn't "the correct word" in speech; it's the expected word in official and professional writing, especially in France and in Québec. Your choice depends on the reader.

<Example fr="Une puriste dira « mercatique », mais ses collègues disent souvent « marketing »." en="A purist will say 'mercatique', but her colleagues often say 'marketing'." />

## The model: a language week

The article below is original, about an invented company. Each term appears with its label.

*Cette semaine, l'entreprise Lumiva organise sa première semaine de la langue française. L'idée vient de l'équipe de la mercatique, que tout le monde, ici, appelle encore « le marketing ». Chaque matin, un courriel propose un mot du jour : lundi, c'était « mot-dièse », pour remplacer « hashtag » dans nos messages officiels.*

*Les collègues de Montréal ont ajouté leurs propres termes. Ils ont expliqué que, chez eux, on dit « une infolettre » et « un balado », et ils ont demandé aux équipes de Lyon de ne pas divulgâcher la fin de la série que tout le bureau regarde.*

*Personne n'est obligé de changer sa façon de parler. Mais, pour les documents envoyés aux clients et aux ministères, l'entreprise recommande les termes officiels. Comme le dit une collègue, « dans un courriel au ministère, je n'écris pas mail ; entre collègues, si ».*

## En résumé

Un anglicisme est un mot ou un emploi anglais en français, comme un mail ou le marketing. Pour beaucoup d'anglicismes, on propose un équivalent officiel. Recommander un terme, c'est le proposer pour les textes officiels et les documents de travail, et pas interdire le mot anglais. En France, une commission de terminologie propose les termes, qui sont publiés au Journal officiel et réunis sur FranceTerme. Courriel, mot-dièse et mercatique sont des termes officiels, et on dit une lettre d'information pour une newsletter. Le covoiturage, lui, est le mot de tous les jours. Au Québec, l'Office québécois de la langue française recommande divulgâcher, la baladodiffusion et une infolettre. Un ordinateur et un logiciel sont des mots français employés depuis longtemps. La loi Toubon, la loi du 4 août 1994, demande le français dans certaines parties de la vie publique. Les puristes préfèrent les termes officiels, d'autres pensent que l'usage décide. Le bon choix dépend du lecteur.

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These five words name what goes wrong when a word is *imprécis* (imprecise). *Le mot juste* is the exact word for what you mean. *Une impropriété* is a real word used in a sense it doesn't have: *une infraction* for a break-in. *Un barbarisme* is a form that doesn't exist at all: *la nettoyation*. *Un mot passe-partout* is a catch-all word such as *faire*, *chose* or *mettre*, which is never wrong but rarely exact.

## Five kinds of imprecision

Each lesson of the unit gives one way a word can miss its target. Naming the kind of error tells you where to look for the right word.

1. **A paronym** is the neighbour of the word you wanted: *imminent* for *éminent*. Look at the pair, and check the partner word.
2. **An impropriété from a false friend** takes the English sense: *une évidence* for proof. Translate the French back literally, and check.
3. **A barbarism** is a regular-looking word that doesn't exist: *la nettoyation*. Check every word you've built in a dictionary.
4. **A register error** puts an anglicism where an official reader expects the French term: *un mail* in a letter to the town hall. Name the reader first.
5. **A catch-all word** is vague rather than wrong: *faire une enquête*. Look for the verb the noun usually goes with: *mener une enquête*.

A polysemous word is a special case. It's never the wrong word, but it can leave the reader unsure: give it a partner that makes its sense clear.

## How to choose the right word

Finding an error is half the work; the other half is choosing what replaces it. As a rule of thumb, work through four questions.

- **What do I mean, in other words?** Say the idea in a plain sentence first: "the burglars forced the door". The plain sentence often contains the right word, or points to it.
- **Which word usually goes with it?** Many choices are settled by the partner word: *mener une enquête*, *saisir une occasion*, *soutenir une idée*. You met many such pairs in <LessonLink id="c1-collocations" text="the lesson on collocations" />, and they're the fastest way to the exact word.
- **Who is reading?** An official reader expects the official term and no criticized use; a colleague doesn't mind *un mail*.
- **Does the word exist, in this sense?** For any word you've built or any word that looks like English, a dictionary has the last word.

When two words are both possible, choose the more precise one, not the more impressive one. A formal letter isn't better for being long: *mener une enquête* is exact, while *procéder à la réalisation d'une enquête* only adds words.

*Madame la Maire, je vous écris au nom de l'association de quartier des Tilleuls, à la suite du cambriolage de notre local, la semaine dernière. Des cambrioleurs sont entrés par effraction pendant la nuit et ont emporté notre ordinateur et une partie de nos dossiers.*

*Ce local appartient à la ville, et nous l'occupons depuis plus de dix ans grâce à un accord avec la mairie. Il accueille chaque semaine des cours de français pour adultes, une aide aux devoirs et les réunions de plusieurs associations du quartier.*

*La police a mené une enquête, mais elle n'a trouvé aucune preuve. Selon un éminent spécialiste de la sécurité, venu sur place, la porte arrière était trop vieille pour résister.*

*Cet événement est sans aucun doute le plus grave depuis la création de l'association. Il nous a fait comprendre que notre local, qui sert à la fois de bureau, de salle de réunion et de lieu d'accueil pour les enfants, doit rester un lieu polyvalent et sûr.*

*Nous souhaitons donc saisir cette occasion pour vous présenter un projet. Plusieurs familles ont soutenu l'idée de remplacer les portes et les fenêtres du rez-de-chaussée. Le nettoyage du local, que nous ferons nous-mêmes, commencera dès la fin des travaux.*

*Nous avons demandé trois devis à des entreprises de la région, et le moins cher nous paraît raisonnable. Nous pouvons en payer une partie grâce aux dons des habitants, mais pas tout. Nous savons que la mairie doit faire des choix, et que la conjoncture actuelle est difficile pour toutes les communes. Nous espérons pourtant que ce projet, modeste et utile, pourra être étudié lors du prochain conseil municipal. Les enfants qui viennent le mercredi nous demandent déjà quand ils pourront revenir.*

*En matière de sécurité, la question relève, selon nous, de la mairie. Vous trouverez nos coordonnées dans le courriel qui accompagne cette lettre, et nous restons à votre disposition pour en parler lors d'une réunion.*

*Veuillez agréer, Madame la Maire, l'expression de nos salutations distinguées.*

(The translation is given above, with the source text.)

## Before and after

| Before | After | Kind of error |
|---|---|---|
| *par infraction* | *par effraction* | paronym (an impropriété) |
| *a fait une enquête* | *a mené une enquête* | catch-all verb |
| *aucune évidence* | *aucune preuve* | false friend |
| *un imminent spécialiste* | *un éminent spécialiste* | paronym |
| *définitivement* | *sans aucun doute* | false friend |
| *un lieu versatile* | *un lieu polyvalent* | false friend |
| *cette opportunité* | *cette occasion* | criticized use, in a formal letter |
| *ont supporté l'idée* | *ont soutenu l'idée* | false friend |
| *la nettoyation* | *le nettoyage* | barbarism |
| *le mail* | *le courriel* | anglicism, for an official reader |

Three words in the letter look suspicious but are right. *La question relève de la mairie* uses *relever de* in its sense "to be a matter for". *En matière de sécurité* is the formal "as regards", which suits a letter to the town hall. And *la conjoncture actuelle* is the right member of its pair: the economic situation, not a guess.

## Your turn

Write a paragraph of 80 to 120 words for a formal letter to an administration: a request, a complaint or a proposal. Then revise it word by word, as in the table above: for each word that might be imprecise, name the kind of error, then choose *le mot juste*. The writing task in the exercises gives you a word count, a checklist and a model to compare with when you've finished.

<Callout type="warning" title="A real word isn't always the right word">
  After the unit on form, learners often trust any word the spellchecker accepts. *Une infraction*, *une évidence* and *versatile* are all real French words, so no tool will flag them. Check meaning as well as spelling: **entrer par effraction**, **aucune preuve**, **un lieu polyvalent**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a list of your own imprecise words">
  Each time a teacher, a colleague or a dictionary corrects one of your words, add it to a short personal list, with the right word next to it. Before you send a formal text, search it for every word on the list. Most writers repeat the same few imprecisions, so a list of ten words catches a surprising number of them.
</Callout>
