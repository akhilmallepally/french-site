By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand the most common French proverbs, recognize them when people half-quote or twist them, and translate them by an equivalent rather than word for word.

## What you already know

You know many French idioms from <LessonLink id="b2-idioms-everyday" text="the lesson on everyday idioms" />, such as *il pleut des cordes* and *appeler un chat un chat*, and the term *une expression imagée*. An idiom is a phrase you use inside a sentence; a proverb is a whole sentence that states a general truth. This lesson is about proverbs.

## What a proverb is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

*Un proverbe* is a short, fixed sentence that gives advice or states a truth about life: *mieux vaut tard que jamais*. *Un dicton* is similar but often more local or practical, about the weather or the seasons. Both have a fixed form: you don't change their words.

People introduce a proverb with *comme dit le proverbe* or *comme on dit*, or simply drop it into the conversation.

<Example fr="Comme dit le proverbe, mieux vaut tard que jamais." en="As the saying goes, better late than never." />
<Example fr="Mon grand-père connaît un dicton pour chaque saison." en="My grandfather knows a saying for every season." />

## Effort and patience

<VocabTable group="effort" />

Many French proverbs encourage effort. *Qui ne risque rien n'a rien* (nothing ventured, nothing gained) pushes someone to try. *Petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid* praises slow, steady work. *C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron* says that you learn by doing. *Vouloir, c'est pouvoir* says that will is enough; in speech, people also say *quand on veut, on peut*.

<Example fr="Tu hésites à postuler ? Qui ne risque rien n'a rien !" en="You're hesitating to apply? Nothing ventured, nothing gained!" />
<Example fr="Ton français progresse : petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid." en="Your French is improving: slow and steady wins the race." />
<Example fr="Tu peux réussir : vouloir, c'est pouvoir." en="You can succeed: where there's a will, there's a way." />

## Caution and judgement

<VocabTable group="caution" />

Other proverbs warn. *Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué* means don't count on a success too early. *L'habit ne fait pas le moine* warns against judging by appearances. *Il n'y a pas de fumée sans feu* suggests that a rumour has some basis, and it's often used to justify suspicion. *La nuit porte conseil* advises waiting before deciding. *Les absents ont toujours tort* observes that people who aren't there get the blame.

<Example fr="On n'a pas encore signé le contrat : il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué." en="We haven't signed the contract yet: don't count your chickens before they're hatched." />
<Example fr="Ne décide pas ce soir : la nuit porte conseil." en="Don't decide tonight: sleep on it." />
<Example fr="Tout le monde en parle, et il n'y a pas de fumée sans feu." en="Everyone is talking about it, and there's no smoke without fire." />
<Example fr="Il n'était pas à la réunion, et comme on dit, les absents ont toujours tort." en="He wasn't at the meeting, and as they say, the absent are always in the wrong." />

## Life and people

<VocabTable group="life" />

*Après la pluie, le beau temps* comforts someone after a difficulty. *Tout vient à point à qui sait attendre* praises patience. *Qui se ressemble s'assemble* notes that people of the same kind stay together, often with a hint of criticism.

<Example fr="Courage, après la pluie, le beau temps !" en="Chin up, things will get better!" />
<Example fr="Sois patient : tout vient à point à qui sait attendre." en="Be patient: good things come to those who wait." />
<Example fr="Ils sont toujours ensemble : qui se ressemble s'assemble." en="They're always together: birds of a feather flock together." />

## Half-quoting and twisting proverbs

Because everyone knows them, French speakers often say only the first half of a proverb, and leave the listener to finish it: *bon, qui ne risque rien…*, *tu sais ce qu'on dit : petit à petit…*. Saying the whole proverb can even sound a little heavy in conversation.

Writers and advertisers also twist proverbs for effect, changing one word: a sports article might say that a team *a vendu la peau de l'ours trop tôt*. Recognizing the original is the key to the joke.

<Example fr="L'équipe a vendu la peau de l'ours trop tôt : elle a perdu dans les dernières minutes." en="The team counted its chickens too early: it lost in the last minutes." />

## Translating proverbs

Don't translate a proverb word for word by default. Look for the proverb that does the same job in the other language. Sometimes the two match closely (*mieux vaut tard que jamais*, better late than never); often they use different images (*il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours*, don't count your chickens). If there's no equivalent, translate the meaning plainly.

As a rule of thumb, use one proverb per conversation or text at most: a proverb gives weight to a point, but several in a row sound like a parody.

## En français

Inès has been offered a job abroad. She talks to her grandmother.

- *Mamie, on m'a proposé un poste à Montréal, mais j'ai peur.* (Grandma, I've been offered a job in Montreal, but I'm scared.)
- *Peur de quoi ? Qui ne risque rien…* (Scared of what? Nothing ventured…)
- *… n'a rien, je sais. Mais je ne parle pas encore bien anglais.* (… nothing gained, I know. But I don't speak English well yet.)
- *C'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron. Tu apprendras sur place.* (Practice makes perfect. You'll learn there.)
- *Et si ça ne marche pas ?* (And what if it doesn't work out?)
- *Tu n'as pas encore commencé ! Il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours…* (You haven't even started! Don't count your chickens…)
- *Mamie, ce proverbe parle d'un succès, pas d'un échec.* (Grandma, that proverb is about success, not failure.)
- *Tu as raison. Alors : après la pluie, le beau temps !* (You're right. Then: things will get better!)
- *Tu as un proverbe pour tout.* (You have a proverb for everything.)
- *À mon âge, on en a appris quelques-uns. Et ne décide pas ce soir.* (At my age, you've learned a few. And don't decide tonight.)
- *La nuit porte conseil, c'est ça ?* (Sleep on it, is that it?)
- *Exactement. Mieux vaut décider tard que mal.* (Exactly. Better to decide late than badly.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Proverbs have a fixed form">
  Learners often change a word in a proverb, which makes it sound wrong rather than creative. Keep the exact form: **l'habit ne fait pas le moine**, **les absents ont toujours tort**. Twist a proverb only when you know the original well and want a deliberate effect.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn the first half">
  For each proverb, learn the first half by heart and say the rest aloud only when you're sure. French speakers will finish it for you, and recognizing the first half in a conversation is what matters most.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un proverbe est une phrase courte et fixe qui donne un conseil ou dit une vérité sur la vie. On ne change pas ses mots. Certains proverbes encouragent l'effort : qui ne risque rien n'a rien, petit à petit, l'oiseau fait son nid, c'est en forgeant qu'on devient forgeron. D'autres conseillent d'être prudent : il ne faut pas vendre la peau de l'ours avant de l'avoir tué, l'habit ne fait pas le moine, la nuit porte conseil. Après la pluie, le beau temps fait du bien après une difficulté. À l'oral, on dit souvent seulement le début du proverbe, et l'autre personne comprend la suite. Pour traduire un proverbe, il faut chercher un proverbe qui a le même sens, et pas traduire mot à mot. Mieux vaut dire un seul proverbe à la fois.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand and use idioms for arguing, negotiating and working together, and judge when each one fits the register.

## What you already know

You know everyday idioms such as *mettre les pieds dans le plat*, *avoir du pain sur la planche* and *appeler un chat un chat* from <LessonLink id="b2-idioms-everyday" text="the lesson on everyday idioms" />, and idioms with *y* and *en* (*s'en sortir*, *en rester là*) from <LessonLink id="b2-idiomatic-y-en" text="the lesson on idiomatic y and en" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-proverbs-and-sayings" text="the lesson on proverbs" />, you saw whole sentences with a fixed form. This lesson adds idioms you'll hear in meetings, debates and the news.

## A note on register

Idioms are typical of speech and of lively writing such as journalism, columns and speeches. Most of the idioms here are everyday French: fine in a conversation at work, in an email to a colleague and in a newspaper article, but out of place in a formal letter or an academic essay. Two are familiar (*mettre la main à la pâte*, *payer les pots cassés*). The notes in the tables give a label for each.

## Arguing

<VocabTable group="argue" />

An argument that *tient la route* is solid. Someone who *n'y va pas par quatre chemins* says things directly; someone who *tourne autour du pot* avoids the point. *Noyer le poisson* is to hide an awkward question under other topics, and it's often used to criticize a politician or a manager. *Enfoncer une porte ouverte* is to state the obvious.

<Example fr="Ton argument ne tient pas la route : les chiffres disent le contraire." en="Your argument doesn't hold up: the figures say the opposite." />
<Example fr="La directrice n'y est pas allée par quatre chemins : le projet est annulé." en="The director didn't beat about the bush: the project is cancelled." />
<Example fr="Au lieu de répondre, le ministre a noyé le poisson." en="Instead of answering, the minister dodged the question." />
<Example fr="Dire qu'il faut écouter les clients, c'est enfoncer une porte ouverte." en="Saying that we must listen to customers is stating the obvious." />

## Negotiating and conflict

<VocabTable group="deal" />

In a negotiation, each side may have to *mettre de l'eau dans son vin* (tone down its demands). When the two positions are far apart, someone suggests *couper la poire en deux*. A manager who *ménage la chèvre et le chou* tries not to upset anyone. *Prendre le taureau par les cornes* is to face a problem head on, and *jeter l'éponge* is to give up.

<Example fr="Pour trouver un accord, chacun devra mettre de l'eau dans son vin." en="To reach an agreement, everyone will have to compromise." />
<Example fr="Vous demandez dix, nous proposons six : coupons la poire en deux." en="You're asking for ten, we're offering six: let's split the difference." />
<Example fr="Il essaie de ménager la chèvre et le chou, et il ne satisfait personne." en="He tries to please both sides, and he satisfies nobody." />
<Example fr="Il est temps de prendre le taureau par les cornes." en="It's time to take the bull by the horns." />

## Work and money

<VocabTable group="work" />

At work, you may *avoir carte blanche* (a free hand) for a project. Starting before the basics are in place is *mettre la charrue avant les bœufs*. Colleagues facing the same difficulty are *dans le même bateau*. Doing two things at once is *faire d'une pierre deux coups*, and joining in the work is *mettre la main à la pâte*. When money is short, everyone has to *se serrer la ceinture*, and those who weren't responsible may *payer les pots cassés*.

<Example fr="On a carte blanche pour organiser la soirée." en="We have a free hand to organize the party. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Recruter avant d'avoir le budget, c'est mettre la charrue avant les bœufs." en="Hiring before having the budget is putting the cart before the horse." />
<Example fr="Nous sommes tous dans le même bateau : il faudra se serrer la ceinture." en="We're all in the same boat: we'll have to tighten our belts." />
<Example fr="Ce sont toujours les salariés qui paient les pots cassés." en="It's always the employees who pay for others' mistakes. (familiar)" />

## Using idioms well

An idiom works when it fits the situation and the listener knows it. As a rule of thumb, use one idiom per short exchange, and keep its fixed words: *dans le même bateau*, not *dans un même bateau*. Many idioms also have a negative form that's very common: *ça ne tient pas la route*.

When translating, look for the English idiom that does the same job. Some share the image (*prendre le taureau par les cornes*); most don't (*noyer le poisson*, to dodge the question). If no idiom fits, translate the meaning.

## En français

Nadia and Julien prepare a meeting about their team's budget.

- *Bon, je n'y vais pas par quatre chemins : notre budget baisse de vingt pour cent.* (Right, I won't beat about the bush: our budget is going down by twenty per cent.)
- *Vingt pour cent ? On va devoir se serrer la ceinture.* (Twenty per cent? We'll have to tighten our belts. (spoken))
- *Et la directrice a carte blanche pour choisir les projets.* (And the director has a free hand to choose the projects.)
- *Alors ce sont nos projets qui vont payer les pots cassés.* (Then it's our projects that will pay for it. (familiar))
- *Pas forcément. Si on propose un bon plan, ça peut tenir la route.* (Not necessarily. If we propose a good plan, it can hold up. (spoken))
- *Et si on réunissait les deux projets en un seul ? On ferait d'une pierre deux coups.* (And what if we combined the two projects into one? We'd kill two birds with one stone. (spoken))
- *Bonne idée. Mais attention à ne pas mettre la charrue avant les bœufs : d'abord les chiffres.* (Good idea. But careful not to put the cart before the horse: figures first.)
- *Tu as raison. Et en réunion, pas question de noyer le poisson.* (You're right. And in the meeting, no dodging the question.)
- *D'accord. Si la directrice demande trop, on coupera la poire en deux.* (Agreed. If the director asks for too much, we'll split the difference.)
- *Et si elle refuse tout ?* (And if she refuses everything?)
- *On ne jette pas l'éponge. Nous sommes tous dans le même bateau.* (We don't give up. We're all in the same boat. (spoken))
- *Alors au travail : tout le monde met la main à la pâte !* (Then let's get to work: everyone mucks in! (familiar))

<Callout type="warning" title="Dans le même bateau, not dans un même bateau">
  Learners often change an article or a word in an idiom, which makes it sound wrong. Idioms have a fixed form: **nous sommes dans le même bateau**, **mettre la charrue avant les bœufs**, **couper la poire en deux**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Collect idioms by situation">
  Note new idioms under the situation where you'd use them (a negotiation, a meeting, a disagreement) rather than in an alphabetical list. When the situation comes up, the idioms come with it.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Les expressions imagées sont courantes à l'oral, au travail et dans la presse, mais on les évite dans une lettre officielle ou une dissertation. Pour parler d'un argument, on dit qu'il tient la route, ou qu'il ne la tient pas. Une personne directe n'y va pas par quatre chemins, et une personne qui évite le sujet tourne autour du pot ou noie le poisson. Dans une négociation, chacun met de l'eau dans son vin, et on peut couper la poire en deux. Ménager la chèvre et le chou, c'est essayer de plaire aux deux côtés. Au travail, on peut avoir carte blanche, faire d'une pierre deux coups ou mettre la main à la pâte. Quand l'argent manque, on se serre la ceinture. Ces expressions ont une forme fixe : il ne faut pas changer leurs mots.

## What you already know

You know *bilingue*, *une langue officielle*, *une langue nationale* and *le plurilinguisme* from <LessonLink id="b2-culture-francophone-africa" text="the lesson on francophone Africa" />, and *le patrimoine* from <LessonLink id="b2-passive-voice" text="the lesson on the passive" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-law-and-institutions" text="the lesson on law and institutions" />, you saw the Constitution and the courts. This lesson looks at the languages spoken in France besides French.

## One official language, many languages

<VocabTable group="status" />

France has one official language. Since 1992, the Constitution has said: *la langue de la République est le français*. But France is also home to many *langues régionales*, which were the everyday languages of much of the population for centuries. Since 2008, the Constitution has added that *les langues régionales appartiennent au patrimoine de la France*.

The relationship between French and the regional languages is a long debate. For some, a single language guarantees *l'unité* of the Republic and equal access to public life; for others, recognizing regional languages protects a heritage that is disappearing. France signed the European *charte* on regional or minority languages in 1999, but has not *ratifié* it. This lesson presents the positions, not a verdict.

<Example fr="Pour certains, une seule langue garantit l'unité de la République." en="For some, a single language guarantees the unity of the Republic." />

<Example fr="La France a signé la charte, mais elle ne l'a pas ratifiée." en="France signed the charter, but it hasn't ratified it." />

Linguists distinguish between a language and *un dialecte* (a regional variety of a language), but the line is often debated, and speakers themselves don't always agree.

## The main regional languages

<VocabTable group="languages" />

Each language below gets a short portrait. The lesson gives no speaker figures: they're estimated in different ways, and they change.

**Le breton.** Spoken in western Brittany, Breton is *une langue celtique*, related to Welsh and Cornish. It's often seen on road signs and in festival names.

**L'occitan.** Occitan is *une langue romane* of the south of France, with several varieties (Provençal, Gascon, Languedocien…). It was a major literary language in the Middle Ages, the language of the troubadours.

**L'alsacien.** In Alsace, *l'alsacien* is a Germanic dialect, close to the German spoken across the Rhine.

**Le basque.** Basque is spoken on both sides of the Pyrenees, in France and Spain. It doesn't belong to any of the main European language families, and its origin is still debated.

**Le corse.** Corsican is a Romance language, close to the Italian of Tuscany.

**Les créoles.** In Guadeloupe, Martinique, Guyane and La Réunion, *les créoles* were born from contact between French and other languages. They're full languages, with their own grammar, not "broken French".

France has other regional languages too, such as Catalan, Picard and Flemish in the north.

<Example fr="Le basque ne fait partie d'aucune grande famille de langues européennes." en="Basque isn't part of any of the main European language families." />

## Regional French

Even where the regional languages are rarely spoken, they leave traces in the French of each region: an accent, a few words, a turn of phrase. Linguists call this *le français régional*.

<VocabTable group="regional" />
 In the south-west, for example, many people say *une chocolatine* where Paris says *un pain au chocolat*; the two words cause friendly arguments across France. In the south, the final *e* of words is often pronounced, as in Occitan.

<Example fr="Dans le sud-ouest, on demande souvent une chocolatine." en="In the south-west, people often ask for a chocolatine. (regional)" />

These differences are labelled as regional in dictionaries; they're not mistakes. As a learner, recognize them, and keep to standard French when you write, unless you're quoting someone.

## Language and identity

For many people, a regional language is less a daily tool than a link with family, a place and a history. Singing in Breton at a festival, putting a Basque name on a shop or learning a few words of Creole from a grandparent are ways of keeping that link. Debates about regional languages are therefore often debates about identity, and they can be sensitive: as elsewhere in this course, describe positions rather than judge them.

## Teaching and passing on

<VocabTable group="today" />

Today, regional languages can be studied at school, from optional classes to bilingual streams. *Des écoles immersives*, run by associations, teach mainly in the regional language: Diwan schools for Breton, Calandretas for Occitan and Ikastolak for Basque. *Des panneaux bilingues* show town names in two languages in several regions.

For most regional languages, the key question is *la transmission*: whether parents still speak the language to their children. Where family transmission has weakened, schools and associations try to take over.

<Example fr="Dans les écoles immersives, la majorité des cours ont lieu en breton." en="In immersion schools, the majority of lessons are taught in Breton." />
<Example fr="L'enseignement des langues régionales existe dans de nombreuses écoles." en="Regional languages are taught in many schools." />

This lesson looks at the French-speaking world as a whole.

## Francophonie and Francophonie

<VocabTable group="institution" />

With a small *f*, *la francophonie* is all the people and countries that use French. With a capital, *la Francophonie* is the set of institutions that bring together French-speaking countries. The main one is *l'OIF*, l'Organisation internationale de la Francophonie, based in Paris. Its origins go back to an agency created in 1970 at a conference in Niamey, in Niger.

The OIF brings together *des pays membres* where French has very different roles: an official language, a language of school, a language of culture, or a language shared by part of the population. About every two years, the heads of State and government meet at *un sommet*. The OIF works on the French language, education, culture and cooperation between its members.

<Example fr="Les chefs d'État des pays membres se réunissent lors d'un sommet." en="The heads of State of the member countries meet at a summit." />

French is also *une langue de travail* in many international organizations, including the United Nations and the European Union, and one of the official languages of the Olympic movement.

<Example fr="Le français est une langue de travail des Nations unies." en="French is a working language of the United Nations." />

The spread of French outside Europe is linked to *la colonisation*. After independence, many countries kept French as an official or working language, alongside their national languages. Historians and the countries concerned discuss this history; this lesson states the facts and doesn't judge.

## French in Europe

<VocabTable group="europe" />

**La Belgique.** Belgium has three official languages: French, *le néerlandais* (Dutch) and *l'allemand* (German). French is spoken mainly in Wallonia and in Brussels. Belgians say *septante* (70) and *nonante* (90).

**La Suisse.** Switzerland has four national languages: German, French, Italian and *le romanche*. French is spoken in the west. Many Swiss French speakers say *septante* and *nonante*, and in some *cantons* also *huitante* (80). Swiss citizens often vote on specific questions in *une votation*.

<Example fr="La semaine prochaine, les Suisses votent lors d'une votation sur les transports." en="Next week, the Swiss are voting in a referendum on transport." />

**Le Luxembourg.** You met it at A2: French is one of the country's languages, alongside Luxembourgish and German.

<Example fr="Dans certains cantons suisses, on dit huitante pour quatre-vingts." en="In some Swiss cantons, people say huitante for eighty. (regional)" />

## French in the Americas and overseas

<VocabTable group="world" />

<Example fr="En Guyane et à Mayotte, le français est la langue officielle." en="In French Guiana and Mayotte, French is the official language." />
<Example fr="La Nouvelle-Calédonie est un territoire très loin de Paris." en="New Caledonia is a territory very far from Paris." />

**Le Canada.** Canada is officially bilingual at the federal level; Québec has French as its only official language, as you saw at B1. Acadians in the east also speak French.

**Haïti.** French and Haitian Creole are both official languages.

**Les outre-mer.** In Guadeloupe, Martinique, *la Guyane*, La Réunion and *Mayotte*, French is the official language, alongside Creoles and other local languages. Other *territoires*, such as New Caledonia and French Polynesia, have their own status.

## Status at a glance

The status of French differs from one country to another, and the words for it are precise.

| Where | Status of French |
|---|---|
| France, Québec, the overseas departments | the only official language |
| Belgium, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Canada (federal) | one of several official languages |
| many African countries | an official language, or the language of school and administration, alongside national languages |
| international organizations | an official or working language |

As a rule of thumb, ask two questions about any country: is French official, and is it the language people speak at home? The two answers are often different. In many African countries, French is the language of school and administration, while most people speak a national language at home.

## French in Africa today

You saw at B2 that African French creates its own words: *une essencerie* (a petrol station) in Senegal, *s'enjailler* (to have fun) in Côte d'Ivoire. These words are labelled as regional, like *septante* or *magasiner*. Young speakers in cities such as Abidjan also mix French with local languages in new ways, and some of their words reach dictionaries.

<Example fr="Au Sénégal, on peut dire une essencerie pour une station-service." en="In Senegal, you can say une essencerie for a petrol station. (regional)" />

## Varieties and regionalisms

French isn't one single language spoken the same way everywhere: it has *des variétés*. Each has its accent and its *régionalismes*, words used in one area. A careful speaker labels them: *(Belgique)*, *(Suisse)*, *(Québec)*. None is "better" French; in international writing, standard French is the common reference.

<Example fr="Le mot courriel est un régionalisme du Québec qu'on dit aussi en France." en="The word courriel is a Québec regionalism that is also used in France." />

## A short article

The article below is an original text written for this lesson.

*Le français, une langue en partage*

*Le français n'est pas seulement la langue de la France. Il est partagé par de nombreux pays, sur tous les continents, et la majorité des personnes qui le parlent vivent aujourd'hui sur d'autres continents que l'Europe. Les mots voyagent aussi : courriel, venu du Québec, est entré dans le français de France.*

*En Europe, il est une langue officielle en Belgique, en Suisse et au Luxembourg, où il vit à côté d'autres langues. On y compte parfois autrement : septante, nonante, et même huitante dans certains cantons suisses.*

*En Amérique, le Québec a fait du français sa seule langue officielle, tandis que le Canada est bilingue. En Afrique, le français est arrivé avec la colonisation ; après leur indépendance, beaucoup de pays l'ont gardé comme langue officielle ou langue de l'école, à côté de leurs langues nationales. Dans les rues d'Abidjan, le français se mélange aux langues locales et crée des mots nouveaux, que les dictionnaires finissent parfois par accueillir.*

*Pour faire vivre ce partage, les pays membres de l'OIF se réunissent tous les deux ans environ lors d'un sommet. Ils parlent de l'enseignement, de la culture et de la place du français dans le monde.*

*Cette langue commune n'est pas pour autant une langue unique. Chaque pays a ses mots, ses accents et ses expressions. Un Belge, une Québécoise et un Ivoirien se comprennent, mais chacun reconnaît vite d'où vient l'autre. C'est sans doute la force de la francophonie : une langue partagée, et mille façons de la parler.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(French, a shared language. French isn't only the language of France. It is shared by many countries, on every continent, and the majority of the people who speak it now live on continents other than Europe. Words travel too: courriel, which came from Québec, has entered the French of France. In Europe, it is an official language in Belgium, Switzerland and Luxembourg, where it lives alongside other languages. People there sometimes count differently: septante, nonante, and even huitante in some Swiss cantons. In America, Québec has made French its only official language, while Canada is bilingual. In Africa, French arrived with colonization; after independence, many countries kept it as an official language or a language of school, alongside their national languages. In the streets of Abidjan, French mixes with local languages and creates new words, which dictionaries sometimes end up accepting. To keep this sharing alive, the member countries of the OIF meet about every two years at a summit. They talk about education, culture and the place of French in the world. This common language isn't a single, uniform language, though. Each country has its words, its accents and its expressions. A Belgian man, a Québec woman and an Ivorian man understand each other, but each quickly recognizes where the other comes from. That is probably the strength of the French-speaking world: one shared language, and a thousand ways of speaking it.)

<Callout type="warning" title="La Francophonie, la francophonie">
  Learners often mix up the two spellings. With a capital, **la Francophonie** is the institution (the OIF and its summits); with a small letter, **la francophonie** is the French-speaking world itself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Label what you hear">
  When you meet a word you don't find in a standard dictionary, check whether it's a regionalism, and note it with its label: *septante (Belgique, Suisse)*, *magasiner (Québec)*. You'll understand more speakers, and you'll know which words to keep for which audience.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to rewrite one message in four registers, from slang to administrative French and back, and to avoid the mistake of mixing them.

## What you already know

You know the three registers *familier*, *courant* and *soutenu*, with pairs such as *bagnole*, *voiture*, *automobile*, from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />. You know spoken forms (*t'es*, *y a*, *ouais*, *du coup*) from <LessonLink id="b2-colloquial-french" text="the lesson on spoken, everyday French" />, and slang (*le fric*, *relou*, *c'est la galère*, *kiffer*) from <LessonLink id="b2-culture-verlan-and-slang" text="the lesson on verlan and slang" />. In <LessonLink id="c1-formal-correspondence" text="the lesson on high-register correspondence" />, you wrote to institutions. This lesson asks you to move between all of them on purpose.

## Four levels

<VocabTable group="levels" />

For this lesson, think of four levels:

- **Slang and familiar French**: friends, family, text messages. *Ça craint, on se gèle.*
- **Everyday standard French**: colleagues, neighbours, most emails. *Il fait froid, le chauffage est en panne.*
- **Formal French**: a manager, a client, a public talk. *Le chauffage ne fonctionne plus depuis lundi.*
- ***Le registre administratif***: letters to a landlord, a town hall, a company's services. *Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir faire intervenir un technicien.*

To *monter d'un registre* (move up) or *descendre d'un registre* (move down), you change several things at once: the words, the grammar, the length of the sentences and the formulas.

<Example fr="Pour écrire au propriétaire, il faut monter d'un registre." en="To write to the landlord, you need to move up a register." />

## What changes

Each step up the scale changes four things.

- **Words**: *le fric* → *l'argent*; *l'appart* → *l'appartement* → *le logement*; *le chauffage est mort* → *en panne* → *ne fonctionne plus*.
- **Grammar**: dropped *ne* comes back, *on* becomes *nous*, *ça* becomes *cela*, intonation questions become inversion or formulas.
- **Sentence length**: slang uses short, broken sentences; administrative French uses longer sentences, with participle clauses and connectors.
- **Formulas**: *salut* and *bises* become *Madame, Monsieur* and a full closing formula.

<VocabTable group="slang" />

At the bottom of the scale, slang words carry emotion: *ça craint* (that's bad), *galérer* (to struggle), *se geler* (to be freezing). Short forms such as *l'appart* belong to the same level. They're natural with friends and wrong in any formal text.

<Example fr="On galère depuis lundi, et on se gèle dans l'appart." en="We've been struggling since Monday, and we're freezing in the flat. (familiar)" />

<VocabTable group="formal" />

At the top, administrative French uses precise nouns and set formulas: *le locataire* and *le propriétaire*, *un technicien* who will *intervenir*, *dans les meilleurs délais*, *faire le nécessaire*.

<Example fr="Je vous prie de bien vouloir faire le nécessaire dans les meilleurs délais." en="Please do what is necessary as soon as possible. (formal)" />

## Shifting up and down

Moving up, work in this order: replace the slang words, restore the grammar (*ne*, *nous*, *cela*), add the formulas, and only then lengthen the sentences. Moving down, do the reverse: cut the formulas, shorten the sentences, and choose everyday words before you add any slang.

As a rule of thumb, don't overshoot. A message to a colleague written in administrative French sounds cold or ironic; a letter to a landlord with a slang word sounds careless. Choose the level for the reader, then keep it.

## Who is reading?

Before you write, ask three questions: who is the reader, what do you want from them, and will the message be kept? A text to a friend is read once and forgotten; a letter to a landlord may be read again months later, by someone else. As a rule of thumb, the more official the reader and the longer the message will be kept, the higher the register. With the same reader, the level can also change with the situation: you may write *salut* to a colleague in a chat, but *bonjour* in an email that your manager will also read.

<Example fr="Je vous écris pour vous signaler un problème de chauffage." en="I'm writing to inform you of a heating problem. (formal)" />

## Mixing registers

<Example fr="Ce message est un mélange des registres : il commence par Madame et finit par bises." en="This message mixes registers: it starts with Madame and ends with bises." />

The most common mistake at C1 isn't choosing the wrong register: it's mixing two in the same text. A formal letter with *un truc*, or a text to a friend with *je vous saurais gré de*, sounds strange or comic. Reread each message once, only for register, and replace every word that belongs to another level. Mixing on purpose is a source of humour, as you saw in <LessonLink id="c1-culture-satire-and-humour" text="the lesson on satire and humour" />, but only when the reader sees the joke.

## One message, four registers

The four messages below are original texts written for this lesson. They all say the same thing: the heating in a flat has broken down. The people are invented.

*1. Texto à un ami*

*Salut Karim ! Le chauffage est mort depuis lundi, on se gèle dans l'appart. Ça craint grave. Tu peux me passer un radiateur ce soir ? Je te le rends dès que c'est réparé. Merci, t'es top !*

*2. Message à une voisine*

*Bonjour Madame Garnier, le chauffage de notre immeuble est en panne depuis lundi. Il fait très froid chez nous, et les enfants dorment avec deux pulls. Est-ce que vous avez le même problème ? Si c'est le cas, on pourrait écrire ensemble au propriétaire : à deux, on sera peut-être plus vite entendus. Bonne soirée, Lucas, du troisième étage.*

*3. Courriel à son responsable*

*Bonjour Monsieur Dubois, je vous écris pour vous prévenir que j'arriverai vers dix heures demain matin. Le chauffage de mon logement ne fonctionne plus depuis lundi, et je dois passer à l'agence de mon propriétaire avant de venir au bureau. Je serai bien sûr présent à la réunion de onze heures. Bien cordialement, Lucas Martin.*

*4. Lettre au propriétaire*

*Monsieur,*

*Locataire de l'appartement situé au troisième étage, je me permets de vous signaler que le chauffage ne fonctionne plus depuis lundi. La température du logement est désormais très basse, ce qui rend la vie difficile à toute la famille. Malgré deux appels à votre agence, le problème persiste. Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir faire intervenir un technicien dans les meilleurs délais. Je reste à votre disposition pour toute information.*

*Je vous prie d'agréer, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*

*Lucas Martin*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(1. Text to a friend. Hi Karim! The heating's been dead since Monday, we're freezing in the flat. It's seriously bad. Can you lend me a heater tonight? I'll give it back as soon as it's fixed. Thanks, you're the best! (slang) 2. Message to a neighbour. Hello Madame Garnier, the heating in our building has been broken since Monday. It's very cold in our flat, and the children are sleeping in two jumpers. Do you have the same problem? If so, we could write to the landlord together: with two of us, we may be heard more quickly. Have a good evening, Lucas, from the third floor. 3. Email to his manager. Hello Mr Dubois, I'm writing to let you know that I'll arrive at about ten tomorrow morning. The heating in my flat hasn't worked since Monday, and I need to go to my landlord's agency before coming to the office. I'll of course be at the eleven o'clock meeting. Kind regards, Lucas Martin. 4. Letter to the landlord. Dear Sir, As the tenant of the third-floor flat, I am writing to inform you that the heating has not been working since Monday. The temperature in the flat is now very low, which makes life difficult for the whole family. Despite two calls to your agency, the problem persists. I would be grateful if you would arrange for a technician to come as soon as possible. I remain at your disposal for any further information. Yours faithfully, Lucas Martin.)

## How the four versions differ

- **Words**: *le chauffage est mort* (1), *en panne* (2), *ne fonctionne plus* (3, 4); *l'appart* (1), *chez nous* (2), *mon logement* (3), *le logement* (4).
- **Grammar**: *t'es* and an intonation question in 1; *est-ce que* in 2; full sentences in 3; an apposition (*locataire de l'appartement…*) and *je vous saurais gré de* in 4.
- **Formulas**: *salut* and *t'es top* (1); *bonjour Madame Garnier* and *bonne soirée* (2); *bonjour Monsieur Dubois* and *bien cordialement* (3); *Monsieur* and the full closing formula (4).
- **Content**: each version gives only what its reader needs: a favour (1), a common problem and an offer to act together (2), the effect on work (3), the facts and a formal request (4).

## En français

Lucas asks his colleague Inès to check the register of his draft letter to the landlord.

- *Inès, tu as cinq minutes ? J'aimerais ton avis sur ma lettre au propriétaire.* (Inès, have you got five minutes? I'd like your opinion on my letter to the landlord.)
- *Bien sûr, montre-moi. Le début est parfait : « je me permets de vous signaler ».* (Of course, show me. The start is perfect: "je me permets de vous signaler".)
- *Et la suite ? J'ai écrit « le chauffage est mort ». C'est trop familier ?* (And the rest? I wrote "le chauffage est mort". Is that too familiar?)
- *Oui, dans une lettre, on écrit plutôt « le chauffage ne fonctionne plus ».* (Yes, in a letter you'd rather write "le chauffage ne fonctionne plus".)
- *D'accord. Et à la fin, j'ai mis « merci d'avance, bonne journée ».* (OK. And at the end, I put "merci d'avance, bonne journée".)
- *Ça, c'est un mélange des registres. Mets la formule complète : « Je vous prie d'agréer… ».* (That's a mix of registers. Put the full formula: "Je vous prie d'agréer…".)
- *C'est long, mais tu as raison. Tu penses qu'il va répondre vite ?* (It's long, but you're right. Do you think he'll answer quickly?)
- *Je ne sais pas. Mais il saura exactement ce que tu demandes.* (I don't know. But he'll know exactly what you're asking for.)
- *À Karim, j'ai envoyé un texto : « on se gèle, ça craint ».* (To Karim, I sent a text: "on se gèle, ça craint".)
- *Là, c'est parfait ! Avec un ami, une lettre administrative serait bizarre.* (That's perfect! With a friend, an administrative letter would be strange.)
- *Donc il faut savoir monter et descendre d'un registre.* (So you need to know how to move up and down a register.)
- *Exactement. Le même message, mais pas les mêmes mots.* (Exactly. The same message, but not the same words.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't mix registers in one text">
  The commonest C1 mistake is a familiar word in a formal message, or the reverse. In a letter to a landlord, write **le chauffage ne fonctionne plus**, not *le chauffage est mort*; to a friend, **tu peux…?**, not *je vous saurais gré de*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Write the same message four times">
  Once a week, take one small event (a late train, a lost key) and write it four times: a text to a friend, a message to a neighbour, an email to a manager and a formal letter. It takes ten minutes, and it trains exactly the skill this lesson teaches.
</Callout>

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These three words help you talk about how people speak. *Une variante* is another form of the same word or phrase: *huitante* and *quatre-vingts* are two variants of the same number. *Le parler* of a place or a group is the way people speak there, with its accent, its words and its turns of phrase. *Une expression régionale* is a word or phrase used in one region; you met the same idea as *un régionalisme* in the lesson on La Francophonie, and the two are close in meaning.

<Example fr="Le parler de cette ville a ses propres mots et son propre accent." en="The local speech of this town has its own words and its own accent." />

## Three registers, one message

A proverb, an idiom, a slang word and a formal formula are all correct French, but each one has its place. As a rule of thumb, write the three versions in this order:

1. **Start in the middle**, with the everyday standard version. It holds all the facts: what, when, where, who.
2. **Go down** for a friend: cut the formulas, shorten the sentences, add one or two familiar words, and keep only the facts your friend needs.
3. **Go up** for an institution: replace every familiar word, use the precise nouns, add the request formula and the full closing formula, and check that every fact matches the standard version.

If a fact changes in one version, change it in the other two.

## Where proverbs and idioms fit

A proverb or an idiom gives a message warmth and colour, but not every register welcomes it. As a rule of thumb:

- **In a text to a friend**: idioms are natural, often in their most familiar form (*faut mettre la main à la pâte*, with the *il* dropped, as people say it).
- **In a message to neighbours or colleagues**: one proverb, introduced by *comme dit le proverbe*, works well as a friendly conclusion.
- **In an administrative letter**: leave them out. A letter to an institution states facts and makes a request; a proverb there sounds like a joke.

Use one at a time: two proverbs in a short message sound like a lesson.

## Regional words across registers

Regional words don't belong to a register. For a writer from a Swiss canton where *huitante* is used, *huitante* and *le souper* are the normal words, in a text to a friend as much as in a letter to the town council. That's why, in the model below, they appear in all three versions.

This differs from slang. *Ça craint* is familiar everywhere; *huitante* is standard in some Swiss cantons and not used in others. So the question to ask isn't "is this word formal?" but "will my reader know it?". As the lesson on regional languages advised, recognize these words, and, as a learner writing to a reader in France, keep to *quatre-vingts* and *le dîner*. When you quote a Swiss speaker or write for a Swiss reader, the regional words are the right choice.

## A model message

These messages are original texts written for this lesson. The town, the association and the people are invented. The writer, Léa, lives in a Swiss town where *huitante* is used.

*1. Texto à une amie*

*Salut Chloé ! Le souper du quartier, c'est mort pour samedi : il va pleuvoir toute la journée. On le fait samedi prochain, même heure. On est déjà huitante, t'imagines ? Du coup, faut que tout le monde mette la main à la pâte pour les tables. Tu viens nous aider ? Bises !*

*2. Message aux voisins*

*Chers voisins, à cause de la pluie annoncée pour samedi, le souper du quartier est reporté au samedi suivant, à la même heure, sur la place du Marché. Huitante personnes ont déjà répondu oui : merci à tous ! Nous cherchons encore quelques personnes pour installer les tables à partir de dix-sept heures. Si vous pouvez aider, répondez à ce message. Comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps. À samedi prochain ! Léa Favre, pour l'association de quartier.*

*3. Lettre à la commune*

*Madame, Monsieur,*

*En raison des fortes pluies annoncées pour samedi, l'association de quartier que je représente a décidé de reporter son souper au samedi suivant. Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir nous autoriser à installer notre souper sur la place du Marché ce jour-là, aux mêmes horaires, de dix-huit heures à vingt-trois heures. Huitante habitants ont déjà annoncé qu'ils seraient présents. Nous installerons les tables nous-mêmes à partir de dix-sept heures, et nous nous engageons à libérer et à nettoyer la place à la fin de la soirée. Je reste à votre disposition pour toute information.*

*Veuillez agréer, Madame, Monsieur, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*

*Léa Favre, présidente de l'association de quartier*

(1. Text to a friend. Hi Chloé! The neighbourhood supper's dead for Saturday: it's going to rain all day. We're doing it next Saturday, same time. There are already eighty of us, can you imagine? So everyone has to lend a hand with the tables. Are you coming to help us? Love! (familiar; huitante and souper are regional, Switzerland) 2. Message to the neighbours. Dear neighbours, because of the rain forecast for Saturday, the neighbourhood supper is postponed to the following Saturday, at the same time, on the Place du Marché. Eighty people have already said yes: thank you all! We're still looking for a few people to set up the tables from five o'clock. If you can help, reply to this message. As the saying goes, every cloud has a silver lining. See you next Saturday! Léa Favre, for the neighbourhood association. 3. Letter to the town council. Dear Sir or Madam, Owing to the heavy rain forecast for Saturday, the neighbourhood association that I represent has decided to postpone its supper to the following Saturday. I would be grateful if you would allow us to hold our supper on the Place du Marché on that day, at the same times, from six to eleven in the evening. Eighty residents have already said that they will attend. We will set up the tables ourselves from five o'clock, and we undertake to clear and clean the square at the end of the evening. I remain at your disposal for any information. Yours faithfully, Léa Favre, president of the neighbourhood association.)

## How the message uses the unit

- **Registers**: *c'est mort*, *t'imagines*, *du coup* and *faut que* with the *il* dropped (1); the neutral passive *est reporté* and *chers voisins* (2); *en raison de*, *je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir*, *nous nous engageons à* and the full closing formula (3).
- **One proverb and one idiom**: *comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps* in the message to the neighbours, where it is both literal and figurative; *mettre la main à la pâte* in the text to a friend. The letter has neither.
- **Two regional words**, labelled: *huitante* and *le souper* appear in all three versions, because they are Léa's normal words.
- **The same facts**: the supper moves to the following Saturday, at the same time, on the same square, with eighty people already coming; the tables go up from five o'clock.

## Your turn

Choose one small event in your own life: a postponed party, a cancelled class, a change of address. Write it three times, in about sixty words each: a text to a friend, a message to neighbours or colleagues, and a letter to an institution. Use one proverb and one idiom, each in a version where it fits, and keep both out of the letter. If you know a regional word from a French-speaking country, use it in a version written for a reader from that country, and label it.

## Check your work

- Do the three versions give the same facts, with no detail changed between them?
- Does each version keep one register from start to finish, with no familiar word in the letter and no formal formula in the text?
- Is the proverb introduced naturally, and is it in the right version?
- Does the letter open with *Madame, Monsieur* and end with a full closing formula?
- Is every regional word labelled, and would your reader know it?

<Callout type="warning" title="No proverb in an administrative letter">
  Learners who have just learned a proverb often want to use it everywhere. In a letter to an institution, it sounds like a joke. Write **en raison des fortes pluies annoncées**, not *comme dit le proverbe, après la pluie, le beau temps*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a list of local words">
  When you watch a film or read a newspaper from Belgium, Switzerland, Québec or Africa, note each word you don't know from France in a separate list, with the country beside it. Over a few months, that list will help you understand speakers from many places, not only from Paris.
</Callout>
