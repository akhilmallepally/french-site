By the end of this lesson you'll be able to read and use the vocabulary of French law and institutions: how a text becomes law, which courts judge what, and the words of a court case.

## What you already know

You know *une loi*, *l'État* and *un citoyen* from <LessonLink id="b1-culture-french-republic" text="the lesson on the French Republic" />, and *le Parlement*, *un projet de loi* and *adopter une loi* from <LessonLink id="b2-politics-and-society" text="the lesson on politics and institutions" />. You also know *porter plainte* from <LessonLink id="b1-making-a-complaint" text="the lesson on making a complaint" />. This lesson adds the words you need to read official texts and news about the courts.

A note before you start: this lesson explains words, not anyone's rights. For a real legal question, ask a lawyer or an official advice service.

## From a bill to a text in force

<VocabTable group="texts" />

French law rests on a hierarchy of texts. At the top is *la Constitution*. Below it are *les lois*, voted by the Parliament. Below the laws are *les décrets*, adopted by the government, often to say how a law will be applied. Each level must respect the one above it.

When the Parliament has adopted a law, the President of the Republic *promulgue* it, and it's published in *le Journal officiel*. It then *entre en vigueur* (comes into force), sometimes on a date the text itself sets. A text can later be *abrogé* (repealed) by a new text.

<Example fr="La loi a été promulguée puis publiée au Journal officiel." en="The law was promulgated and then published in the official journal." />
<Example fr="Cette règle entrera en vigueur le 1er janvier." en="This rule will come into force on 1 January." />
<Example fr="Le gouvernement a abrogé l'ancien décret." en="The government repealed the old decree." />

*Une disposition* is one provision of a text, and *en vertu de* introduces the text that justifies a decision: *en vertu de cette disposition* (under this provision).

<Example fr="En vertu de cette loi, les entreprises sont tenues d'informer leurs clients." en="Under this law, companies are required to inform their customers." />

## Two orders of courts

<VocabTable group="courts" />

France has two separate orders of courts. *La justice* in the ordinary sense (disputes between people, companies, and criminal cases) starts in *un tribunal*, goes on appeal to *une cour d'appel*, and ends at *la Cour de cassation*. Disputes with public bodies (a town hall, a ministry, a public service) go to the administrative courts: *le tribunal administratif*, then the administrative court of appeal, and at the top *le Conseil d'État*.

*Le Conseil d'État* has two roles: it's the highest administrative court, and it advises the government on its draft laws and decrees. *Le Conseil constitutionnel* is not an ordinary court: it checks that laws respect the Constitution.

*Une juridiction* is the general word for any court. To take a case to a court is *saisir* it.

<Example fr="Elle travaille dans la justice depuis dix ans." en="She has worked in the courts for ten years." />
<Example fr="L'association a saisi le tribunal administratif." en="The association took the case to the administrative court." />
<Example fr="Le Conseil constitutionnel vérifie que les lois respectent la Constitution." en="The Conseil constitutionnel checks that laws respect the Constitution." />

## The words of a case

<VocabTable group="case" />

*Un procès* is a trial or a court case, where *un juge* or *une juge* decides and each side is helped by *un avocat* or *une avocate*. The decision is *un jugement*. A side that disagrees can *faire appel* (appeal). Against a decision of an administration, you take legal action: in formal writing, *former un recours*; *déposer un recours* is also standard, and everyday administrative French often says *faire un recours*.

<Example fr="Elle a perdu son procès, mais elle a fait appel du jugement." en="She lost her case, but she appealed against the ruling." />
<Example fr="Plusieurs habitants ont formé un recours contre ce projet." en="Several residents have taken legal action against this project." />

## Rights and duties

<VocabTable group="rights" />

*Un droit* is a right, and *le droit* is law as a subject: *elle fait des études de droit*. *Être tenu de* + infinitive is the formal way to say that the law requires something; the participle agrees with the subject. *Juridique* means "relating to law", like a legal question or a legal text.

<Example fr="Les propriétaires sont tenus de faire ces travaux." en="Owners are required to carry out this work." />
<Example fr="C'est une question juridique difficile." en="It's a difficult legal question." />

## Reading news about the law

News reports use this vocabulary in set patterns, often in the passive. Here are a few to recognize.

<Example fr="Le Conseil d'État a été saisi." en="The case has been taken to the Conseil d'État." />
<Example fr="Le jugement a été confirmé en appel." en="The ruling was upheld on appeal." />
<Example fr="Le texte entre en vigueur aujourd'hui, mais un recours a déjà été formé." en="The text comes into force today, but legal action has already been taken against it." />

Keep two things apart when you read: the word for a court tells you which order it belongs to, and the verb tells you what happened (*saisir*, *juger*, *faire appel*, *abroger*). Many articles mix them in one sentence.

## The whole list

| Step or body | French |
|---|---|
| top of the hierarchy | *la Constitution* |
| voted by the Parliament | *une loi* |
| adopted by the government | *un décret* |
| signed into law, published | *promulguer*, *le Journal officiel* |
| come into force, repeal | *entrer en vigueur*, *abroger* |
| ordinary courts | *un tribunal*, *une cour d'appel*, *la Cour de cassation* |
| administrative courts | *le tribunal administratif*, …, *le Conseil d'État* |
| constitutional check | *le Conseil constitutionnel* |
| a case | *un procès*, *un juge*, *un avocat*, *un jugement*, *faire appel*, *former un recours* |

## En français

Sarah tells her friend Lucas, a law student, about something she read in the paper.

- *Tu as lu le journal ? Un nouveau décret entre en vigueur lundi.* (Have you read the paper? A new decree comes into force on Monday.)
- *Sur quoi ?* (About what?)
- *Sur les horaires des magasins le dimanche. Une association veut former un recours.* (About shop opening hours on Sundays. An association wants to take legal action.)
- *Contre un décret, c'est en général le Conseil d'État qui juge.* (Against a decree, it's generally the Conseil d'État that rules.)
- *Pas le tribunal administratif ?* (Not the administrative court?)
- *Non, pour un décret, on saisit le plus souvent tout de suite le Conseil d'État.* (No, for a decree, you usually go straight to the Conseil d'État. (spoken))
- *Et si l'association gagne ?* (And if the association wins?)
- *Le Conseil d'État peut annuler le décret. Mais le gouvernement peut aussi l'abroger lui-même.* (The Conseil d'État can cancel the decree. But the government can also repeal it itself.)
- *Tu veux devenir avocat ?* (Do you want to become a lawyer?)
- *Peut-être, ou juge. Mais attention, je ne donne pas de conseils juridiques !* (Maybe, or a judge. But careful, I don't give legal advice!)
- *Rassure-toi, je n'ai pas de procès en cours.* (Don't worry, I don't have a court case going on.)
- *Tant mieux. Un procès, ça peut durer longtemps.* (Just as well. A court case can last a long time. (spoken))

<Callout type="warning" title="Former un recours in formal writing">
  Learners often use *faire* with every legal noun, and sometimes drop the article. In formal writing, choose the established collocation: **former un recours** (against a decision; everyday French also says *faire un recours*), **faire appel** (against a ruling), **porter plainte** (with the police or a court).
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Name the order of the court">
  When you read about a case, decide first whether the court is ordinary (*tribunal*, *cour d'appel*, *Cour de cassation*) or administrative (*tribunal administratif*, *Conseil d'État*). It tells you who the other side is: a person or company, or a public body.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le droit français repose sur une hiérarchie de textes. La Constitution est au-dessus de tout, puis viennent les lois, votées par le Parlement, et les décrets, adoptés par le gouvernement. Le président de la République promulgue la loi, qui est publiée au Journal officiel, puis elle entre en vigueur. Un nouveau texte peut l'abroger. En France, il existe deux ordres de juridictions. Les tribunaux, les cours d'appel et la Cour de cassation jugent les conflits entre personnes ou entreprises. Le tribunal administratif et le Conseil d'État jugent les conflits avec une mairie, un ministère ou un service public. Le Conseil constitutionnel vérifie que les lois respectent la Constitution. Dans un procès, un juge décide et les avocats défendent chaque partie. Contre un jugement, on fait appel, et contre une décision publique, on forme un recours.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to follow economic news and discuss public finance, economic policy, savings and markets with precise vocabulary.

## What you already know

You know *la croissance*, *la récession*, *la conjoncture*, *le PIB*, *l'inflation* and *investir* from <LessonLink id="b2-economy-and-business" text="the lesson on the economy and business" />, and *un taux* and *pour cent* from <LessonLink id="b2-presenting-data" text="the lesson on presenting data" />. *Emprunter*, *un prêt* and *rembourser* come from <LessonLink id="b1-money-and-banking" text="the lesson on money and banking" />. This lesson moves from companies to the State and the markets.

A note on figures: the examples here describe mechanisms, not the real state of any economy. For real figures, check an official source on the day you need them.

## Public finance

<VocabTable group="public" />

*Le budget de l'État* sets what the State will spend, *les dépenses publiques*, and what it will receive, *les recettes*: mostly *les impôts*. *La fiscalité* is the tax system as a whole. When spending is higher than revenue, the budget shows *un déficit*. To cover it, the State borrows, and the total it owes is *la dette publique*. *S'endetter* is to get into debt, for a State, a company or a household.

<Example fr="Cette année, les dépenses publiques ont été plus élevées que les recettes." en="This year, public spending was higher than revenue." />
<Example fr="Pour financer son déficit, l'État s'endette." en="To fund its deficit, the State borrows." />
<Example fr="Le gouvernement veut réformer la fiscalité des petites entreprises." en="The government wants to reform taxation for small businesses." />

## Economic policy: relance or rigueur

<VocabTable group="policy" />

Economic debates often oppose two policies. *La relance* means spending more, or taxing less, to *relancer l'économie* (boost it). *La rigueur budgétaire* means controlling or cutting public spending to reduce the deficit. Each side has its arguments; the vocabulary itself is neutral, but the choice of word can show a point of view, so a careful writer names both.

*Un ménage*, in economics, is a household: the people who share a home and a budget. Economists talk about *la consommation des ménages* and *l'épargne des ménages*.

<Example fr="Le plan de relance doit aider les ménages et les entreprises." en="The stimulus plan is meant to help households and businesses." />
<Example fr="Certains économistes défendent la rigueur budgétaire, d'autres la relance." en="Some economists support budgetary restraint, others a stimulus." />

## Rates, savings and markets

<VocabTable group="markets" />

*Un taux d'intérêt* is the price of borrowing. *La banque centrale* can *relever* or *baisser ses taux*: when they go up, borrowing costs more; when they go down, it costs less. The rates themselves *montent* or *baissent*; the bank *relève* them (or *remonte* them, after a fall).

*L'épargne* is the money people put aside, and *épargner* is to save. That money can go into *un placement* (an investment), which *rapporte* (yields) more or less, with more or less risk. *La Bourse* is the stock exchange, where *les actions* you know from B2 are bought and sold. *Une obligation*, in finance, is a bond: a loan to a State or a company that pays interest. Elsewhere, it keeps its everyday meaning of a duty.

<Example fr="La banque centrale a relevé ses taux pour faire baisser l'inflation." en="The central bank raised its rates to bring inflation down." />
<Example fr="Les ménages épargnent davantage quand l'avenir est incertain." en="Households save more when the future is uncertain." />
<Example fr="Un placement sans risque rapporte en général moins." en="A risk-free investment usually yields less." />
<Example fr="Les marchés financiers ont réagi à la décision." en="The financial markets reacted to the decision." />

## Words that look familiar

Several words in this field are false friends, or have a second meaning that changes everything.

- **La Bourse**, with a capital, is the stock exchange; **une bourse** is a student grant.
- **Les recettes** of the State are its revenue; in a kitchen, *une recette* is a recipe.
- **Un placement** is an investment of savings, not a work placement, which is *un stage*.
- **Une obligation** is a bond in finance and a duty elsewhere.

<Example fr="Elle a obtenu une bourse pour ses études." en="She got a grant for her studies." />
<Example fr="Les recettes de l'État ont augmenté grâce à la croissance." en="The State's revenue rose thanks to growth." />

When a word seems odd in an economic article, check whether it has a financial meaning before you translate it.

## Reading economic news

Economic articles combine this vocabulary with the trend verbs you know from B2 (*augmenter*, *chuter*, *se stabiliser*) and with hedging. A careful reader notices who is speaking: a forecast is *une prévision*, not a fact, and journalists often use the conditionnel for it.

<Example fr="Selon le ministère, le déficit devrait baisser l'an prochain." en="According to the ministry, the deficit should fall next year." />

As with law, keep the words and the opinions apart. *La dette publique augmente* is a fact you can check; *la dette est trop élevée* is a judgement someone makes.

## En français

Lucas is reading an economic article. He asks his sister Emma, who works in a bank, to explain it.

- *Emma, ici ils disent que la banque centrale a relevé ses taux. Ça veut dire quoi ?* (Emma, here they say the central bank raised its rates. What does that mean? (spoken))
- *Que les taux d'intérêt montent : emprunter coûte plus cher.* (That interest rates are going up: borrowing costs more.)
- *Alors qui gagne et qui perd ?* (So who wins and who loses?)
- *En général, l'épargne rapporte un peu plus, et ceux qui empruntent paient plus cher.* (In general, savings bring in a bit more, and people who borrow pay more.)
- *Et l'article parle aussi du déficit public.* (And the article also talks about the public deficit.)
- *C'est quand l'État dépense plus que ses recettes.* (That's when the State spends more than its revenue.)
- *Et pour le payer, il s'endette ?* (And to pay for it, it borrows?)
- *Exactement. C'est comme ça que la dette publique augmente.* (Exactly. That's how the public debt grows.)
- *Et une obligation, c'est quoi ? Un devoir ?* (And what's an obligation? A duty? (spoken))
- *Pas ici. En finance, c'est un prêt à l'État ou à une entreprise.* (Not here. In finance, it's a loan to the State or a company.)
- *Tu en as acheté, toi ?* (Have you bought any? (spoken))
- *Je ne parle pas de mes placements, même à mon frère !* (I don't talk about my investments, even to my brother!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Une obligation: a bond in finance">
  Learners often read *une obligation* in an economic article as "an obligation". In finance, it's a bond: **acheter des obligations de l'État** means buying government bonds, not taking on duties.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Fact or judgement?">
  When you read an economic article, mark each sentence F (a fact you could check) or J (a judgement). *Le déficit a baissé* is F; *la rigueur est nécessaire* is J. It trains you to report economic debates neutrally, which the synthesis and the essay both require.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le budget de l'État fixe les dépenses publiques et les recettes, qui viennent surtout des impôts. La fiscalité est l'ensemble du système des impôts. Quand les dépenses sont plus élevées que les recettes, il y a un déficit public, et l'État s'endette. La dette publique est le total de ce que l'État doit. Un gouvernement doit choisir entre deux politiques : la relance, c'est-à-dire dépenser plus pour faire repartir l'économie, ou la rigueur budgétaire, c'est-à-dire réduire les dépenses pour réduire le déficit. La banque centrale peut relever ou baisser ses taux d'intérêt. Les ménages épargnent et choisissent des placements, qui rapportent plus ou moins. À la Bourse, on achète des actions, et une obligation est un prêt à l'État ou à une entreprise. Il faut séparer les faits, comme une hausse de la dette, et les jugements.

## What you already know

You know the polite conditionnel and *je vous serais reconnaissant de* from <LessonLink id="b1-formal-email" text="the lesson on writing a formal email" />, *nous serions prêts à* and *faire un geste* from <LessonLink id="b2-negotiating" text="the lesson on negotiating" />, and *présenter ses excuses* and *un contretemps* from <LessonLink id="b2-regret-and-reproach" text="the lesson on regret and reproach" />. You also know *ne* alone with *pouvoir* and *savoir* (*je ne saurais dire*, *de ne pouvoir*) from <LessonLink id="c1-literary-negation" text="the lesson on literary negation" />. Diplomatic French combines all of these.

## What makes a sentence diplomatic

Diplomatic language doesn't hide the message: the refusal or the criticism is still clear. What changes is who carries it. A blunt sentence puts the speaker against the other person (*je refuse*, *vous vous êtes trompé*). A diplomatic sentence moves the weight elsewhere: onto the situation, onto a rule, or onto "us".

Four tools do most of the work:

- **Impersonal structures**: *il ne nous sera pas possible de* instead of *je refuse*.
- **The conditionnel and the future**: *il serait préférable de*, *nous ne manquerons pas de*.
- **Softening adverbs and phrases**: *malheureusement*, *à notre grand regret*, *sauf erreur de ma part*.
- **Understatement**: *ce n'est pas tout à fait ce que nous attendions* for "we're disappointed".

## Saying no

<VocabTable group="refuse" />

*Il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de* + infinitive is the standard written refusal: it's impersonal, it uses the future, and *malheureusement* shows regret. *Ne pas être en mesure de* says that circumstances, not will, prevent you. *À notre grand regret* opens a refusal warmly, and *en l'état* limits it to the present situation, which leaves the door open.

<Example fr="Il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de vous accorder ce délai." en="Unfortunately, we will not be able to grant you this extension." />
<Example fr="En l'état, nous ne sommes pas en mesure de modifier le contrat." en="As things stand, we are not in a position to change the contract." />
<Example fr="À notre grand regret, nous ne pouvons pas accepter votre offre." en="To our great regret, we cannot accept your offer." />

## Pointing out an error or disagreeing

<VocabTable group="soften" />

To correct someone, make the thing the subject, not the person, and leave room for being wrong. *Sauf erreur de ma part* does that in three words. *Un malentendu* (a misunderstanding) blames nobody. *Il serait préférable de* turns a criticism into a suggestion. And *la déception*, named as a feeling about the result, is easier to hear than a reproach.

<Example fr="Sauf erreur de ma part, la facture comporte une erreur." en="Unless I'm mistaken, the invoice contains an error." />
<Example fr="Il y a peut-être un malentendu sur le jour de livraison." en="There may be a misunderstanding about the delivery day." />
<Example fr="Il serait préférable de nous prévenir plus tôt." en="It would be better to let us know earlier." />

**Understatement** says less than you mean. *Ce n'est pas tout à fait ce que nous attendions* (it's not quite what we expected) is a polite way to say you're disappointed. French readers decode it at once, so you don't need anything stronger.

## Promising and keeping the door open

<VocabTable group="promise" />

A diplomatic refusal usually ends with something positive. *Nous ne manquerons pas de* + infinitive is a formal promise: "we will be sure to". *Prendre bonne note de* acknowledges a request without accepting it. *Dans la mesure du possible* limits a promise honestly. In business, *un geste commercial* (a discount, a credit) can make up for a refusal, especially for a *fidèle* customer.

<Example fr="Nous avons pris bonne note de votre demande et ne manquerons pas de vous tenir informée." en="We have duly noted your request and will be sure to keep you informed." />

## The five steps of a diplomatic refusal

As a rule of thumb, a written refusal follows five steps.

1. **Thank** the person for the request or the message.
2. **Acknowledge** their point: *nous comprenons tout à fait votre déception*.
3. **Refuse**, impersonally, and give a short reason, in the same sentence or a little later: *il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de…*.
4. **Offer** something else, if you can: a geste commercial, another date, a partial solution.
5. **Close** positively: *nous ne manquerons pas de…*, *dans la mesure du possible*.

## An exchange of emails

The two emails below are original texts written for this lesson. The company and the people are invented.

*Objet : Commande de mars*

*Madame,*

*Nous sommes clients de votre société depuis dix ans. Notre dernière commande est arrivée avec deux semaines de retard, ce qui nous a causé de vraies difficultés. Ce n'est pas la première fois : en janvier, une livraison était déjà arrivée en retard. Nous vous demandons donc une remise exceptionnelle de vingt pour cent sur la facture, ainsi que la livraison gratuite pour nos prochaines commandes. Nous comptons sur votre compréhension.*

*Cordialement,*

*Paul Martin*

*Objet : RE : Commande de mars*

*Monsieur,*

*Je vous remercie de votre message et je comprends tout à fait votre déception : un retard de deux semaines n'est pas ce que vous pouvez attendre d'un partenariat de dix ans.*

*À notre grand regret, il ne nous sera pas possible de vous accorder une remise de vingt pour cent, ni la livraison gratuite pour toutes vos commandes : nos conditions commerciales ne permettent pas de remise supérieure à dix pour cent. En revanche, nous vous proposons un geste commercial de dix pour cent sur cette facture, ainsi que la livraison gratuite pour votre prochaine commande.*

*Ce retard venait d'un problème chez notre transporteur, que nous avons depuis changé. Sauf erreur de ma part, la livraison de janvier était arrivée avec un jour de retard ; nous vous présentons nos excuses pour ces deux retards. Nous ne manquerons pas de vous prévenir, dans la mesure du possible, si un nouveau retard se présente. Je reste à votre disposition pour en parler par téléphone.*

*Je vous prie d'agréer, Monsieur, mes salutations distinguées.*

*Claire Dubois, service clients*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Subject: March order. Dear Madam, We have been customers of your company for ten years. Our last order arrived two weeks late, which caused us real difficulties. It isn't the first time: in January, a delivery had already arrived late. We therefore ask you for a special discount of twenty per cent on the invoice, as well as free delivery for our next orders. We count on your understanding. Kind regards, Paul Martin. Subject: RE: March order. Dear Sir, Thank you for your message; I fully understand your disappointment: a two-week delay is not what you can expect from a ten-year partnership. To our great regret, we will not be able to grant you a twenty per cent discount, nor free delivery for all your orders: our commercial terms do not allow discounts above ten per cent. On the other hand, we offer you a goodwill gesture of ten per cent on this invoice, as well as free delivery for your next order. This delay came from a problem with our carrier, which we have since changed. Unless I'm mistaken, the January delivery arrived one day late; we apologize for both delays. We will be sure to let you know, as far as possible, if there is another delay. I remain at your disposal to talk about it by phone. Yours faithfully, Claire Dubois, customer service.)

## How the reply works

- **Thanks and acknowledgement**: *je vous remercie*, *je comprends tout à fait votre déception*.
- **The refusal**: *à notre grand regret, il ne nous sera pas possible de*, with *ni* for the second item, and a short reason: *nos conditions commerciales ne permettent pas de remise supérieure à dix pour cent*.
- **The offer**: *en revanche, nous vous proposons un geste commercial*.
- **The explanation** of the March delay, stated plainly: *ce retard venait d'un problème chez notre transporteur*.
- **An apology** for both delays, with the January delay softened by *sauf erreur de ma part*: *nous vous présentons nos excuses pour ces deux retards*.
- **The positive close**: *nous ne manquerons pas de*, *dans la mesure du possible*, *je reste à votre disposition*.

Notice that the reply repeats both requests before refusing them, so the client knows he was read carefully.

## En français

Madame Dubois calls Monsieur Martin after sending her reply.

- *Monsieur Martin, vous avez reçu mon message ?* (Mr Martin, did you get my message?)
- *Oui. Ce n'est pas tout à fait ce que nous attendions.* (Yes. It's not quite what we expected.)
- *Je comprends tout à fait. Vingt pour cent, en l'état, ce n'est pas possible.* (I fully understand. Twenty per cent, as things stand, isn't possible.)
- *Et pour la livraison gratuite ?* (And free delivery?)
- *Pour la prochaine commande, oui. Pour toutes, nous ne sommes pas en mesure de le faire.* (For the next order, yes. For all of them, we're not in a position to do it.)
- *Il y a peut-être un malentendu : nous pensions à six mois seulement.* (There may be a misunderstanding: we were only thinking of six months.)
- *Ah, c'est différent. Je ne peux rien vous promettre, mais j'en parle à ma directrice.* (Ah, that's different. I can't promise anything, but I'll talk to my manager about it.)
- *Merci. Il serait préférable de nous répondre avant la fin du mois.* (Thank you. It would be better to answer us before the end of the month.)
- *Nous ne manquerons pas de le faire, dans la mesure du possible.* (We'll be sure to do so, as far as possible.)
- *Très bien. Nous restons des clients fidèles, vous savez.* (Very good. We're still loyal customers, you know.)
- *Et nous y tenons beaucoup. Je vous rappelle très vite.* (And that matters a lot to us. I'll call you back very soon.)
- *Merci, Madame. Bonne journée.* (Thank you. Have a good day.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Nous ne manquerons pas de, not nous ne manquerons pas à">
  Learners often mix up the two constructions of *manquer*. The formal promise takes **de**: **nous ne manquerons pas de vous tenir informé**. With *à*, *manquer* means "to be missed by": *vous nous manquerez*.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Read your refusal as the other person">
  Before you send a refusal, read it once as if you were the person receiving it. Underline every sentence where "you" did something wrong, and rewrite it with the situation or the document as the subject. The message stays the same; the relationship survives.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to write a formal letter to an institution, from the layout to the closing formula, and to read the replies you get.

## What you already know

You know *l'objet*, *veuillez trouver ci-joint* and *je vous serais reconnaissant de* from <LessonLink id="b1-formal-email" text="the lesson on writing a formal email" />, and *Madame, Monsieur*, *je me permets de* and *je vous prie d'agréer… salutations distinguées* from <LessonLink id="b1-cover-letter" text="the lesson on the CV and cover letter" />. You know how to refuse diplomatically from <LessonLink id="c1-diplomatic-language" text="the lesson on diplomatic language" />. This lesson moves up one register: letters to a university, a town hall, a ministry or a court.

## The layout

<VocabTable group="layout" />

A formal French letter follows a fixed layout. As a rule of thumb:

- **Top left**: *l'expéditeur*, with name and address.
- **Below, on the right**: *le destinataire*, sometimes with *à l'attention de* + a person or a department.
- **Then**: the place and date (*Lyon, le 12 mars*), the references (*N/Réf.*, *V/Réf.*) if there are any, and *l'objet*.
- **The salutation**: *Madame, Monsieur,* if you don't know the person; *Madame la Directrice,* or *Monsieur le Maire,* if they have a title. Never add the name after the title.

<Example fr="L'adresse de l'expéditeur se trouve en haut, et celle du destinataire juste en dessous, à droite." en="The sender's address is at the top, and the recipient's just below it, on the right." />
<Example fr="Merci de rappeler nos références dans votre réponse." en="Please quote our reference in your reply." />

*Un courrier* is the formal word for a letter or for mail in general: *votre courrier du 5 mars*.

<Example fr="En réponse à votre courrier du 5 mars, je vous adresse les documents demandés." en="In reply to your letter of 5 March, I am sending you the documents requested." />

## Opening the letter

<VocabTable group="opening" />

The first sentence says why you write. *En réponse à* refers to a previous letter. *Par la présente* (by this letter) opens a formal statement. *J'ai l'honneur de* + infinitive is typical of administrative French, especially to a public authority: it sounds old-fashioned in business, but it's still usual in letters to institutions. *Solliciter* means to request formally.

<Example fr="Par la présente, je vous informe de ma démission." en="I hereby inform you of my resignation." />
<Example fr="J'ai l'honneur de solliciter un délai supplémentaire." en="I have the honour to request an extension." />

*Suite à* is very common in emails and letters, but some careful writers prefer *à la suite de*, which you know from <LessonLink id="c1-formal-relatives-and-apposition" text="the lesson on formal relatives" />, or *en réponse à*.

## Making a request

<VocabTable group="request" />

At C1, two formulas replace *pouvez-vous*. *Je vous saurais gré de* (I would be grateful if you would) uses the conditionnel of *savoir*, and it's usually followed by *bien vouloir* + infinitive. *Je vous prie de bien vouloir* is a polite instruction. *Veuillez* + infinitive, which you know, is firmer. When you receive a document, you *accusez réception de* it.

<Example fr="Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir m'envoyer votre réponse avant la fin du mois." en="I would be grateful if you would send me your reply before the end of the month." />
<Example fr="Nous accusons réception de votre dossier et vous en remercions." en="We acknowledge receipt of your file and thank you for it." />

## Closing the letter

<VocabTable group="closing" />

A formal French letter ends with a full sentence, not a word. The formula repeats the salutation between commas, then names the "expression" you send. As a rule of thumb, the choice depends on the person:

- **To someone you don't know, or an equal**: *l'expression de mes salutations distinguées*.
- **To a superior, or with more respect**: *l'expression de ma considération distinguée*.
- **To a high authority** (a minister, a prefect): *l'assurance de ma haute considération*.

<Example fr="Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame la Présidente, l'expression de ma considération distinguée." en="Yours faithfully (to the President, respectful)." />

*Cordialement* and *bien à vous* end emails between colleagues; in a letter to an institution, they're too short.

Before the formula, the last paragraph often lists the attachments, with *ci-joint* or *vous trouverez en pièce jointe*, and can mention *un certificat médical* or any other supporting document.

## Letters and emails

Many institutions now accept requests by email. The register doesn't change: the subject line works as *l'objet*, the salutation keeps the title, and the closing is still a full sentence, though some writers shorten it to *Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame, mes salutations distinguées*. Attach documents in PDF and name them in the message. What you lose is the layout at the top: give your full name, and any reference number, in the first lines instead.

<Example fr="Vous trouverez ci-joint le certificat médical mentionné dans ma demande." en="Please find attached the medical certificate mentioned in my request." />

Whatever the medium, reread for consistency: the same title in the salutation and the closing; *je* for the person who signs and *nous* for the institution, each used in the same way throughout; and one request per letter.

## How the letters work

- **The request** follows the layout of the lesson (sender, recipient with her title, place and date, objet), opens with *j'ai l'honneur de solliciter*, gives the reason and a supporting document (*le certificat médical ci-joint*), then asks with *je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir*.
- **The salutation** uses the title, *Madame la Présidente*, and the closing repeats it.
- **The reply** opens with *en réponse à votre courrier* and *j'accuse réception de*, grants the request partly (*un délai vous est accordé*, *au plus tard le 20 juin*) and ends with a request of its own.
- **The closings differ**: the student writes to a superior (*ma considération distinguée*); the president replies with *mes salutations distinguées*.

## En français

Inès shows her draft to her father, who worked for years in a town hall.

- *Papa, tu peux relire ma lettre à la présidente de l'université ?* (Dad, can you read over my letter to the university president?)
- *Bien sûr. Tu commences par « Chère Madame » ? Non, écris « Madame la Présidente ».* (Of course. You start with "Chère Madame"? No, write "Madame la Présidente".)
- *D'accord. Et pour demander le délai ?* (OK. And to ask for the extension?)
- *« J'ai l'honneur de solliciter un délai supplémentaire. » C'est l'usage.* ("J'ai l'honneur de solliciter un délai supplémentaire." That's the custom.)
- *Ça fait un peu ancien, non ?* (It sounds a bit old-fashioned, doesn't it? (spoken))
- *Un peu, mais à l'université et à la mairie, on l'écrit encore.* (A little, but at the university and the town hall, people still write it. (spoken))
- *Et j'ai mis « Pouvez-vous m'accorder un mois ? ».* (And I put "Can you give me a month?".)
- *Plutôt : « Je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir m'accorder un délai d'un mois ».* (Rather: "I would be grateful if you would grant me a one-month extension".)
- *Et à la fin, « Cordialement » ?* (And at the end, "Cordialement"?)
- *Trop court. Écris la formule complète, avec « Madame la Présidente » entre deux virgules.* (Too short. Write the full formula, with "Madame la Présidente" between two commas.)
- *Je mets le certificat médical en pièce jointe ?* (Shall I attach the medical certificate?)
- *Oui, et dis-le dans la lettre : « comme l'indique le certificat ci-joint ».* (Yes, and say so in the letter: "as the attached certificate shows".)

<Callout type="warning" title="Madame la Directrice, not Madame la Directrice Dubois">
  Learners often add the name after the title, as in English. In French, the title replaces the name: **Madame la Directrice,** **Monsieur le Maire,** and the closing repeats it the same way.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a letter skeleton">
  Write one model letter with every fixed part in place (layout, salutation with title, *j'ai l'honneur de*, *je vous saurais gré de*, the full closing) and keep it as a file. For each new letter, change only the content between the formulas.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to chair a meeting in French: open it, give the floor, keep to time, calm a disagreement, put a proposal to the vote and record the decisions.

## What you already know

You know *l'ordre du jour*, *prendre la parole*, *passer la parole à*, *trancher*, *à l'unanimité* and *un compte rendu* from <LessonLink id="b2-meetings-and-calls" text="the lesson on meetings and professional calls" />, and *couper la parole à* and *revenons-en à* from <LessonLink id="b2-debating" text="the lesson on taking part in a debate" />. *La séance*, *lever la séance* and *le procès-verbal* come from <LessonLink id="c1-participle-clauses" text="the lesson on participle clauses" />. At B2 you took part in meetings; here you lead them.

## The role of the chair

*Le président* or *la présidente de séance* has three jobs: keep the meeting to its agenda and its time, make sure everyone can speak, and turn the discussion into decisions. Each job has its own formulas, and they're more formal than everyday conversation, even among colleagues who know each other well.

## Opening

<VocabTable group="open" />

A formal meeting opens with a fixed sentence, then the purpose and the agenda.

<Example fr="Il est dix-huit heures : je déclare la séance ouverte." en="It's six o'clock: I declare the meeting open." />
<Example fr="Nous sommes réunis pour décider de l'aménagement du parking." en="We're here to decide how to fit out the car park." />

In a less formal team meeting, *bon, on commence ?* does the same job in speech. Choose by the setting: a board or an association's general meeting needs the formula; a weekly team meeting doesn't.

## Giving the floor and managing time

<VocabTable group="floor" />

The chair *donne la parole à* each speaker; speakers *prennent la parole*. *Un tour de table* gives everyone a turn, often at the start. To keep to time, interrupt politely and postpone: *je me permets de vous interrompre* and *nous y reviendrons* are the two most useful sentences of a chair.

<Example fr="Je donne la parole à Madame Martin." en="I give the floor to Madame Martin." />
<Example fr="Je me permets de vous interrompre : nous y reviendrons après le vote." en="May I interrupt you: we'll come back to that after the vote." />

## Managing disagreement

<VocabTable group="conflict" />

When a discussion gets heated, the chair *apaise* (calms) and *recadre le débat* (brings it back to the point). The diplomatic tools from the last lessons work here too: acknowledge the point, name a possible misunderstanding, and return to the agenda.

<Example fr="Je comprends tout à fait votre position. Il y a peut-être un malentendu." en="I fully understand your position. There may be a misunderstanding." />
<Example fr="Revenons-en à l'ordre du jour : nous devons décider aujourd'hui." en="Let's get back to the agenda: we have to decide today." />

<Example fr="La présidente a su apaiser les échanges." en="The chair managed to calm the discussion." />

*Les échanges* is the neutral word for the discussion itself: *les échanges ont été vifs* (the discussion was heated).

## Deciding

<VocabTable group="decide" />

When the discussion is over, the chair *met la proposition aux voix* (puts it to the vote). A vote *à main levée* (by a show of hands) is common in small meetings. Some participants may *s'abstenir*. The result is announced, and the chair *acte* the decision: records it formally.

<Example fr="Je mets la proposition aux voix. Qui est pour ?" en="I'm putting the proposal to the vote. Who is in favour?" />
<Example fr="La proposition est adoptée ; deux participants se sont abstenus." en="The proposal is adopted; two participants abstained." />
<Example fr="Nous actons donc la création d'un parking à vélos." en="We therefore record the decision to create a bike park." />

If the vote isn't needed, the chair can also *trancher*, as you saw at B2, or note that everyone agrees: *la décision est prise à l'unanimité*.

## Closing and the written record

<VocabTable group="close" />

Before closing, the chair *fait la synthèse des échanges* in two or three sentences, then reads the decisions. Afterwards, someone writes it up. *Un procès-verbal* is the full, official record; *un compte rendu* reports the discussion; *un relevé de décisions* lists mainly what was decided, who will do it and by when, with very little about the discussion.

<Example fr="Pour faire la synthèse des échanges : nous sommes d'accord sur le principe, mais pas sur le calendrier." en="To sum up the discussion: we agree on the principle, but not on the schedule." />
<Example fr="L'ordre du jour étant épuisé, je lève la séance." en="As the agenda has been covered, I close the meeting." />

## Chairing online

Many meetings now take place by *visioconférence*, which you know from B2. The chair's job is the same, but the signals change. Ask participants to *couper leur micro* when they're not speaking, and to raise their hand in the tool rather than interrupt. Give the floor by name, because people can't see who you're looking at. Before a vote, say clearly how it will work.

<Example fr="Pour voter, je vous propose de lever la main dans l'outil, et je compterai les voix." en="To vote, I suggest you raise your hand in the tool, and I'll count the votes." />

Online, silence is harder to read than in a room. As a rule of thumb, after each important point, ask a direct question (*avez-vous des remarques ?*) and wait a few seconds before moving on.

## En français

The meeting of 12 March, during point 2.

- *Je donne la parole à Monsieur Petit.* (I give the floor to Monsieur Petit.)
- *Merci. Pour la fête, je propose le 14 juin : il y a moins de monde en vacances.* (Thank you. For the party, I suggest 14 June: fewer people are on holiday.)
- *Le 14 ? C'est le jour de la fête de l'école ! Les familles ne viendront pas.* (The 14th? That's the day of the school party! Families won't come.)
- *Ce n'est pas vrai, l'an dernier…* (That's not true, last year…)
- *Je me permets de vous interrompre. Je comprends tout à fait vos deux positions.* (May I interrupt you. I fully understand both your positions.)
- *Mais la fête de l'école, c'est important pour les familles.* (But the school party matters to families.)
- *Il y a peut-être un malentendu. Monsieur Petit, vous tenez au 14, ou seulement à un jour avant les vacances ?* (There may be a misunderstanding. Monsieur Petit, do you insist on the 14th, or simply on a day before the holidays?)
- *Avant les vacances, surtout.* (Before the holidays, mainly.)
- *Revenons-en à l'ordre du jour : nous devons choisir un jour aujourd'hui. Le 7 juin est avant les vacances, et l'école n'a pas de fête ce jour-là. Je propose le 7 juin.* (Let's get back to the agenda: we have to choose a day today. 7 June is before the holidays, and the school has no party that day. I suggest 7 June.)
- *Le 7, ça me va.* (The 7th suits me. (spoken))
- *Moi aussi.* (Me too.)
- *Je mets la proposition aux voix, à main levée. Qui est pour le 7 juin ?* (I'm putting the proposal to a show-of-hands vote. Who is in favour of 7 June?)
- *Nous sommes tous pour.* (We're all in favour.)
- *La décision est prise à l'unanimité. Nous l'actons.* (The decision is taken unanimously. We record it.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Je donne la parole à…, not je prends la parole à…">
  Learners often use *prendre la parole* for the chair. The chair **donne** or **passe la parole à** someone; a speaker **prend la parole**. *Je prends la parole à Madame Martin* means nothing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Prepare three sentences">
  Before you chair a meeting, write three sentences on a card: one to open (*je déclare la séance ouverte*), one to interrupt (*je me permets de vous interrompre : nous y reviendrons*), and one to decide (*je mets la proposition aux voix*). Under pressure, these are the ones you'll need.
</Callout>

## A few new words

<VocabTable group="new" />

These four words are frequent in professional letters. *Donner suite à* is to act on a request or an offer; it's often used in the negative, in a refusal. *Un cahier des charges* sets out the requirements a project or a supplier must meet. *Un ordre de mission* is the document that authorizes a work trip. *Le montant* is an amount of money.

<Example fr="Mon ordre de mission couvre le voyage et l'hôtel." en="My travel authorization covers the journey and the hotel." />
<Example fr="Le montant de la subvention sera payé en juin." en="The amount of the grant will be paid in June." />

## Announcing a decision: the plan

A letter that announces a decision, especially a mixed one, follows a clear order. As a rule of thumb:

1. **The fixed parts**: references, *à l'attention de*, place and date, *l'objet*, the salutation with a title.
2. **The context**: *en réponse à votre courrier du…*, and who decided, when (*lors de sa séance du…*).
3. **The positive decision first**, with its conditions: *le conseil a décidé de donner suite à…*, *compte tenu de…*.
4. **The refusal**, diplomatic and justified: *en revanche, il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de…*, with the reason, and the rule if there is one (*en vertu de…*).
5. **The request**: what the other side must do, and by when (*je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir… avant le…*).
6. **An alternative or a promise**: *sauf erreur de ma part…*, *nous ne manquerons pas de…*.
7. **The full closing formula**, repeating the title.

Putting the positive decision first makes the refusal easier to accept, and putting the request after both decisions shows what happens next.

## A model letter

This letter is an original text written for this lesson. The town, the association and the people are invented.

*Mairie de Saint-Aubin, service des associations*

*N/Réf. : SA-114*

*À l'attention de Madame Clara Moreau, présidente de l'association « Théâtre en ville »*

*Saint-Aubin, le 18 avril*

*Objet : votre demande de subvention et de salle*

*Madame la Présidente,*

*En réponse à votre courrier du 2 mars, j'ai l'honneur de vous informer que le conseil municipal a étudié votre demande lors de sa séance du 15 avril.*

*Le conseil a décidé de donner suite à votre demande de subvention pour le festival d'été. Compte tenu de la rigueur budgétaire qu'impose cette année la baisse des recettes de la ville, le montant de cette subvention sera cependant limité à la moitié de la somme demandée.*

*En revanche, il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de mettre la salle des fêtes à votre disposition en juillet : des travaux, prévus depuis l'an dernier, y auront lieu pendant tout l'été. Nous mesurons tout à fait la déception que cette décision peut causer.*

*En vertu du règlement municipal, toute manifestation publique doit respecter un cahier des charges. Afin que la subvention puisse être payée, je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir nous adresser, avant le 31 mai, le budget complet du festival et le cahier des charges signé.*

*Sauf erreur de ma part, votre association a déjà joué dans la cour de l'école l'an dernier. Ce lieu pourrait, sous réserve de l'accord de la directrice, accueillir certaines représentations. Nous ne manquerons pas de vous tenir informée de sa réponse.*

*Je vous prie d'agréer, Madame la Présidente, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*

*Pour le maire, l'adjointe chargée des associations*

(Saint-Aubin town hall, associations department. Our ref.: SA-114. For the attention of Madame Clara Moreau, president of the association "Théâtre en ville". Saint-Aubin, 18 April. Subject: your request for a grant and a hall. Madam President, In reply to your letter of 2 March, I have the honour to inform you that the town council examined your request at its meeting of 15 April. The council decided to act on your request for a grant for the summer festival. In view of the budgetary restraint imposed this year by the fall in the town's revenue, the amount of this grant will, however, be limited to half of the sum requested. ## How the letter uses the unit

- **Formal correspondence**: the references (*N/Réf.*), *à l'attention de*, *l'objet*, *Madame la Présidente* in the salutation and the closing, *en réponse à votre courrier*, *j'ai l'honneur de vous informer*, *je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir*.
- **Meetings**: the decision was taken *lors de sa séance du 15 avril*.
- **Finance**: *la rigueur budgétaire*, *la baisse des recettes*, *le montant*, *le budget complet*.
- **Law and rules**: *en vertu du règlement municipal*, *sous réserve de l'accord de la directrice*.
- **Diplomacy**: the positive decision comes first; the refusal uses *en revanche*, *il ne nous sera malheureusement pas possible de*, a reason and *nous mesurons tout à fait la déception*; the close uses *sauf erreur de ma part* and *nous ne manquerons pas de*.
- **New words**: *donner suite à*, *le montant*, *un cahier des charges*.

## Your turn

You work for a company. A colleague, Monsieur Lambert, has asked to travel to a trade fair in Lyon next month, with a hotel for three nights. Your manager agrees to the trip, but only for two nights, because of the travel budget. Write the letter (or formal email) that tells him, in eight to ten sentences.

Follow the seven steps of the plan. Use *donner suite à* for the positive decision, *un ordre de mission* for the document he must sign, a diplomatic refusal for the third night, and a request with *je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir* and a date. Close with the full formula.

## Check your work

- Does the letter put the positive decision before the refusal?
- Is the refusal impersonal (*il ne nous sera pas possible de*) and justified in one short sentence?
- Is every request dated and polite (*je vous saurais gré de bien vouloir… avant le…*)?
- Does the closing formula repeat the salutation exactly?
- Did you avoid *cordialement* and the name after a title?

<Callout type="warning" title="Donner suite à votre demande, not donner suite de votre demande">
  Learners often use *de* after *suite*, as in *à la suite de*. The verb takes **à**: **nous ne pourrons pas donner suite à votre demande**, **le conseil a décidé de donner suite à votre offre**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Good news first, then the no">
  When a letter must say yes to one thing and no to another, write the yes in the first paragraph after the context, and the no in the next one, opening with *en revanche*. Readers accept a refusal better when they've just read that part of their request was granted.
</Callout>
