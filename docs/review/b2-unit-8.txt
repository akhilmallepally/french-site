By the end of this lesson you'll be able to recognize familiar, standard and formal French, and choose the right register for the person you're talking to.

## What you already know

You've chosen between *tu* and *vous* since <LessonLink id="a1-subject-pronouns" text="the lesson on subject pronouns" />, and you saw the workplace rules in <LessonLink id="b1-culture-work-life" text="the lesson on work life" />. You know questions with *est-ce que* and with inversion (*parlez-vous… ?*) from <LessonLink id="a1-yes-no-questions" text="the lesson on yes/no questions" />, and the formal written style from <LessonLink id="b1-formal-email" text="the lesson on the formal email" />. Since A1, you've also seen that people often drop *ne* when they speak.

## Three levels

<VocabTable group="levels" />

French distinguishes three *niveaux de langue*.

- *Le registre familier* is for friends and family: relaxed, often spoken, with its own words.
- *Le registre courant* is standard French, correct everywhere: at work, in shops, with people you don't know well.
- *Le registre soutenu* is careful, formal French: official letters, speeches, literature.

The right register depends on *l'interlocuteur*, the person you're speaking to, and on the situation. Good speakers *adaptent leur discours*: the same person speaks differently to a friend, to a colleague and to a judge. When you're unsure, choose *le registre courant*: it's rarely out of place.

<Example fr="Avec mes amis, je parle au registre familier ; au travail, j'adapte mon discours." en="With my friends, I speak in the familiar register; at work, I adapt the way I speak." />
<Example fr="Le niveau de langue dépend toujours de l'interlocuteur." en="The level of language always depends on the person you're talking to." />

## Words in threes

<VocabTable group="words" />

Many everyday words have a familiar, a standard and a formal version.

| Familiar | Standard | Formal |
|---|---|---|
| *une bagnole* | *une voiture* | *une automobile* |
| *un bouquin* | *un livre* | *un ouvrage* |
| *le boulot* | *le travail* | *un emploi* |
| *bosser* | *travailler* | (no special word) |
| *bouffer* | *manger* | (no special word) |
| (no special word) | *se dépêcher* | *se hâter* |
| (no special word) | *habiter* | *demeurer* |

Not every word has three levels, so the table has gaps. *Un emploi* is also a standard word for a job, as in *une offre d'emploi*.

<Example fr="Il a acheté une nouvelle bagnole avec l'argent de son boulot." en="He bought a new car with the money from his job. (familiar)" />
<Example fr="Cet ouvrage a été écrit par une autrice célèbre." en="This book was written by a famous author. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Je bosse tard ce soir, on bouffera après." en="I'm working late tonight, we'll eat afterwards. (familiar, spoken)" />
<Example fr="Le directeur demeure à Paris." en="The director lives in Paris. (formal)" />

## Questions

The way you ask a question also shows your register.

- In familiar French, you keep statement order and raise your voice: *tu viens ?*, *tu fais quoi ?*
- In standard French, you use *est-ce que*: *est-ce que tu viens ?*
- In formal French, you put the pronoun after the verb: *venez-vous ?*

With a noun subject, formal French keeps the noun first and adds a pronoun after the verb: this is called complex inversion. Add *-t-* between two vowels.

<Example fr="Est-ce que Paul vient ce soir ? Paul vient-il ce soir ?" en="Is Paul coming tonight? (standard, then formal)" />
<Example fr="Votre fille arrive-t-elle demain ?" en="Is your daughter arriving tomorrow? (formal)" />

## Negation, on and ça

<VocabTable group="formal" />

Three small features change with the register.

- To negate, familiar French drops *ne* (*je sais pas*), standard French keeps it (*je ne sais pas*), and formal French can use *ne… guère* (hardly): *il ne parle guère*.
- For "we", familiar and standard speech use *on* (*on y va*); careful writing prefers *nous*.
- For "that", speech uses *ça*; formal writing prefers *cela*.

With *pouvoir* and *savoir*, formal French can use *ne* alone, without *pas*: *je ne saurais dire* (I couldn't say), *je ne peux vous répondre*.

<Example fr="Je ne saurais dire si le train partira à l'heure." en="I couldn't say whether the train will leave on time. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Il ne mange guère depuis sa maladie." en="He has hardly eaten since his illness. (formal)" />

## Inversion after an adverb

In formal writing, a few adverbs at the start of a sentence are followed by inversion. After *peut-être* and *sans doute*, the inversion is the formal choice; in speech, people say *peut-être que* with normal order. After *aussi*, the inversion changes the meaning: at the start of a sentence, *aussi* means "therefore".

<Example fr="Peut-être est-il malade. Peut-être qu'il est malade." en="Perhaps he's ill. (formal, then spoken)" />
<Example fr="Sans doute a-t-elle raison." en="She's probably right. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Le magasin était fermé. Aussi avons-nous fait les courses ailleurs." en="The shop was closed. So we did the shopping elsewhere. (formal)" />
<Example fr="Nous avons aussi fait les courses." en="We also did the shopping. (standard)" />

## Mixing registers

A common mistake is mixing registers inside one text. A formal letter with *j'ai pas*, or a text message with *aussi ai-je*, sounds strange. Choose one register at the start, from the person and the situation, and keep it to the end.

## En français

Malik needs a car for a day. He asks three people.

- *Salut ! Tu peux me prêter ta bagnole ce week-end ?* (Hi! Can you lend me your car this weekend? familiar)
- *Oui, pas de problème. Tu me la rends dimanche ?* (Yes, no problem. Will you give it back on Sunday?)
- *Promis. Je sais pas comment te remercier !* (Promise. I don't know how to thank you! familiar, dropped ne)
- *C'est rien, c'est normal.* (It's nothing, it's normal. familiar)
- *Excusez-moi, est-ce que vous pourriez me prêter votre voiture demain ?* (Excuse me, could you lend me your car tomorrow? standard)
- *Oui, bien sûr. Vous en avez besoin pour quoi ?* (Yes, of course. What do you need it for?)
- *Je dois voir un client, et ma voiture est chez le garagiste.* (I have to see a client, and my car is at the garage.)
- *Pas de problème, je vous donne une clé.* (No problem, I'll give you a key.)
- *Madame, excusez-moi de vous déranger. Accepteriez-vous de me prêter votre automobile une heure ?* (Madam, excuse me for disturbing you. Would you agree to lend me your car for an hour? formal)
- *Voilà une demande bien curieuse ! Pourquoi en avez-vous besoin ?* (That's a rather strange request! Why do you need it?)
- *Ma mère est malade, et je dois me hâter : je ne saurais dire quand le taxi arrivera.* (My mother is ill, and I must hurry: I couldn't say when the taxi will arrive.)
- *Dans ce cas, prenez-la, et hâtez-vous !* (In that case, take it, and hurry!)

## One story, two registers

The same story told twice: first in a text message to a friend, then in a formal letter. Both texts are original.

*Salut Léa ! Désolé, j'ai perdu le bouquin que tu m'as prêté. Je l'avais dans le train, et après, plus rien. J'ai cherché partout, mais je l'ai pas retrouvé. Je sais vraiment pas comment c'est arrivé, et je sais que tu y tenais beaucoup. Je suis vraiment nul ! Je t'en achète un autre, c'est promis. On se voit samedi ? Bon, j'y vais, je suis en retard pour le boulot !*

*Madame,*

*Je me permets de vous écrire concernant l'ouvrage que vous m'avez prêté. Je suis désolé de vous annoncer que je l'ai perdu dans le train. J'ai cherché partout, sans succès, et je ne saurais dire comment cela est arrivé. Je sais que vous teniez beaucoup à cet ouvrage. Aussi ai-je décidé de vous en acheter un nouveau, que je vous apporterai samedi. Je regrette sincèrement cette erreur, et je vous présente toutes mes excuses.*

*Veuillez agréer, Madame, l'expression de mes salutations distinguées.*


(Hi Léa! Sorry, I've lost the book you lent me. I had it on the train, and then, nothing. I looked everywhere, but I didn't find it. I really don't know how it happened, and I know you really cared about it. I'm so useless! I'll buy you another one, I promise. See you on Saturday? Right, I'm off, I'm late for work! / Madam, I am writing to you concerning the book you lent me. I am sorry to tell you that I lost it on the train. I searched everywhere, without success, and I cannot say how it happened. I know that you were very attached to this book. I have therefore decided to buy you a new one, which I shall bring you on Saturday. I sincerely regret this mistake, and I offer you my apologies. Yours faithfully.)

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't mix registers in one text">
  Learners often write a formal letter and drop *ne* out of habit: *je vous remercie, mais j'ai pas reçu le colis* sounds careless. In a letter, keep the whole text standard or formal: **je n'ai pas reçu le colis**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask who is listening">
  Before you speak or write, ask yourself one question: who is my *interlocuteur*? A friend, a colleague or a stranger in an official role? Decide the register at that moment, then check your questions, your negations and your *on* or *nous* against it.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to understand everyday spoken French, with its shortcuts and fillers, and use them with friends.

## What you already know

You've known since <LessonLink id="a1-negation-ne-pas" text="the lesson on negation" /> that people often drop *ne* when they speak. You know *bref* and *figure-toi* from <LessonLink id="b1-telling-an-anecdote" text="the lesson on telling an anecdote" />, and the three registers from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />. This lesson looks closely at the familiar register as people really speak it.

## Spoken grammar

You already know the four big shortcuts from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />: dropped *ne*, *on* for *nous*, *ça* for *cela*, and questions in statement order (*tu fais quoi ?*). Everyday speech adds three more.

- Speakers drop *il* in *il faut*: *faut partir* (we've got to go), *faut pas* (you mustn't).
- They use *c'est* for *ce sont*, even with a plural: *c'est mes amis* (they're my friends).
- They announce the subject first, then repeat it as a pronoun: *mon frère, il travaille à Lyon*; *moi, je préfère le thé*.

<Example fr="Faut pas oublier les billets !" en="We mustn't forget the tickets! (spoken)" />
<Example fr="C'est mes voisins, ils sont très sympas." en="They're my neighbours, they're really nice. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Ma sœur, elle adore ce film." en="My sister loves this film. (spoken)" />

In writing, use the standard forms: *il ne faut pas*, *ce sont mes amis*, *ma sœur adore ce film*.

## Reductions

<VocabTable group="reductions" />

Fast speech also shortens common words. *Tu es* becomes *t'es*, *il y a* becomes *y a*, and *je ne sais pas* becomes *chais pas*. *T'inquiète* is short for *ne t'inquiète pas*. You'll see these forms in text messages, but not in careful writing.

<Example fr="T'es sûr ? Y a plus de pain." en="Are you sure? There's no bread left. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Chais pas où il est, t'inquiète." en="Dunno where he is, don't worry. (spoken)" />

## Yes, no and maybe

<VocabTable group="answers" />

*Ouais* is a relaxed *oui*, and *nan* a relaxed *non*. *Mouais* is a reluctant yes: the speaker agrees, but isn't convinced.

<Example fr="Tu viens ? Ouais, j'arrive !" en="Are you coming? Yeah, I'm on my way! (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Le film était bien ? Mouais, pas génial." en="Was the film good? Meh, not great. (spoken)" />

## Fillers

<VocabTable group="fillers" />

Fillers give the speaker time to think. *Bah* or *ben* (well) start an answer; *euh* fills a pause. *Genre* (like, sort of) softens a word or introduces an example. *Quoi*, at the end of a sentence, sums up and asks the listener to agree, like "you know".

<Example fr="Ben, euh, je crois que je vais rester ici." en="Well, um, I think I'll stay here. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Il est arrivé avec un sac genre énorme." en="He turned up with a sort of huge bag. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="C'était nul, quoi." en="It was rubbish, you know. (spoken)" />

## Linking ideas

<VocabTable group="linking" />

*Du coup* (so, as a result) is everywhere in spoken French, and many people find it overused. In writing, use *donc* or *par conséquent*, which you know from <LessonLink id="b2-logical-connectors" text="the lesson on logical connectors" />. *En gros* and *grosso modo* both mean "roughly, basically", and they give a summary without details.

<Example fr="J'ai raté le bus, du coup je suis venu à pied." en="I missed the bus, so I walked here. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="En gros, il veut tout changer." en="Basically, he wants to change everything. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="Le voyage coûte grosso modo trois cents euros." en="The trip costs roughly three hundred euros. (spoken)" />

## Intensifiers

<VocabTable group="intensifiers" />

Familiar French has its own words for "very". *Trop* means "too" in standard French, but in familiar speech it often means "so, really": *c'est trop bien* is a compliment. *Vachement* means "really" and is common among all ages. *Carrément* means "totally". *Grave*, used as an adverb, is youth speech: *c'est grave cool*.

<Example fr="Ce gâteau est trop bon !" en="This cake is so good! (familiar)" />
<Example fr="Il fait vachement froid aujourd'hui." en="It's really cold today. (familiar)" />
<Example fr="Tu viens à la fête ? Carrément !" en="Are you coming to the party? Totally! (familiar)" />

## Vague words

<VocabTable group="vague" />

When a speaker can't find the right word, they say *un truc* (a thing) or *un machin* (a whatsit). Both are very common and quite informal.

<Example fr="Passe-moi le truc, là, sur la table." en="Pass me the thing, there, on the table. (spoken)" />
<Example fr="C'est quoi, ce machin ?" en="What's this thingy? (spoken)" />

## When to use them

Use these features with friends, family and people of your age in relaxed situations. In a job interview, a formal email or an exam, keep to standard French: *je ne sais pas*, *nous*, *cela*, *donc*. In this course, the exercises use standard French unless the instruction asks for the familiar register.

## En français

Jade and Tom, two teenagers, chat after school. Every line is spoken French.

- *Salut ! T'es libre ce soir ?* (Hi! Are you free tonight?)
- *Bah, chais pas. J'ai un truc à finir pour demain.* (Well, dunno. I've got a thing to finish for tomorrow.)
- *Quel truc ?* (What thing?)
- *Le devoir de maths, grosso modo trois exercices.* (The maths homework, roughly three exercises.)
- *Ah ouais, moi aussi. Il est trop difficile, ce devoir !* (Oh yeah, me too. That homework is so hard!)
- *Carrément. Du coup, on le fait ensemble ?* (Totally. So shall we do it together?)
- *Ouais, bonne idée. Chez toi ou chez moi ?* (Yeah, good idea. Your place or mine?)
- *Chez moi, y a personne avant sept heures.* (My place, there's nobody before seven.)
- *Euh, et ton frère ? Il fait vachement de bruit !* (Um, what about your brother? He makes a lot of noise!)
- *Nan, il est au sport. T'inquiète.* (Nah, he's at sport. Don't worry.)
- *Bon. Et après, on regarde le film dont tout le monde parle ?* (OK. And afterwards, shall we watch the film everyone's talking about?)
- *Mouais. On m'a dit qu'il est genre trop long.* (Meh. Someone told me it's, like, way too long.)
- *En gros, tu veux pas le voir, quoi.* (Basically, you don't want to see it, right.)
- *Si, si ! Mais d'abord, les maths. C'est grave important !* (I do, I do! But maths first. It's seriously important!)

Two of these lines in standard French:

<Example fr="Je ne sais pas. J'ai quelque chose à finir pour demain." en="I don't know. I have something to finish for tomorrow. (standard)" />
<Example fr="Chez moi, il n'y a personne avant sept heures." en="At my place, there's nobody before seven. (standard)" />

<Callout type="warning" title="Du coup, not in an email">
  Learners who hear *du coup* all day start writing it everywhere. In an email or an essay, *du coup, je vous envoie le dossier* sounds careless. Write **je vous envoie donc le dossier**, or use **par conséquent**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Translate one conversation back">
  Listen to a short conversation between young French speakers, in a series or a video. Write down three spoken lines, then rewrite each one in standard French. Going from spoken to standard and back trains your ear and keeps your writing correct.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Le français parlé est différent du français écrit. À l'oral, on oublie souvent le ne de la négation, on dit on à la place de nous, et ça à la place de cela. On pose aussi des questions sans inversion, comme tu fais quoi. Certains mots sont plus courts : t'es pour tu es, y a pour il y a, chais pas pour je ne sais pas. Bah, ben et euh donnent du temps pour réfléchir, et quoi termine une phrase. Du coup, en gros et grosso modo aident à passer d'une idée à l'autre. Trop, vachement, carrément et grave veulent dire très, et un truc ou un machin remplacent un mot qu'on ne trouve pas. On parle ce français avec des amis. Dans un message formel, un entretien ou un examen, on garde le français courant.

By the end of this lesson you'll understand how verlan works, recognize the most common slang words, and know when it's better not to use them.

## What you already know

You know spoken grammar and fillers from <LessonLink id="b2-colloquial-french" text="the lesson on spoken French" />, and familiar words such as *une bagnole*, *un bouquin*, *le boulot* and *bosser* from <LessonLink id="b2-registers" text="the lesson on registers" />. This lesson goes one step further, into slang.

## What slang is

<VocabTable group="terms" />

*L'argot* is slang: words used by a group, often young people, and understood by those who share their world. A slang word is *une expression argotique*. It's more informal than the familiar register, and it changes fast: some words last for decades, others disappear in a few years.

French slang has a famous system of its own, *le verlan*. Its name tells you how it works: *verlan* is *l'envers* (back to front), said back to front. To make a verlan word, you *inversez les syllabes*.

<Example fr="Le mot « verlan » est lui-même un mot de verlan." en="The word verlan is itself a verlan word." />
<Example fr="Pour faire du verlan, on inverse les syllabes d'un mot." en="To make verlan, you swap the syllables of a word." />

## How verlan works

<VocabTable group="verlan" />

The basic rule is simple: cut the word into syllables, then put them in the opposite order. With two syllables, it works directly.

- *Bizarre* (bi-zarre) gives *zarbi* (weird).
- *Louche* (lou-che) gives *chelou* (weird, shady).
- *Lourd* gives *relou* (annoying): the final sound comes first.
- *Fou*, with one syllable, is turned round sound by sound: *ouf* (crazy, amazing).

Often, vowels change or disappear on the way, so you can't always guess the original. *Femme* gives *meuf*, and *énervé* gives *vénère*. Learn the common words as words, and use the rule to understand new ones.

<Example fr="Ce concert était ouf !" en="That concert was amazing! (slang)" />
<Example fr="Il est vénère parce qu'il a raté son train." en="He's angry because he missed his train. (slang)" />
<Example fr="Ton histoire est chelou." en="Your story is weird. (slang)" />
<Example fr="C'est zarbi, il ne répond pas au téléphone." en="It's weird, he isn't answering the phone. (slang)" />
<Example fr="Sa meuf est très sympa." en="His girlfriend is really nice. (slang)" />

## People and money

<VocabTable group="people" />

Some of the most common slang words are not verlan at all. *Un mec* is a guy, and also a boyfriend; *une nana* is a woman or a girl; *un pote* is a mate.

<Example fr="Je suis sorti avec deux potes hier soir." en="I went out with two mates last night. (slang)" />
<Example fr="C'est qui, ce mec ?" en="Who's that guy? (slang)" />
<Example fr="Cette nana est très drôle." en="That girl is really funny. (slang)" />

<VocabTable group="money" />

Money has many slang words. The two you'll hear most are *le fric* and *la thune*.

<Example fr="J'ai plus de fric, je peux pas sortir." en="I've got no money left, I can't go out. (slang)" />
<Example fr="Ce voyage coûte trop de thune." en="This trip costs too much cash. (slang)" />

## Feelings and situations

<VocabTable group="feelings" />

*Kiffer* means to love or to be really into something. *Crevé* is knackered. *Flipper* is to freak out, or to be scared. *Se marrer* is to have a laugh. And when everything goes wrong, *c'est la galère*.

<Example fr="Je kiffe cette chanson." en="I love this song. (slang)" />
<Example fr="Après le match, j'étais crevée." en="After the match, I was knackered. (slang, a woman speaking)" />
<Example fr="Elle flippe avant chaque examen." en="She freaks out before every exam. (slang)" />
<Example fr="On s'est bien marrés au cinéma." en="We had a good laugh at the cinema. (slang)" />
<Example fr="Plus de bus après minuit, c'est la galère !" en="No buses after midnight, it's a nightmare! (slang)" />

## Where it comes from

Verlan is old, but it spread widely from the 1980s and 1990s, in the suburbs of big cities and then in rap music. From there, films, series and social networks brought many words to the whole country. Some, like *relou* or *meuf*, are now understood by most French people, even those who never use them.

Slang ages quickly. A word that sounds cool to teenagers today may sound old-fashioned to their younger brothers and sisters in ten years.

## When not to use it

Slang is for friends of your own age. At work, in a shop, with older people or in any official situation, it sounds rude or ridiculous. As a learner, the most useful skill is to understand slang in films, songs and conversations. Use it yourself only when you're sure of the meaning and the situation.

## En français

Emma, an exchange student, asks her French friend Lucie about messages from Lucie's younger brother.

- *Lucie, ton frère m'a écrit : « Ce film est ouf ». Ça veut dire quoi ?* (Lucie, your brother wrote to me: "This film is ouf". What does that mean?)
- *C'est du verlan : « ouf », c'est « fou ». Il veut dire que le film est génial.* (It's verlan: ouf is fou. He means the film is brilliant.)
- *Et « relou » ? Il dit que son prof est relou.* (And relou? He says his teacher is relou.)
- *« Relou », c'est « lourd » à l'envers. Il n'aime pas son prof, quoi.* (Relou is lourd back to front. He doesn't like his teacher, basically.)
- *D'accord. Et pourquoi il écrit « j'ai plus de thune » ?* (OK. And why does he write "j'ai plus de thune"?)
- *La thune, c'est l'argent. Il n'a plus d'argent, comme toujours !* (La thune is money. He has no money left, as always!)
- *Ha ! Et à la fin : « je suis crevé, je vais me coucher ».* (Ha! And at the end: "je suis crevé, je vais me coucher".)
- *Crevé, c'est très fatigué. Ça, ce n'est pas du verlan, c'est de l'argot.* (Crevé means very tired. That isn't verlan, it's slang.)
- *Je peux dire ces mots, moi aussi ?* (Can I say these words too?)
- *Avec tes amis, oui. Mais pas avec ma mère, ni avec tes professeurs !* (With your friends, yes. But not with my mother, or with your teachers!)

## A magazine piece

This is an original magazine piece about verlan.

*Le verlan, une langue à l'envers*

*Le verlan est une forme d'argot très française. Son principe est simple : on inverse les syllabes d'un mot. Le mot « verlan » lui-même vient de « l'envers », dit à l'envers. Ainsi, « fou » devient « ouf », « louche » devient « chelou » et « bizarre » devient « zarbi ». Parfois, les voyelles changent aussi : « femme » donne « meuf ».*

*Le verlan existe depuis longtemps, mais il devient très populaire dans les années 1980 et 1990, d'abord dans la banlieue des grandes villes, puis dans le rap. Aujourd'hui, beaucoup de jeunes le parlent, et certains mots sont maintenant dans le français de tous les jours : « relou » ou « meuf » sont compris dans toute la France.*

*Mais ces mots vieillissent vite. Un mot que tout le monde dit aujourd'hui peut sembler démodé dans dix ans, et les jeunes inventent toujours de nouveaux mots.*

*Pour un étudiant étranger, le verlan est surtout important pour comprendre les jeunes, les films et les chansons. Pour le parler, il vaut mieux être prudent : un mot mal choisi peut faire rire tout le monde.*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

(Verlan, a back-to-front language. Verlan is a very French form of slang. Its principle is simple: you swap the syllables of a word. The word verlan itself comes from l'envers, said back to front. <Callout type="warning" title="No slang at an interview">
  Learners proud of new slang sometimes use it in the wrong place. *Je kiffe ce boulot*, said in a job interview, sounds disrespectful. Say **j'aime beaucoup ce travail**, and keep *kiffer* for your friends.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep a slang list with a date">
  When you meet a slang word, write it down with three things: its standard French meaning, where you heard it (a film, a song, a friend) and the year. Slang changes fast, so the date tells you later whether the word is still current.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to avoid the false friends that English speakers get wrong most often.

## What you already know

You've already met many false friends, and the table near the end of this lesson brings them together. Each one has a link to the lesson that taught it. This lesson adds the most common new ones, grouped by meaning.

## How false friends work

A false friend, *un faux ami*, looks like an English word but means something else. Some are completely different (*un car* is a coach), others are close but not the same (*excité* is stronger than "excited"). The danger is that you don't check them, because you think you already know them.

## People and feelings

<VocabTable group="people" />

**Sensible** means "sensitive": easily hurt, or reacting quickly. For "sensible", French says *raisonnable*.

<Example fr="Il est très sensible : il ne supporte pas les critiques." en="He's very sensitive: he can't stand criticism." />
<Example fr="Ta décision est raisonnable." en="Your decision is sensible." />

**Excité** means "overexcited" or "worked up", and it can have a sexual sense, so English speakers often say it by mistake. To say you're looking forward to something, use *avoir hâte de*.

<Example fr="Les enfants sont excités avant la fête." en="The children are overexcited before the party." />
<Example fr="J'ai hâte de vous voir samedi." en="I can't wait to see you on Saturday." />

**Rude** means "harsh, tough": *un hiver rude* is a hard winter. For "rude" in the English sense, say *impoli*. **Une déception** is a disappointment; you know *déçu* (disappointed).

<Example fr="Le travail à la ferme est rude." en="Work on the farm is tough." />
<Example fr="Ce film a été une grande déception." en="This film was a big disappointment." />

## Actions

<VocabTable group="actions" />

**Assister à** means "to attend": *assister à une réunion*, *assister à un concert*. Without *à*, *assister quelqu'un* means "to help someone", like the English "assist".

<Example fr="Toute l'équipe a assisté à la réunion." en="The whole team attended the meeting." />

**Supporter** means "to put up with, to bear": *je ne supporte pas le bruit* (I can't stand noise). It's also used for supporting a sports team, but not for supporting an idea or a person in need: for that, say *soutenir* or *aider*.

<Example fr="Je ne supporte pas d'attendre." en="I can't bear waiting." />

**Réaliser** means "to carry out, to make": *réaliser un projet*, *réaliser un film*. You know *réaliser un rêve* from <LessonLink id="b1-dreams-and-ambitions" text="the lesson on dreams and ambitions" />. Many people also use *réaliser* for "to realize", but careful speakers prefer *se rendre compte que*, which you know from <LessonLink id="b1-plus-que-parfait" text="the lesson on the plus-que-parfait" />.

<Example fr="Cette entreprise a réalisé un projet ambitieux." en="This company carried out an ambitious project." />
<Example fr="Je me suis rendu compte de mon erreur." en="I realized my mistake." />

**Blesser** means "to injure" or "to hurt", not "to bless". You know *se blesser* (to hurt yourself).

<Example fr="Ses mots m'ont blessé." en="His words hurt me." />

## Things and places

<VocabTable group="things" />

**Une location** is a rental, and *louer* is to rent: *une voiture de location*. **Un car** is a coach between towns. **Une cave** is a cellar. **Large** means "wide", and "large" is *grand*. **Un avertissement** is a warning, and **un préjudice** is harm or damage, in formal or legal French.

<Example fr="Nous avons pris une voiture de location pour les vacances." en="We took a rental car for the holidays." />
<Example fr="Le car pour Lyon part à huit heures." en="The coach to Lyon leaves at eight." />
<Example fr="Il garde ses bouteilles de vin à la cave." en="He keeps his bottles of wine in the cellar." />
<Example fr="Cette rue est large, mais la maison est petite." en="This street is wide, but the house is small." />
<Example fr="L'entreprise a reçu un dernier avertissement." en="The company received a final warning." />
<Example fr="Ce retard lui a causé un préjudice." en="This delay caused him harm." />

## Possibly, not eventually

<VocabTable group="possibly" />

**Éventuellement** means "possibly, if need be", and **éventuel** "possible". For "eventually", say *finalement* or *à la fin*. This one causes many misunderstandings in emails.

<Example fr="Je peux éventuellement venir lundi." en="I could possibly come on Monday." />
<Example fr="Contactez-nous en cas de problème éventuel." en="Contact us in case of any possible problem." />

## A strategy

When a French word looks exactly like an English one, stop for a second before you use it. Check its meaning in a dictionary, and look at an example sentence. The words that look the most familiar are the ones that cause the most mistakes.

## En français

Tom, an English intern, talks to his colleague, Madame Leroy. She kindly corrects three mistakes.

- *Bonjour, madame. Excusez-moi, je suis en retard : j'ai attendu le car pendant une heure.* (Hello. Sorry, I'm late: I waited for the coach for an hour.)
- *Ce n'est pas grave. Vous avez assisté à la réunion de lundi ?* (It doesn't matter. Did you attend Monday's meeting?)
- *Oui, et je suis très excité par ce projet !* (Yes, and I'm very excited about this project!)
- *Attention, « excité » a parfois un sens gênant. Dites plutôt : « j'ai hâte de commencer ce projet ».* (Careful, excité sometimes has an awkward meaning. Say instead: I can't wait to start this project.)
- *Merci ! Et le directeur est très sensible : il a écouté toutes mes idées.* (Thank you! And the director is very sensible: he listened to all my ideas.)
- *Vous voulez dire « raisonnable », je pense. « Sensible », c'est quelqu'un qui est vite blessé !* (You mean raisonnable, I think. Sensible is someone who gets hurt easily!)
- *Ah oui, raisonnable. Je cherche aussi une location pour la fin de mon stage, éventuellement pour un mois de plus.* (Oh yes, raisonnable. I'm also looking for a rental for the end of my internship, possibly for another month.)
- *Bonne idée. Vous cherchez un grand appartement ?* (Good idea. Are you looking for a big flat?)
- *Non, petit. Mais la dernière fois, le propriétaire était très rude avec moi.* (No, small. But last time, the landlord was very rude to me.)
- *« Rude » veut dire dur. Pour « rude » en anglais, dites « impoli ».* (Rude means harsh. For rude in the English sense, say impoli.)
- *Merci, madame. Trois erreurs en une matinée : quelle déception !* (Thank you. Three mistakes in one morning: what a disappointment!)

<Callout type="warning" title="Éventuellement isn't eventually">
  In emails, learners write *éventuellement, nous avons signé le contrat* to mean "in the end". The French reader understands "possibly", which changes the whole message. Write **finalement, nous avons signé le contrat**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Test the words that look easy">
  Keep a short list of words that look English, and test yourself on them once a week: say the French word, then its real meaning, then the trap. Ten words on a card are enough. The list catches the mistakes you'd never think of checking.
</Callout>

## En résumé

Un faux ami est presque comme un mot anglais, mais il a un autre sens. Sensible veut dire qu'on est vite blessé, et pour l'autre sens, on dit raisonnable. Éventuellement veut dire peut-être, pas finalement. Assister à, c'est être présent à un événement. Une location est quelque chose qu'on loue, un car est un bus entre deux villes, et large ne veut pas dire grand. Rude veut dire dur, une déception, c'est quand on est déçu, et excité peut avoir un sens gênant : on dit plutôt avoir hâte de. Avant de choisir un mot qui est trop comme l'anglais, il faut vérifier son sens. Dans le cours, vous connaissez déjà d'autres faux amis : actuellement, une librairie, rester, prétendre, sans doute et un comédien.

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to take the floor in a debate, interrupt politely, answer an argument and bring the discussion back to its subject.

## What you already know

You know how to agree and disagree (*être d'accord*, *avoir tort*) from <LessonLink id="b1-agreeing-disagreeing" text="the lesson on agreeing and disagreeing" />, and *un débat* and *défendre une idée* from <LessonLink id="b1-presenting-an-argument" text="the lesson on presenting an argument" />. You know *prendre la parole* and *passer la parole* from <LessonLink id="b2-meetings-and-calls" text="the lesson on meetings and calls" />, and *un contre-argument* from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing opinions" />. This lesson is about what happens when two people want to speak at once.

## The roles in a debate

<VocabTable group="roles" />

A debate on the radio or on television has *un modérateur* or *une modératrice*, who gives the floor, keeps time and stops the guests from all speaking together. Each guest has *un temps de parole*. Many guests try to *avoir le dernier mot*, because the last words are the ones listeners remember.

<Example fr="La modératrice donne la parole à chaque invité à son tour." en="The moderator gives the floor to each guest in turn." />
<Example fr="Votre temps de parole est presque fini, madame." en="Your speaking time is almost up, madam." />
<Example fr="Il veut toujours avoir le dernier mot." en="He always wants to have the last word." />

## Taking and keeping the floor

<VocabTable group="floor" />

To get the floor while someone is speaking, ask politely.

- To ask for the floor, use *si je peux me permettre* (if I may) or *permettez-moi de* + infinitive (allow me to).
- To keep the floor, say *laissez-moi finir* (let me finish).
- To protest when someone cuts you off, say *je ne vous ai pas interrompu*, or *vous me coupez la parole* (you're interrupting me).

<Example fr="Si je peux me permettre, j'aimerais rebondir sur ce que vous venez de dire." en="If I may, I'd like to pick up on what you've just said." />
<Example fr="Permettez-moi de finir ma phrase, s'il vous plaît." en="Allow me to finish my sentence, please." />
<Example fr="Je ne vous ai pas interrompu, alors laissez-moi finir." en="I didn't interrupt you, so let me finish." />

*Couper la parole à quelqu'un* is a normal part of a lively debate, but do it with a polite phrase first. In French debates, people interrupt more than in many English-speaking ones, so these phrases are useful both to take the floor and to defend it.

## Answering an argument

<VocabTable group="rebut" />

When you disagree, you can *réfuter* an argument (show that it's wrong), *contredire* someone (say the opposite) or *objecter que* (raise an objection). To keep your position, say *je maintiens que*. To stay polite, use *je ne partage pas votre avis* rather than *vous avez tort*.

<Example fr="Je ne partage pas votre avis sur ce point." en="I don't share your view on this point." />
<Example fr="Permettez-moi de vous contredire : les chiffres disent le contraire." en="Allow me to contradict you: the figures say the opposite." />
<Example fr="On peut objecter que les livraisons deviendront plus difficiles." en="One could object that deliveries will become more difficult." />
<Example fr="Je maintiens que c'est un argument de poids." en="I maintain that it's a strong argument." />

Note that *contredire* is irregular in one form: *vous contredisez*, not *vous contredites*. And before you answer *une objection*, show that you've understood it with *si je comprends bien* (*si je comprends bien, vous dites que…*), which you know from <LessonLink id="b2-negotiating" text="the lesson on negotiating" />.

## Bringing the debate back

<VocabTable group="refocus" />

Debates often drift away from their subject. The moderator, or a guest, can bring them back.

- To refocus, say *revenons-en à* + the subject: *revenons-en à la question des voitures*.
- To dismiss a side issue, say *là n'est pas la question* or *ce n'est pas le sujet*.
- To use someone's point as a starting point, say *je voudrais rebondir sur ce que vous avez dit*.

<Example fr="Là n'est pas la question : nous parlons de la sécurité des piétons." en="That's not the point: we're talking about pedestrian safety." />
<Example fr="Ce n'est pas le sujet de notre débat, revenons-en aux commerçants." en="That's not what we're discussing, let's get back to the shopkeepers." />

## Summarizing the other side fairly

A strong debater sums up the other side's best argument before answering it. It shows that you've listened, and it makes your answer more convincing.

<VocabTable group="topic" />

<Example fr="Si je vous comprends bien, vous pensez que les commerçants vont perdre des clients." en="If I understand you correctly, you think shopkeepers will lose customers." />
<Example fr="C'est justement la sécurité des piétons qui m'inquiète." en="It's precisely the safety of pedestrians that worries me." />

Then use the mise en relief from <LessonLink id="b2-mise-en-relief" text="the lesson on mise en relief" /> to put your own main point in focus, as in the second example.

## En français

A radio debate: should cars be banned from the city centre? Claire Dubois is the moderator. Monsieur Bernard is a shopkeeper; Madame Roux works for a cyclists' association.

- *Monsieur Bernard, vous êtes commerçant. Faut-il interdire les voitures au centre-ville ?* (Mr Bernard, you're a shopkeeper. Should cars be banned from the town centre?)
- *Je ne le pense pas. Beaucoup de mes clients viennent en voiture, surtout les personnes âgées.* (I don't think so. Many of my customers come by car, especially elderly people.)
- *Si je peux me permettre, les piétons dépensent aussi de l'argent !* (If I may, pedestrians spend money too!)
- *Madame, je ne vous ai pas interrompue. Laissez-moi finir, s'il vous plaît.* (Madam, I didn't interrupt you. Let me finish, please.)
- *Madame Roux, vous aurez votre temps de parole. Monsieur Bernard, continuez.* (Ms Roux, you'll have your speaking time. Mr Bernard, go on.)
- *Merci. Sans voitures, il y aura moins de clients, et certains magasins fermeront.* (Thank you. Without cars, there will be fewer customers, and some shops will close.)
- *Madame Roux, que répondez-vous ?* (Ms Roux, what's your answer?)
- *Permettez-moi de le contredire. Dans d'autres villes, les rues sans voitures attirent plus de gens.* (Allow me to contradict him. In other towns, car-free streets attract more people.)
- *Mais là n'est pas la question : mes clients habitent loin !* (But that's not the point: my customers live far away!)
- *Je maintiens que la pollution et le bruit sont un vrai problème pour les habitants.* (I maintain that pollution and noise are a real problem for residents.)
- *Revenons-en aux personnes âgées. Comment viendront-elles en ville ?* (Let's get back to elderly people. How will they come into town?)
- *Avec de meilleurs transports en commun. C'est un argument de poids, je crois.* (With better public transport. That's a strong argument, I think.)
- *Je partage votre avis sur les transports, mais ils doivent exister avant l'interdiction.* (I share your view on transport, but it has to exist before the ban.)
- *Merci à tous les deux. Nous allons maintenant écouter nos auditeurs.* (Thank you both. We'll now hear from our listeners.)

## The moderator's summary

This is an original extract from the end of the same programme: the moderator sums up the debate before the listeners call in.

*« Merci à nos deux invités. Résumons ce débat, qui a été long mais toujours respectueux.*

*Monsieur Bernard, commerçant au centre-ville, est contre une interdiction totale des voitures. Selon lui, beaucoup de clients, surtout les personnes âgées et les familles qui habitent loin, ont besoin de leur voiture. Il objecte aussi que les livraisons deviendront plus difficiles, et que certains magasins vont peut-être fermer. Il propose donc de garder quelques rues ouvertes aux voitures.*

*Madame Roux, au contraire, maintient que la pollution et le bruit sont un vrai problème pour les habitants. Elle réfute l'idée que les rues sans voitures font perdre des clients : dans d'autres villes, il y a plus de piétons, et ils dépensent aussi de l'argent. Elle ajoute que les rues sans voitures sont plus sûres pour les enfants.*

*Sur un point, nos deux invités sont d'accord : une interdiction n'est possible que si les transports en commun sont meilleurs. Désormais, c'est à vous, chers auditeurs, de nous donner votre avis. »*

<VocabTable group="glossary" />

("Thank you to our two guests. Let's sum up this debate, which was long but always respectful. Mr Bernard, a shopkeeper in the town centre, is against a total ban on cars. In his view, many customers, especially elderly people and families who live far away, need their car. He also objects that deliveries will become more difficult, and that some shops may close. So he suggests keeping a few streets open to cars. Ms Roux, on the contrary, maintains that pollution and noise are a real problem for residents. She refutes the idea that car-free streets lose customers: in other towns, there are more pedestrians, and they spend money too. She adds that car-free streets are safer for children. On one point, our two guests agree: a ban is only possible if public transport is better. From now on, it's up to you, dear listeners, to give us your view.")

<Callout type="warning" title="Laissez-moi finir, without de">
  Under pressure, learners often say *laissez-moi de finir*, on the model of *permettez-moi de*. *Laisser* takes the infinitive directly: **laissez-moi finir**. *Permettre* needs *de*: **permettez-moi de finir**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Restate before you rebut">
  Before you answer an argument, restate it in one sentence, starting with *si je vous comprends bien*. It gives you a few seconds to think, it shows respect, and it stops the other person from saying you misunderstood them.
</Callout>

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to persuade an audience, not just answer an opponent: with appeals, rhetorical questions, imagined situations and well-placed concessions.

## What you already know

You can already soften an opinion with *il me semble que* and *dans une certaine mesure*, from <LessonLink id="b2-nuancing-opinions" text="the lesson on nuancing opinions" />, and concede a point with *certes… mais*, from <LessonLink id="b2-concession-opposition" text="the lesson on concession and opposition" />. In <LessonLink id="b2-debating" text="the lesson on debating" />, you learned to take the floor and answer an argument. This lesson is about the other side of a debate: convincing the people who listen.

## Persuading and convincing

<VocabTable group="means" />

French distinguishes two ways of winning people over. You know *convaincre* (to convince), which works through reason and arguments. *Persuader* (to persuade) also works through feelings: it can *faire appel à l'émotion* or *jouer sur* the listener's fears and hopes. A good *stratégie* uses both, but a speaker who only plays on emotion soon loses the audience's trust.

<Example fr="Ce discours fait appel à l'émotion plus qu'à la raison." en="This speech appeals to emotion more than to reason." />
<Example fr="Elle joue sur nos peurs pour nous persuader." en="She plays on our fears to persuade us." />
<Example fr="Sa stratégie est simple : un argument, puis un exemple." en="Her strategy is simple: one argument, then an example." />

## Appealing to the listener

<VocabTable group="appeals" />

An appeal speaks to the listener directly and asks them to agree. *Avouez que* (you must admit that) is the strongest; *convenez que* (you'll agree that) is more formal and gentler. Both take the indicative, because they present a fact. *Croyez-moi* (believe me) puts your own credibility on the table, so use it once, not in every sentence.

<Example fr="Avouez que la situation d'aujourd'hui ne convient à personne." en="You must admit that today's situation suits nobody." />
<Example fr="Convenez que nous avons déjà beaucoup attendu." en="You'll agree that we've already waited a long time." />
<Example fr="Croyez-moi, ce changement aidera tout le monde." en="Believe me, this change will help everyone." />

## Rhetorical questions

<VocabTable group="questions" />

A rhetorical question doesn't wait for an answer: the speaker expects the audience to think "yes". *N'est-il pas vrai que* (isn't it true that) takes the indicative, because the speaker presents the fact as true. After *ne pensez-vous pas que* (don't you think that), both moods are used: the indicative when you present the idea as obvious, the subjunctive in more formal speech.

<Example fr="N'est-il pas vrai que nous manquons tous de temps ?" en="Isn't it true that we're all short of time?" />
<Example fr="Ne pensez-vous pas que nous ayons besoin de changer ?" en="Don't you think that we need to change? (formal)" />
<Example fr="Ne pensez-vous pas que nous avons besoin de changer ?" en="Don't you think that we need to change?" />

## Imagining

<VocabTable group="imagine" />

To make the audience picture a situation, use *imaginons que* (let's imagine that) or *admettons que* (let's suppose that). In careful French, both usually take the subjunctive, because the situation isn't real; you'll also hear the indicative in speech. *Admettons que* is also a concession: you accept the other side's idea for a moment, to show where it leads.

<Example fr="Imaginons que la bibliothèque soit ouverte le soir." en="Let's imagine that the library is open in the evening." />
<Example fr="Admettons que vous ayez raison : que faisons-nous ensuite ?" en="Let's suppose you're right: what do we do next?" />

## Adding weight

<VocabTable group="weight" />

To add a stronger argument after a first one, use *qui plus est* (what's more) or *sans compter que* + indicative (not to mention that). They're more formal than *en plus* or *de plus*. Keep *un argument massue* (a knockout argument) for the end: it's the point the other side can't answer.

<Example fr="Le projet coûte peu ; qui plus est, il est simple à mettre en œuvre." en="The project costs little; what's more, it's easy to carry out." />
<Example fr="Nous devons agir vite, sans compter que les examens arrivent bientôt." en="We need to act quickly, not to mention that the exams are coming soon." />
<Example fr="Son argument massue : tout le monde y gagne." en="His knockout argument: everyone gains from it." />

## Strategic concession

<VocabTable group="concede" />

The most persuasive speakers concede a point before the other side can use it. *J'en conviens* (I grant you that) accepts the objection; then *néanmoins* or *mais* turns it round. *Quoi qu'on en dise* (whatever people say) dismisses criticism in advance; it uses the subjunctive *dise*, like *quoi que* in <LessonLink id="b2-relative-prepositions" text="the lesson on relative pronouns after prepositions" />.

<Example fr="Ce changement a un prix, j'en conviens. Néanmoins, il est nécessaire." en="This change has a cost, I grant you that. Nevertheless, it's necessary." />
<Example fr="Quoi qu'on en dise, cette idée marche déjà dans d'autres villes." en="Whatever people say, this idea already works in other towns." />

## En français

Inès is rehearsing a speech for the student union vote. Her friend Hugo gives her advice.

- *Écoute le début : « Avouez que la bibliothèque ferme trop tôt. »* (Listen to the start: "You must admit that the library closes too early.")
- *C'est fort. Mais ne commence pas trop vite : pose d'abord une question.* (It's strong. But don't start too fast: ask a question first.)
- *D'accord : « N'est-il pas vrai que beaucoup d'étudiants travaillent le soir ? »* (OK: "Isn't it true that many students work in the evening?")
- *Parfait. Tout le monde va penser « oui ». Et ensuite ?* (Perfect. Everyone will think "yes". And then?)
- *« Imaginons que la bibliothèque soit ouverte jusqu'à minuit. »* ("Let's imagine that the library is open until midnight.")
- *Bien. Mais les autres vont dire que ça coûte cher.* (Good. But the others will say it's expensive.)
- *Je l'ai prévu : « Ce projet a un prix, j'en conviens. Néanmoins, il aidera tout le monde. »* (I've planned for that: "This project has a cost, I grant you that. Nevertheless, it will help everyone.")
- *Excellent, tu leur réponds avant eux. Tu as un argument massue ?* (Excellent, you answer them before they do. Do you have a knockout argument?)
- *Oui : d'autres universités l'ont déjà fait, sans aucun problème.* (Yes: other universities have already done it, without any problem.)
- *Garde-le pour la fin. Et joue un peu sur l'émotion, mais pas trop.* (Keep it for the end. And play a bit on emotion, but not too much.)
- *Comme ça : « Croyez-moi, vos soirées d'examen ne seront plus les mêmes » ?* (Like this: "Believe me, your exam evenings will never be the same"?)
- *Oui ! Avec ça, tu vas les persuader.* (Yes! With that, you'll persuade them.)

## The speech

This is Inès's speech, an original text.

*Chers étudiants,*

*Avouez que notre bibliothèque ferme trop tôt. N'est-il pas vrai que beaucoup d'entre nous travaillent le soir, pour payer leurs études ? Le matin, ils ont cours ; le soir, ils travaillent. Pour eux, réviser à la bibliothèque est presque impossible.*

*Imaginons que la bibliothèque soit ouverte jusqu'à minuit, et le dimanche. Chacun pourrait choisir son heure, et personne ne serait obligé de venir tard. Qui plus est, nous aurions enfin un lieu calme pour préparer nos examens, sans compter que beaucoup d'entre nous n'ont pas de bureau chez eux.*

*Ce projet a un prix, j'en conviens. Néanmoins, d'autres universités l'ont déjà fait, sans aucun problème, et le coût peut être partagé avec la ville. Quoi qu'on en dise, ce n'est donc pas un rêve : c'est une question de choix.*

*Ne pensez-vous pas que nos études méritent cet effort ? Croyez-moi, vos soirées d'examen ne seront plus les mêmes. Ce vote est le vôtre : votez pour ce projet !*

(Dear students, You must admit that our library closes too early. Isn't it true that many of us work in the evening, to pay for our studies? In the morning they have classes; in the evening they work. For them, revising at the library is almost impossible. Let's imagine that the library is open until midnight, and on Sundays. Everyone could choose their time, and nobody would have to come late. What's more, we would finally have a quiet place to prepare for our exams, not to mention that many of us don't have a desk at home. This project has a cost, I grant you that. Nevertheless, other universities have already done it, without any problem, and the cost can be shared with the town. Whatever people say, it isn't a dream: it's a question of choice. Don't you think our studies deserve this effort? Believe me, your exam evenings will never be the same. This vote is yours: vote for this project!)

## How the speech persuades

- It opens with an appeal (*avouez que*) and a rhetorical question, so the audience agrees from the first lines.
- It makes the audience picture the change with *imaginons que* + subjunctive.
- It adds weight with *qui plus est* and *sans compter que*.
- It concedes the cost (*j'en conviens*) before the other side can raise it, then answers with an example.
- It ends with emotion and a direct call to vote.


<Callout type="warning" title="Imaginons que vous ayez raison">
  After *imaginons que*, learners often keep the indicative from English: *imaginons que vous avez raison*. In careful French, the imagined situation takes the subjunctive: **imaginons que vous ayez raison**, **admettons qu'il soit trop tard**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Answer the objection first">
  Before you give a speech, write down the strongest objection someone could make to it. Then answer it yourself, in the middle of your speech, with *j'en conviens* and *néanmoins*. An audience trusts a speaker who has thought about the other side.
</Callout>

## A model stock-take

This is an original model text, *Mon bilan de B2*: a learner looks back on the level. Each paragraph keeps one time frame, and together they use a structure from every unit.

*Mon bilan de B2*

*Il y a un an, je parlais déjà assez bien, mais j'avais beaucoup de lacunes. Je ne savais pas nuancer mes opinions, et je comprenais mal les journaux.*

*Aujourd'hui, je lis la presse sans difficulté : je reconnais le passif et le discours indirect. Je cherche des livres qui me fassent réfléchir, et je reconnais même le passé simple dans les romans. Bien que mon accent ne soit pas parfait, je parle avec plus d'aisance, et je sais choisir mon registre.*

*Si j'avais commencé plus tôt, j'aurais sans doute progressé plus vite.*

*L'année prochaine, je ferai lire mes textes à une amie, et j'approfondirai la littérature. Il est probable que je passerai l'examen de C1 dans deux ans.*

*Tout compte fait, mon parcours d'apprentissage est long, mais c'est un vrai plaisir. Je me suis fixé un nouvel objectif : perfectionner mon français, quoi qu'on en dise !*

(My B2 stock-take. A year ago, I already spoke quite well, but I had a lot of gaps. I couldn't nuance my opinions, and I understood the newspapers badly. Today, I read the press without difficulty: I recognize the passive and reported speech. I look for books that make me think, and I even recognize the passé simple in novels. Although my accent isn't perfect, I speak more fluently, and I know how to choose my register. If I had started earlier, I would probably have made faster progress. Next year, I'll have a friend read my texts, and I'll study literature in more depth. I'll probably take the C1 exam in two years. All things considered, my learning path is long, but it's a real pleasure. I've set myself a new goal: to perfect my French, whatever people say!)

## How the text uses the grammar

- The first paragraph uses the imparfait for a past situation, and the second uses the present.
- *Bien que mon accent ne soit pas parfait* is a subjunctive conjunction (Unit 1).
- *Si j'avais commencé…, j'aurais progressé* is the unreal past (Unit 2).
- *Le passif* and *le discours indirect* are the news tools of Unit 3.
- *Des livres qui me fassent réfléchir* is a subjunctive in a relative clause, for something you're looking for (Unit 4).
- *Je ferai lire mes textes à une amie* is *faire* + infinitive (Unit 5).
- *Il est probable que je passerai*, with the indicative, comes from Unit 6.
- *Tout compte fait* and *un vrai plaisir* (adjective before the noun) come from Unit 7.
- *Quoi qu'on en dise* is a persuasion phrase from Unit 8.

## Check your work

- Does each paragraph keep one time frame?
- After *bien que*, *avant que* and *il se peut que*, is the verb in the subjunctive?
- After *il est probable que*, is it in the indicative?
- In your regret, is the *si* clause in the plus-que-parfait and the main clause in the conditionnel passé?
- Did you keep the standard register throughout, with *ne* in every negation?

<Callout type="warning" title="Je me suis fixé un objectif">
  With *se fixer un objectif*, learners often make the participle agree with the speaker: *je me suis fixée un objectif*, said by a woman. Here *se* isn't the direct object; *un objectif* is, and it comes after the verb. So the participle doesn't change: **je me suis fixé un objectif**, for a man or a woman.
</Callout>
