# Review needed

Claims in written lessons that a fluent speaker or teacher should confirm. Each entry gives the lesson id and the exact claim as it appears (or is implied) in the lesson. Remove an entry once it's checked.

## a1-alphabet-and-accents

- **"When a line is bad, French speakers often spell with names: *B comme Bernard, D comme Daniel*."** Confirm this is still common practice and that these example names are typical.
- **"Many circumflexes stand where an s was written in older French … forêt (forest), hôpital (hospital), fête (feast)."** Confident about the examples. Confirm the wording "very often" isn't overstated.
- **"In French that's normally the last syllable of a word or short phrase"** (pronunciation hints: where the stress falls). Simplified for beginners; confirm it's acceptable as stated.

## a1-introducing-yourself

- **"*Tu t'appelles comment ?* is the most common way in everyday speech."** Ranking of the informal variants.
- **"*Vous vous appelez comment ?* is also polite, and a little more relaxed."** *Comment vous appelez-vous ?* is the more formal form. Confirm it's polite enough to use with strangers.
- **"*Mon nom est…* isn't wrong, but it sounds stiff, like reading from a form."** Confirm the register judgement.
- **"In formal French paperwork, people often give the surname first and sometimes write it in capitals, for example *MARTIN Sophie*."**

## a1-subject-pronouns

- **"*Tu* is used more freely in Québec."**
- **"Moving from *vous* to *tu* … is usually offered by the older or more senior person."**

## a1-etre-present

- **"*Êtes* is an example [of a circumflex marking a lost s]: older French wrote it *estes*."**
- **"In these quick comments [*C'est bon*, *C'est super*] the describing word stays in its basic form."** True for the examples given. Confirm the generalisation is safe at A1.

## a1-numbers-0-20

- **"Before a consonant, *six*, *huit* and *dix* usually drop their final sound."** Confirm "usually" is the right hedge (e.g. *dix* before a month in dates).
- **"*Neuf* links with a v sound in *neuf ans* and *neuf heures*."** The lesson implies these are the main cases; confirm no other common case is worth adding.
- **"Each one [11–16] is related to its small number: *douze* and *deux*, *treize* and *trois*, … *seize* and *six*."** Presented as a memory aid; confirm it's fair to call them "related".

## a1-countries-nationalities

- **"In French, a nationality that describes someone normally takes a small letter: *Je suis français*."** Exercise ex007 marks *Je suis Français* as non-standard. Confirm this is safe, since a capital after *être* is sometimes seen.
- **"If you're Scottish, Welsh or from Northern Ireland, *britannique* is a safe choice."** Confirm the advice (*écossais* and *gallois* exist but aren't taught here).

## a1-culture-politeness

- **"*Mademoiselle* … is now old-fashioned for adults, and French government forms dropped it in 2012."**
- **"The same routine applies at the pharmacy, at the doctor's, on a small bus, and when you enter a waiting room or a lift with a few other people."** Confirm the examples, especially the lift.
- **"*Excusez-moi, …* is polite for stopping someone, and adding *bonjour* first makes you sound warmer."** Confirm this is right when stopping someone in the street.
- **"Women often greet each other and men this way, while men more often shake hands unless they're close friends or relatives."**
- **"How many kisses depends on where you are: two is the most common, but some regions do three or even four."**
- **"Once you've moved to *tu* with someone, you normally stay there."**
- **"French people generally say *bonjour* to a given person once a day … People say *rebonjour* instead."**

## a1-indefinite-articles

- **"In relaxed conversation you'll also hear *c'est des livres*."** Exercise ex010 accepts it as an answer. Confirm it should be accepted at A1 rather than only mentioned.
- **"a French noun nearly always needs something in front of it."** Simplified for beginners; confirm the hedge is enough.

## a1-plural-nouns

- **"This [-al → -aux] is one of the rare plurals you can actually hear."** Confirm "rare" is fair for an A1 summary.

## a1-family

- **"*Le copain* and *la copine* are more casual and common among younger people. They can also just mean a friend."** Confirm the register note.
- **"*le compagnon* or *la compagne* for a long-term partner."** Confirm this is the best general word to teach at A1.

## a1-avoir-present

## a1-describing-people

- **"You can use two at once by joining them with *et*: *les cheveux longs et noirs*, or simply *les cheveux noirs et longs*."** Confirm both orders read naturally, and whether colour-then-length is preferred.
- **"*Il est brun*, *Elle est blonde*": "the same idea as 'she's a blonde' in English."** Confirm the comparison is fair.
- Translation of *sérieux / sérieuse* as "serious, reliable". Confirm the "reliable" sense is safe to give at A1.

## a1-adjective-agreement

- **"A group that includes even one masculine noun takes the masculine plural."** Standard rule; note that some writers now use other agreement practices. Confirm the simple statement is what you want at A1.
- **"Some adjectives … *gros*" listed under BAGS size words, translated "big, fat".** Confirm this gloss is acceptable.
- *Des* + a BAGS adjective (formal *de petites maisons*) is deliberately not mentioned and not used in exercises. Decide if it should appear here or later.

## a1-er-verbs-present

- **"The great majority of French verbs end in -er, and nearly all of them follow this pattern."** Confirm the wording.

## a1-likes-and-dislikes

- **"*J'aime Paul* can mean you're in love with Paul."** Confirm the phrasing.

## a1-negation-ne-pas

- **"In everyday speech, people very often drop the *ne*."** Confirm the frequency claim. Exercise ex009 accepts only the written form with *ne*.
- **"*Je n'aime pas de thé*" given as wrong "for tea in general".** Confirm the qualifier.

## a1-ir-re-verbs-present

- **"Remembering the English meaning as 'await' helps."** Confirm this memory aid suits A1 learners.
- *réussir* glossed "to succeed, to pass (a test)". Confirm.

## a1-numbers-21-100

- **"In some parts of Switzerland you may also come across *huitante*."** Confirm the wording about where it's used.
- **"French speakers in Belgium and Switzerland mostly use simpler words."** Confirm "mostly".
- **"Numbers ending in 1 use *et* (and) with no hyphens."** Traditional spelling. The 1990 spelling reform allows hyphens throughout (*vingt-et-un*); the answer checker treats both the same. Confirm the traditional form is what you want to teach.
- **"The t of *vingt* is silent throughout the 80s and 90s."** Confirm for all of them, including *quatre-vingt-un* and *quatre-vingt-onze*.
