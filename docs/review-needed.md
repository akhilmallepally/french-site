- **"When a line is bad, French speakers often spell with names: *B comme Bernard, D comme Daniel*."** Confirm this is still common practice and that these example names are typical.
- **"Many circumflexes stand where an s was written in older French … forêt (forest), hôpital (hospital), fête (feast)."** Confident about the examples. Confirm the wording "very often" isn't overstated.
- **"In French that's normally the last syllable of a word or short phrase"** (pronunciation hints: where the stress falls). Simplified for beginners; confirm it's acceptable as stated.

- **"*Tu t'appelles comment ?* is the most common way in everyday speech."** Ranking of the informal variants.
- **"*Vous vous appelez comment ?* is also polite, and a little more relaxed."** *Comment vous appelez-vous ?* is the more formal form. Confirm it's polite enough to use with strangers.
- **"*Mon nom est…* isn't wrong, but it sounds stiff, like reading from a form."** Confirm the register judgement.
- **"In formal French paperwork, people often give the surname first and sometimes write it in capitals, for example *MARTIN Sophie*."**

## a1-numbers-0-20

- **"Before a consonant, *six*, *huit* and *dix* usually drop their final sound."** Confirm "usually" is the right hedge (e.g. *dix* before a month in dates).
- **"*Neuf* links with a v sound in *neuf ans* and *neuf heures*."** The lesson implies these are the main cases; confirm no other common case is worth adding.
- **"Each one [11–16] is related to its small number: *douze* and *deux*, *treize* and *trois*, … *seize* and *six*."** Presented as a memory aid; confirm it's fair to call them "related".
